The Gamma Distribution

Calls reach a help desk at random, about one a minute. How long until the third one? The exponential answers for the first call. Add up several exponential waits and you get the gamma distribution, the standard model for waiting times, rainfall, claim sizes and any uncertain rate.

01

Waiting for the k-th event

In a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\), the gap before each event is an independent exponential wait with mean \(1/\lambda\). The time of the \(k\)-th event is the sum of \(k\) such gaps:

$$\begin{gathered} T_k = E_1 + E_2 + \dots + E_k \\ E_i \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda) \end{gathered}$$

Means and variances of independent waits add, so \(T_k\) has mean \(k/\lambda\) and variance \(k/\lambda^2\). Its distribution is the gamma distribution with shape \(k\) and rate \(\lambda\), written \(\text{Gamma}(k, \lambda)\). For whole-number \(k\) it is also called the Erlang distribution, after the Danish engineer who used it for telephone traffic around 1909.

With \(k = 1\) it is the exponential: most waits are short and the density is highest at 0. With \(k = 2\) or more the density starts at 0, because \(k\) events can’t all happen in an instant, rises to a peak and falls away with a long right tail.

This is the continuous twin of the negative binomial. That distribution stacks geometric waits for successes in separate trials; this one stacks exponential waits in continuous time, just as the exponential is the continuous twin of the geometric.

02

Shape and rate

The density of \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\), with shape \(\alpha\) and rate \(\lambda\), is

$$f(t) = \frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\, t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad t > 0$$

For whole \(\alpha = k\), \(\Gamma(k) = (k - 1)!\), and section 4 explains \(\Gamma\) for other values. The two parameters do different jobs. The rate only stretches the time axis: double \(\lambda\) and every wait halves. The shape decides what the curve looks like. The factor \(t^{\alpha - 1}\) holds it down near 0 and \(e^{-\lambda t}\) pulls it down far out, and the peak sits where they balance, at \((\alpha - 1)/\lambda\).

Shape a gamma

\(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\) densitynormal, same mean and variancemean

Slide the shape up. Below 1 the density shoots up at 0; at 1 it is the exponential; above 1 it is a skewed hump. The skewness is \(2/\sqrt\alpha\), so the hump grows steadily more symmetric, and with Standardize on you can watch it close in on the bell. That is the central limit theorem again: a sum of many independent waits is approximately normal.

Two conventions. This page uses the rate \(\lambda\). Many books and programs use the scale \(\theta = 1/\lambda\) instead, the mean of one exponential wait, so the density reads \(t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t/\theta}/(\Gamma(\alpha)\theta^\alpha)\) and the mean is \(\alpha\theta\). R’s dgamma accepts either a rate or a scale argument; scipy.stats.gamma takes a scale only. Always check which one you are passing.

03

Counting and waiting

A Poisson process can be described by its counts or by its waiting times, and one sentence links the two: the \(k\)-th event has happened by time \(t\) exactly when at least \(k\) events happened by time \(t\). With \(N(t) \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda t)\) the count by time \(t\),

$$\begin{aligned} P(T_k \le t) &= P\big(N(t) \ge k\big) \\ &= 1 - \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^j}{j!} \end{aligned}$$

Sequence by sequence the two questions always give the same answer, so the probabilities are equal. The identity is handy in both directions. Software computes Poisson tail probabilities through the gamma’s cumulative distribution function, and the gamma’s CDF for whole \(k\) is a finite Poisson sum. Differentiating that sum in \(t\) gives the density of section 2.

04

Shapes between the whole numbers

Waiting for 2.5 events makes no sense, but the formula works for any shape \(\alpha > 0\), and real data often wants a shape that is not a whole number. All it needs is a value for \((\alpha - 1)!\) between the whole numbers. That is the gamma function:

$$\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x}\,dx$$

It is exactly what makes the density integrate to 1. Integration by parts gives \(\Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha\,\Gamma(\alpha)\), and \(\Gamma(1) = 1\), so \(\Gamma(k) = (k - 1)!\) for whole \(k\). In between it is a smooth curve through the factorials, with surprises such as \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\). Ratios of gamma functions also give the beta function \(B(\alpha, \beta) = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)\), the constant of the beta distribution.

Fractional shapes are where the gamma earns its keep as a general model for positive, right-skewed quantities. Two classic uses:

  • Rainfall. The amount of rain on a wet day is usually small and occasionally huge. Climatologists fit gamma distributions to it, and the fitted shape is often below 1: a density that is highest near 0 with a long tail of storms.
  • Insurance claims. Claim sizes are positive and skewed. A gamma with a fitted shape is a standard first model, and gamma regression models how the mean claim depends on the policy.

Fitting is easy because the mean is \(\alpha/\lambda\) and the variance \(\alpha/\lambda^2\). Matching a sample mean \(\bar x\) and variance \(s^2\) gives \(\hat\alpha = \bar x^2/s^2\) and \(\hat\lambda = \bar x/s^2\). The shape is the squared ratio of mean to standard deviation, so data whose standard deviation exceeds its mean calls for a shape below 1.

05

An uncertain rate

A rate is a positive number, and when it is unknown or varies between units, the gamma is the natural way to describe it. Two results make it the default.

Mixing: the negative binomial

If every driver, leaf or gene has its own rate \(\Lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(r, \text{mean } \mu)\), and its count is Poisson at that rate, then the counts are negative binomial, with variance \(\mu + \mu^2/r\). This is how overdispersed counts are usually modeled, and the shape \(r\) measures how unequal the units are.

Learning: the conjugate prior

Now there is one rate, and you want to learn it from data. Describe what you believe about it as \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\), shape \(\alpha\) and rate \(\beta\). Watch for a time \(T\) and see \(n\) events. Bayes’ rule multiplies the prior by the Poisson likelihood \(\lambda^n e^{-\lambda T}\), and the result is again a gamma:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{prior: } &\text{Gamma}(\alpha,\ \beta) \\ \text{posterior: } &\text{Gamma}(\alpha + n,\ \beta + T) \end{aligned}$$

The shape counts events and the rate counts time. The prior acts like \(\alpha\) events seen in \(\beta\) hours of earlier watching.

prior \(\text{Gamma}(2, 1)\)posterior95% credible intervaltrue rate

The posterior mean \((\alpha + n)/(\beta + T)\) starts at the prior guess and moves toward the observed rate \(n/T\). Its standard deviation \(\sqrt{\alpha + n}/(\beta + T)\) shrinks like \(1/\sqrt T\), the square-root law once more.

06

Adding and scaling

Waiting for \(a\) events and then \(b\) more is waiting for \(a + b\) events. So independent gammas with the same rate add their shapes, for any positive shapes, not only whole ones:

$$\begin{aligned} &\text{Gamma}(\alpha_1, \lambda) + \text{Gamma}(\alpha_2, \lambda) \\ &\quad = \text{Gamma}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2,\ \lambda) \end{aligned}$$

Scaling changes only the rate: if \(X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\) then \(cX \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda/c)\). Measuring a wait in seconds rather than minutes gives the same shape.

Add two gammas

\(X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_1, \lambda)\)\(Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_2, \lambda)\)\(\text{Gamma}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \lambda)\)20,000 simulated \(X + Y\)

With different rates the sum is no longer a gamma: the waits are measured on different clocks, and there is no single rate to give the total.

A famous special case: chi-square

Square a standard normal and you get \(\text{Gamma}(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). Adding \(\nu\) independent squares adds the shapes, so

$$Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_\nu^2 \sim \chi^2_\nu = \text{Gamma}\big(\tfrac{\nu}{2},\ \tfrac12\big)$$

This is the chi-square distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, behind sample variances and the goodness-of-fit tests used in the Poisson and negative binomial tutorials. With \(\nu = 2\) it is an exponential with mean 2.

Where the formulas come from

The density from the Poisson link

For whole \(k\), section 3 gives \(P(T_k \le t) = 1 - \sum_{j $$\begin{aligned} f(t) &= \lambda e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \\ &= \frac{\lambda^k}{(k-1)!}\,t^{k-1}e^{-\lambda t} \end{aligned}$$

which is the gamma density with \(\Gamma(k) = (k-1)!\).

Mean, variance and the MGF

One exponential wait has MGF \(E[e^{sE}] = \lambda/(\lambda - s)\) for \(s < \lambda\). Independent waits multiply their MGFs, so

$$\begin{aligned} M(s) &= \Big(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda - s}\Big)^{\alpha} = \Big(1 - \frac{s}{\lambda}\Big)^{-\alpha} \\ \ln M(s) &= \frac{\alpha s}{\lambda} + \frac{\alpha s^2}{2\lambda^2} \\ &\quad + \frac{\alpha s^3}{3\lambda^3} + \dots \end{aligned}$$

The cumulants are \(\kappa_j = \alpha (j-1)!/\lambda^j\): mean \(\alpha/\lambda\), variance \(\alpha/\lambda^2\), and \(\kappa_3/\kappa_2^{3/2} = 2/\sqrt\alpha\) for the skewness. The same MGF works directly from the density for any \(\alpha > 0\).

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Shape 1: the exponential. \(\text{Gamma}(1, \lambda) = \text{Exp}(\lambda)\) (exponential tutorial).
  2. Same rate adds. \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha_1, \lambda) + \text{Gamma}(\alpha_2, \lambda) = \text{Gamma}(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \lambda)\) for independent variables (section 6).
  3. The Poisson link. \(P(T_k \le t) = P(\text{Poisson}(\lambda t) \ge k)\) (section 3).
  4. Large shape: normal. \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda) \approx \mathcal N(\alpha/\lambda, \alpha/\lambda^2)\) as \(\alpha\) grows (normal tutorial).
  5. Gamma mixture of Poissons. A Poisson count with a gamma rate is negative binomial (section 5).
  6. Conjugate prior. A \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\) belief about a Poisson rate becomes \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha + n, \beta + T)\) after \(n\) events in time \(T\).
  7. Shares: the beta. For independent \(X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\) and \(Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\beta, \lambda)\), the share \(X/(X+Y)\) follows the beta distribution, independent of \(X + Y\).
  8. Chi-square. \(\chi^2_\nu = \text{Gamma}(\nu/2, 1/2)\), the sum of \(\nu\) squared standard normals (chi-square tutorial).
08

Where you will meet it

Queues and call centers

Erlang built the theory of telephone exchanges on these waiting times. The time to serve several customers, or until the \(k\)-th arrival, is still modeled this way in call centers and networks.

Reliability

A system with \(k - 1\) spare parts, each swapped in when the last one fails, survives until the \(k\)-th failure. With exponential lifetimes that total is gamma.

Rainfall and hydrology

Daily rain amounts and monthly totals are routinely fitted with gamma distributions, and drought indices such as the Standardized Precipitation Index are built on that fit.

Insurance

Individual claim sizes are often modeled as gamma, and gamma-distributed risk levels between policyholders lead to negative binomial claim counts.

Bayesian statistics

The gamma is the conjugate prior for Poisson and exponential rates, and for the precision (one over the variance) of a normal distribution.

Statistical tests

Every chi-square test, from goodness of fit to contingency tables, refers its statistic to a gamma distribution in disguise.

When it fails

The gamma’s right tail falls off exponentially. Losses, incomes and file sizes often have heavier, power-law tails, where lognormal or Pareto models fit better.

Gamma or not?

Which of these follow a gamma distribution, at least as a standard model?

The gamma closes a loop in the series: it is the waiting time behind the Poisson count, the uncertain rate behind the negative binomial, and a sum that turns into a bell.
