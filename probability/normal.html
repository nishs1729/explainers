A visual introduction

The Normal Distribution

Add up enough random things, almost any random things, and the total settles into the same shape: the bell curve. That is the central limit theorem, and it is why one distribution turns up in coin tosses, measurement errors, heights and averages of every kind.

01

Add anything up

Every distribution so far in this series has had its own shape. The Bernoulli is two bars, the geometric and the exponential fall away from zero, the Poisson leans to the right. Yet the Galton board, a Poisson with a large mean and a stack of geometric waits all ended in the same bell. Each of them is a sum of many independent pieces, and that is all it takes.

Take independent draws \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) from any distribution with mean \(\mu\) and a finite standard deviation \(\sigma\). Their sum \(S_n\) has mean \(n\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\sqrt n\). Shift and rescale it so that it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1:

$$Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}$$

The central limit theorem says that as \(n\) grows, the distribution of \(Z_n\) gets closer and closer to one fixed curve, the standard normal distribution, whatever distribution the draws came from.

Try the exponential first. With \(n = 1\) it is the lopsided exponential itself; by \(n = 30\) the histogram hugs the curve, though its right tail is still a little heavy. The uniform gets there almost at once, because it is symmetric to begin with. The rare win is the slowest: with \(n = 20\) most sums are 0 or 1, and you need hundreds of draws before the bell is smooth. Draw your own shape too, even one with two humps: as long as it has some spread, adding it up leads to the same curve.

How fast the bell appears depends mostly on how skewed one draw is. The skewness of a sum of \(n\) draws is the skewness of one draw divided by \(\sqrt n\), so a strongly skewed draw needs many terms before the asymmetry fades.

What the theorem needs. The draws must be independent (or nearly so), and none of them may dominate the total. Above all, the standard deviation \(\sigma\) must be finite. Section 6 shows what happens without it.

Who found it

Abraham de Moivre found the bell curve as an approximation to coin-tossing counts in 1733. Pierre-Simon Laplace extended it to sums of other variables around 1810, and Carl Friedrich Gauss had just used the same curve, in 1809, as the law of errors in astronomical measurements, which is why it is also called the Gaussian distribution. The theorem in its modern generality was proved in the early twentieth century by Lyapunov, Lindeberg and others; the name “central limit theorem” is George Pólya’s, from 1920.

02

The bell curve

The standard normal curve has a simple formula: \(e^{-z^2/2}\), scaled so that the total area under it is 1. Its height at \(z\) is

$$\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^{2}/2}$$

Moving it to center \(\mu\) and stretching it by \(\sigma\) gives the general normal distribution \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\):

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x - \mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)$$

Every normal curve is the same shape. \(\mu\) only slides it along, and \(\sigma\) only stretches it sideways (and squashes it down, so the area stays 1). That is why one table, or one function, is enough for all of them.

Shape a normal curve

\(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\)standard normal, for referencewithin 1, 2, 3 sd of the mean

The shaded bands give the 68–95–99.7 rule: about 68% of the area is within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two and 99.7% within three. So a value more than three standard deviations from the mean happens about 3 times in 1,000. The curve also bends at exactly \(\mu \pm \sigma\): those are its inflection points, where it changes from curving down to curving up.

Why the area is 1: the \(\sqrt{2\pi}\)

Call the area under \(e^{-x^2/2}\) \(I\). Multiply two copies, one in \(x\) and one in \(y\), and switch to polar coordinates, where \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) and the area element is \(r\,dr\,d\theta\):

$$\begin{aligned} I^2 &= \int\!\!\int e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy \\ &= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta \\ &= 2\pi \cdot 1 = 2\pi \end{aligned}$$

So \(I = \sqrt{2\pi}\), and dividing by it makes the area exactly 1. The inner integral works because \(r\,e^{-r^2/2}\) is the derivative of \(-e^{-r^2/2}\).

03

Z-scores and areas

Because every normal curve is the standard one, shifted and stretched, any question about \(X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) can be moved to the standard scale. The z-score of a value \(x\) counts how many standard deviations it is from the mean:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}, \qquad P(X \le x) = \Phi(z)$$

Here \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function: \(\Phi(z)\) is the area under the curve to the left of \(z\). It has no formula in elementary functions, so it is computed numerically; every statistics package and spreadsheet has it (pnorm in R, NORM.S.DIST in spreadsheets, scipy.stats.norm.cdf). Probabilities of ranges are differences of \(\Phi\), and the curve’s symmetry gives \(\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z)\).

Shade an area

Three cut-offs come up again and again. 1.645 leaves 5% in one tail, 1.96 leaves 2.5% in each tail (95% in the middle), and 2.576 leaves 0.5% in each tail (99% in the middle). The 1.96 is the one behind the familiar “± 2 standard errors” of confidence intervals.

The tails thin out very fast. Beyond 3 standard deviations lies 0.13% of the area on each side, beyond 4 about 3 in 100,000, beyond 5 about 3 in 10 million, beyond 6 about 1 in a billion. A normal model says big outliers essentially never happen, which is exactly where real data often disagrees with it. Even the largest of a million normal values is typically under 5 standard deviations from the mean; the laws of such maxima are the subject of extreme value distributions.

04

The square-root law

The central limit theorem is about sums, but it is used mostly for averages. The average \(\bar X = S_n/n\) of \(n\) independent draws has the same shape as the sum, scaled down by \(n\), so it is approximately

$$\bar X \approx \mathcal N\Big(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\Big), \qquad \operatorname{sd}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$$

The spread of an average shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\), not like \(1/n\). The variances of independent draws add, so the sum’s variance is \(n\sigma^2\) and its standard deviation only \(\sigma\sqrt n\); dividing by \(n\) leaves \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). This standard deviation of an average is called its standard error.

Averages of die rolls

one average of \(n\) rolls (300 per row)\(3.5 \pm \sigma/\sqrt n\)

Each row uses four times as many rolls as the one above, and each is half as wide. To halve the error you need four times the data, and to get one more decimal place, a hundred times. This is why polls of 1,000 people are common and polls of 100,000 are not: the second costs a hundred times as much and is only ten times as precise.

The square-root law also explains the 1.96 in a confidence interval. An average lies within \(1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n\) of the true mean 95% of the time, so the interval \(\bar x \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n\) catches the true mean in 95% of samples. That is the Wald interval of the binomial tutorial, with \(\sigma = \sqrt{pq}\).

05

Approximating counts

A binomial count is a sum of \(n\) Bernoulli trials, and a Poisson count with mean \(\lambda\) is a sum of \(\lambda\) independent Poisson(1) counts (for whole \(\lambda\)). So for large \(n\) or \(\lambda\) both are close to a normal with the same mean and variance:

$$\begin{gathered} \text{Bin}(n, p) \approx \mathcal N(np,\ npq) \\ \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \approx \mathcal N(\lambda,\ \lambda) \end{gathered}$$

Counts are whole numbers and the normal is continuous, so give each count \(k\) the stretch from \(k - \tfrac12\) to \(k + \tfrac12\). To approximate \(P(X \le k)\), take the area up to \(k + \tfrac12\). This continuity correction was introduced in the binomial and Poisson tutorials:

$$P(X \le k) \approx \Phi\Big(\frac{k + \tfrac12 - \mu}{\sigma}\Big)$$

Counts versus the bell

bars counted in \(P(X \le k)\)other countsnormal area used as the approximation

The approximation is worst where the count is lopsided: a binomial with \(p\) near 0 or 1, or a Poisson with a small mean. The usual rule of thumb asks for \(np \ge 10\) and \(nq \ge 10\), or \(\lambda \ge 10\). The error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\), and the Berry–Esseen theorem puts a bound on it that grows with \(E|X - \mu|^3/\sigma^3\), a measure of how lopsided and heavy-tailed one draw is. Today software computes exact binomial and Poisson probabilities directly, but the approximation is still how people reason about counts in their heads: a count of about 100 is uncertain by about \(\pm 2\sqrt{100} = \pm 20\).

06

When the bell fails

The central limit theorem has conditions, and the most important is a finite standard deviation. The Cauchy distribution breaks it. It looks harmless, a bell with fatter tails, and is what you get by dividing one standard normal by another. But its tails are so heavy that it has no mean and no standard deviation: now and then a draw is so huge that it outweighs everything before it.

Running averages

number of draws so far, on a log scale

average of normal drawsaverage of Cauchy drawscenter, 0

The normal average closes in on 0, at the \(1/\sqrt n\) pace of section 4. The Cauchy average never does. It drifts, then jumps when one enormous draw arrives, and the average of 5,000 draws is no more reliable than a single draw. In fact it has exactly the same Cauchy distribution as one draw. Distributions with heavy tails like this, where the variance is infinite, turn up in finance, in insurance losses and in the sizes of cities and earthquakes; for them the bell is the wrong picture (see power laws).

Products instead of sums

Many things grow by multiplying: an investment that gains or loses a percentage each year, a population, a cell that grows by a random factor before dividing. The logarithm turns a product into a sum, \(\ln(U_1 U_2 \cdots U_n) = \ln U_1 + \dots + \ln U_n\), so the log of the product is approximately normal. The product itself is then lognormal: piled up on the left with a long tail to the right.

Twenty years of random growth

20,000 runslognormal curvemedianmean

Each year the value is multiplied by a random factor between 0.5 and 1.5, which averages exactly 1. So the mean final value stays at 1. But the typical run loses money: the median is well below 1, because a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to undo it. A few lucky runs end far above 1 and hold up the mean. Switch to the log view and the lopsided pile becomes a bell.

Dependence breaks the theorem too. If the pieces move together, as house prices in one city do, or as loans did in 2008, their sum varies far more than \(\sigma\sqrt n\), and the tails are much heavier than a normal predicts.

A lognormal with a large spread can look deceptively like a power law on a log–log plot; the power laws tutorial shows the trap.

07

Normals add up

The sum of two independent normal variables is exactly normal, not just approximately. Their means add, and so do their variances:

$$\begin{gathered} X \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2),\quad Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2) \\ \Rightarrow\ X + Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1 + \mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2) \end{gathered}$$

The standard deviations do not add. They combine like the sides of a right triangle: \(\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}\), always less than \(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2\). Independent errors partly cancel.

Add two normals

\(X\)\(Y\)\(X + Y\)20,000 simulated sums

Two more rules complete the toolkit. Scaling and shifting keep a normal normal: \(aX + b \sim \mathcal N(a\mu + b,\ a^2\sigma^2)\). And a difference is a sum with a minus sign, so \(X - Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1 - \mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)\): the variances still add. Comparing two noisy measurements is noisier than either one.

These rules also say why the central limit theorem lands on this curve. Take two independent standard normals, add them and divide by \(\sqrt 2\): the result is a standard normal again. The normal is the fixed point of “add up and rescale”. Any shape with finite variance that is added and rescaled over and over is pulled toward that fixed point, and the bell is the only place it can stop.

Where the formulas come from

The MGF, and everything from it

For the standard normal, complete the square in the exponent:

$$\begin{aligned} E[e^{tZ}] &= \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{tz - z^2/2}\,dz \\ &= e^{t^2/2}\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-(z - t)^2/2}\,dz \\ &= e^{t^2/2} \end{aligned}$$

since the last integral is the area under a shifted bell, 1. With \(X = \mu + \sigma Z\) this gives \(M(t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}\). Its logarithm, \(\mu t + \sigma^2t^2/2\), has no terms beyond \(t^2\), so every cumulant after the variance is 0: no skewness, no excess kurtosis. Multiplying two such MGFs adds the means and the variances, which is section 7.

Why the central limit theorem works

Let \(Y = (X - \mu)/\sigma\) have mean 0 and variance 1, so its MGF starts \(M_Y(t) = 1 + t^2/2 + \dots\). The standardized sum is \(Z_n = (Y_1 + \dots + Y_n)/\sqrt n\), and independence multiplies MGFs:

$$\begin{aligned} M_{Z_n}(t) &= M_Y\big(t/\sqrt n\big)^{n} \\ &= \Big(1 + \frac{t^2}{2n} + \dots\Big)^{n} \\ &\to e^{t^2/2} \end{aligned}$$

which is the standard normal’s MGF. The dropped terms are of order \(n^{-3/2}\) and vanish in the limit. Only the mean and the variance of one draw survive; every other detail of its shape is washed out. (A rigorous proof uses the characteristic function \(E[e^{itY}]\), which exists for every distribution, but the idea is the same.)

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Standardizing. \(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma\) is standard normal, so one function \(\Phi\) answers every question.
  2. Sums and differences. Independent normals add to a normal; means add and variances add (section 7).
  3. The central limit theorem. Sums and averages of many independent draws with finite variance are approximately normal (section 1).
  4. The square-root law. An average of \(n\) draws has standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) (section 4).
  5. Limit of the counting distributions. The binomial, Poisson and negative binomial all become normal as their means grow.
  6. Squares: chi-square. The sum of \(k\) squared independent standard normals follows the chi-square distribution with \(k\) degrees of freedom.
  7. Maximum entropy. Of all distributions with a given mean and variance, the normal has the largest entropy: it assumes the least beyond those two numbers.
09

Where you will meet it

Measurement error

A measurement is disturbed by many small independent effects, so its error is close to normal. Gauss used this to justify least squares: with normal errors, the least-squares fit is the most likely one.

Statistics

Confidence intervals, z-tests and much of regression rest on averages being approximately normal. When \(\sigma\) is estimated from a small sample, the t distribution takes the normal’s place.

Quality control

Shewhart’s control charts set limits at the mean \(\pm 3\sigma\). For a normal process only about 3 points in 1,000 fall outside by chance, so a point outside is worth a look.

Diffusion

A particle bumped by molecules takes a sum of tiny random steps, so its position is normal with a variance that grows in proportion to time. This is Brownian motion, as Einstein explained it in 1905.

Traits from many genes

Height and many other traits are shaped by thousands of genetic variants, each with a small effect, plus environment. Their sum is close to normal, as Fisher showed in 1918.

Electronic noise

Thermal noise in a resistor is the combined jitter of vast numbers of electrons, and is very accurately Gaussian. Signal processing and communications are built around it.

When it fails

Financial returns, insurance losses and network traffic have far heavier tails than a normal. Products are lognormal, not normal. Check the tails before trusting the bell.
