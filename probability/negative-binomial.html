The Negative Binomial Distribution

How many misses before the third basket? How many claims from a driver who may be careful or reckless? The same distribution answers both: it counts the failures before the \(r\)-th success, and it is the go-to model for counts that are more spread out than a Poisson allows.

01

Waiting for the r-th success

The geometric distribution waits for the first success. Now wait for the \(r\)-th: keep making independent Bernoulli trials with success probability \(p\) until \(r\) of them have succeeded, and count the failures along the way. Call that count \(Y\).

For \(Y = k\), the run has \(k + r\) trials, and the last one must be the \(r\)-th success. The other \(r - 1\) successes and the \(k\) failures can sit in any order among the first \(k + r - 1\) trials. Every such order has the same probability \(p^r q^k\), with \(q = 1 - p\), so

$$P(Y = k) = \binom{k + r - 1}{k}\, p^{\,r} q^{\,k}$$

for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) This is the negative binomial distribution, written \(Y \sim \text{NB}(r, p)\). With \(r = 1\) it is the geometric again.

Every way to get there

successfailurethe \(r\)-th success, always last

This is the path counting of the binomial tutorial with one twist: the last step is pinned. That is why the coefficient is \(\binom{k + r - 1}{k}\) and not \(\binom{k + r}{k}\).

Two conventions. This page counts failures, so \(Y\) starts at 0. That is what most software uses: dnbinom in R and scipy.stats.nbinom in Python. Some books count trials instead, \(X = Y + r\), starting at \(r\), with mean \(r/p\). It is the same experiment shifted by \(r\). The failure count is also the one that turns up in section 4, where there are no trials at all.

Why “negative”?

The coefficient can be written with a negative top number. Expanding \(\binom{-r}{k} = \frac{(-r)(-r-1)\cdots(-r-k+1)}{k!}\) and pulling out the \(k\) minus signs gives \(\binom{k + r - 1}{k} = (-1)^k\binom{-r}{k}\). So

$$P(Y = k) = \binom{-r}{k}\, p^{\,r}(-q)^k$$

These are the terms of Newton’s binomial series \((1 - q)^{-r} = \sum_k \binom{-r}{k}(-q)^k\), the binomial theorem with a negative power. Multiplied by \(p^r = (1-q)^r\), they add to 1. The binomial distribution comes from expanding \((q + p)^n\); this one comes from \(p^r(1 - q)^{-r}\).

02

The binomial in the mirror

The binomial fixes the number of trials \(n\) and counts the successes. The negative binomial fixes the number of successes \(r\) and counts the trials it takes. They look at the same sequence of trials from opposite ends, and one sentence links them: the \(r\)-th success comes by trial \(n\) exactly when the first \(n\) trials hold at least \(r\) successes. In symbols, with \(Y\) the failures and \(Y + r\) the trials,

$$\begin{aligned} P(Y + r \le n) &= P\big(\text{Bin}(n, p) \ge r\big) \end{aligned}$$

One sequence, two questions

The two answers can never disagree, sequence by sequence, so their probabilities are equal. This is how software computes the negative binomial’s cumulative probabilities: as a binomial tail, or as the regularized incomplete beta function \(I_p(r, k + 1)\) that both share.

03

A stack of geometric waits

Split the run at each success. The failures before the first success, then between the first and the second, and so on, are \(r\) separate waits. The trials are independent and nothing carries over from one success to the next, so the waits are independent geometric counts \(G_1, \dots, G_r\), each with mean \(q/p\) and variance \(q/p^2\) (counting failures). Adding them:

$$\begin{aligned} Y &= G_1 + G_2 + \dots + G_r \\ E[Y] &= \frac{rq}{p}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{rq}{p^{2}} \end{aligned}$$

For the hero’s shooter, \(r = 3\) and \(p = 0.4\): an average of \(3 \times 0.6/0.4 = 4.5\) misses, or 7.5 shots in all, with a standard deviation of about 3.4 misses.

With \(r = 1\) the bars fall from 0, the geometric shape. As \(r\) grows they pull away from 0, turn into a hump, and settle into a bell: a sum of many independent waits obeys the central limit theorem. Each stage adds the same skew, but it is diluted by \(\sqrt r\), so the skewness \((1 + q)/\sqrt{rq}\) fades slowly.

The same story in continuous time is the gamma (or Erlang) distribution, the sum of \(r\) exponential waits. The negative binomial is its discrete twin, just as the geometric is the exponential’s.

04

Counts that are too spread out

Now forget about trials. The Poisson distribution is the standard model for counts of events, and it has a fingerprint: its variance equals its mean. Real counts often fail that test. Accidents per driver, claims per policy, customers per store, sequencing reads per gene: the variance is well above the mean.

The usual reason is that the units differ. Each driver has their own accident rate \(\lambda\), and given that rate their count is Poisson. But a careful driver and a reckless one have very different rates, and that spread of rates adds to the spread of counts. If the rates follow a gamma distribution with shape \(r\) and mean \(\mu\), the counts follow a negative binomial:

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda &\sim \text{Gamma}(\text{shape } r,\ \text{mean } \mu) \\ Y \mid \lambda &\sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \\ \Rightarrow\ Y &\sim \text{NB}\Big(r,\ p = \frac{r}{r + \mu}\Big) \end{aligned}$$

Its mean is \(\mu\) and its variance is

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{r}$$

The first \(\mu\) is the Poisson noise every count has. The extra \(\mu^2/r\) is the spread of the rates themselves. A small \(r\) means very unequal units; as \(r \to \infty\) the rates all equal \(\mu\) and the Poisson comes back.

Slide \(r\) down and the rates spread out. Many units get rates near 0, so zeros pile up, while a few get high rates and produce a long right tail. The Poisson with the same mean misses both. Slide \(r\) to ∞ and the gamma collapses to a spike at \(\mu\): every unit is alike, and the counts are Poisson.

Where the formula comes from

Write the gamma with shape \(r\) and rate \(\beta = r/\mu\). Averaging the Poisson probability over it,

$$\begin{aligned} P(Y = k) &= \int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} \\ &\quad\times \frac{\beta^r\lambda^{r-1}e^{-\beta\lambda}}{\Gamma(r)}\,d\lambda \\ &= \frac{\beta^r}{k!\,\Gamma(r)} \\ &\quad\times \int_0^\infty \lambda^{k+r-1}e^{-(1+\beta)\lambda}\,d\lambda \\ &= \frac{\Gamma(k + r)}{k!\,\Gamma(r)} \\ &\quad\times \Big(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\Big)^{r}\Big(\frac{1}{1+\beta}\Big)^{k} \end{aligned}$$

With \(p = \beta/(1 + \beta) = r/(r + \mu)\) this is \(\binom{k + r - 1}{k}p^r q^k\) again. Nothing in the derivation needs \(r\) to be a whole number, so in this use \(r\) can be any positive number: \(\Gamma(k + r)/(k!\,\Gamma(r))\) takes the place of the binomial coefficient. The variance follows from the law of total variance: \(E[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid\lambda)] + \operatorname{Var}(E[Y\mid\lambda]) = \mu + \mu^2/r\).

The counting family. Three distributions cover most counts, and the variance-to-mean ratio tells them apart.

binomial \((8, 0.5)\): variance 2Poisson(4): variance 4negative binomial \((r = 2)\): variance 12

All three have mean 4. The binomial has a ceiling of \(n\) slots, so its variance \(npq\) is below its mean. The Poisson’s variance equals its mean. The negative binomial’s variance \(\mu + \mu^2/r\) is above it.

05

One distribution, many names

The two stories lead to two natural ways to describe the same distribution. Waiting for successes suggests \((r, p)\). Modeling counts suggests the mean \(\mu\) and a dispersion parameter that says how much extra variance there is. Software and fields mix the two freely, and mixing them up is the most common mistake made with this distribution.

WhereParametersVariance
This page, R dnbinom(k, size, prob), scipy nbinom(n, p)\(r,\ p\)\(rq/p^2\)
R dnbinom(k, size, mu); ecology’s and epidemiology’s \(k\); RNA-seq\(\mu,\ r\)\(\mu + \mu^2/r\)
statsmodels, many GLM texts\(\mu,\ \alpha = 1/r\)\(\mu + \alpha\mu^2\)
Counting trials, not failures: \(X = Y + r\)\(r,\ p\)\(rq/p^2\)

The conversions are

$$\begin{gathered} p = \frac{r}{r + \mu}, \qquad \mu = \frac{rq}{p}, \qquad \alpha = \frac1r \end{gathered}$$

For example, a mean of \(\mu = 4\) with size \(r = 2\) is \(p = 2/6 = 1/3\), dispersion \(\alpha = 0.5\), and variance \(4 + 16/2 = 12\). Note that a larger \(r\) means less overdispersion, while a larger \(\alpha\) means more. Before you trust a fitted value, check which one a program reports.

06

Mites on apple leaves

In 1953 Chester Bliss and Ronald Fisher used the negative binomial to describe how insects spread over plants. One of their examples counts European red mites on 150 apple leaves. Mites cluster: a leaf that suits them, or where a female laid eggs, collects many, while others stay clean. The counts have mean \(\bar x = 172/150 = 1.147\) but variance \(s^2 = 2.27\), about twice the mean.

Fit the mite counts

observed leavesexpected under the chosen fit

Both fits keep the mean at \(\bar x\) and differ only in \(r\). The method of moments matches the variance too: setting \(s^2 = \bar x + \bar x^2/r\) gives

$$\hat r = \frac{\bar x^{2}}{s^{2} - \bar x} = \frac{1.147^{2}}{2.274 - 1.147} \approx 1.17$$

Maximum likelihood picks the \(r\) under which the observed counts are most probable. It has no closed form, so the widget finds it numerically: \(\hat r \approx 1.02\), the value Bliss and Fisher reported. The Poisson expects far too few empty leaves and far too few crowded ones, and the chi-square test rejects it decisively. Either negative binomial fits well.

The method of moments only works when \(s^2 > \bar x\). If the variance is at or below the mean, there is no overdispersion to model and the Poisson (or a binomial) is the better choice.

Where the formulas come from

Everything from the PGF

A geometric count of failures has PGF \(E[s^G] = \sum_j q^j p\, s^j = p/(1 - qs)\). A sum of \(r\) independent ones multiplies them: \(G(s) = \big(p/(1-qs)\big)^r\). Then \(G'(1) = rq/p\) is the mean and \(G''(1) = r(r+1)q^2/p^2\) is \(E[Y(Y-1)]\), so

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(Y) &= G''(1) + G'(1) - G'(1)^2 \\ &= \frac{r(r+1)q^2}{p^2} + \frac{rq}{p} - \frac{r^2q^2}{p^2} \\ &= \frac{rq}{p^2} \end{aligned}$$

The PGF also makes sense for any real \(r > 0\), which is the gamma-mixture case of section 4.

Back to the Poisson

Hold the mean \(\mu\) fixed and let \(r \to \infty\), so \(p = r/(r+\mu) \to 1\). The PGF becomes

$$\begin{aligned} &\Big(\frac{1}{1 + \frac{\mu}{r}(1 - s)}\Big)^{r} \\ &\quad\to e^{-\mu(1-s)} = e^{\mu(s-1)} \end{aligned}$$

which is the Poisson’s PGF. The extra variance \(\mu^2/r\) shrinks to 0 along the way.

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. One success: the geometric. \(\text{NB}(1, p)\) is the geometric distribution of failures before the first success.
  2. Same \(p\) adds. \(\text{NB}(r_1, p) + \text{NB}(r_2, p) = \text{NB}(r_1 + r_2, p)\) for independent counts: waiting for \(r_1\) successes and then \(r_2\) more is waiting for \(r_1 + r_2\).
  3. The binomial mirror. \(P(Y + r \le n) = P(\text{Bin}(n, p) \ge r)\) (section 2).
  4. A gamma mixture of Poissons. If \(\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(r, \text{mean } \mu)\) and \(Y \mid \lambda \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\), then \(Y \sim \text{NB}(r, r/(r+\mu))\) (section 4).
  5. The Poisson limit. With the mean held fixed, \(r \to \infty\) gives back the Poisson.
  6. The continuous twin: the gamma. Shrink each trial to a slice of time, as the exponential tutorial does for the geometric, and the wait for the \(r\)-th event becomes \(\text{Gamma}(r)\).
08

Where you will meet it

Genomics

The number of sequencing reads mapping to a gene varies between samples far more than Poisson noise allows. DESeq2 and edgeR, the standard tools for RNA-seq, model the counts as negative binomial with a dispersion fitted per gene.

Insurance

Claims per policyholder are overdispersed because drivers differ. A gamma-distributed risk with Poisson claims gives the negative binomial, and bonus-malus pricing updates each driver’s rate from their own record.

Accident proneness

Greenwood and Yule introduced the gamma–Poisson mixture in 1920 to describe accidents among munitions workers: a few workers had many accidents, more than equal risks would produce.

Ecology

Insects on plants, parasites in hosts and seedlings in plots are clumped. Ecologists read the fitted \(r\) (usually written \(k\)) as an index of aggregation: the smaller \(k\), the more clumped.

Superspreading

The number of people each infected person infects is often negative binomial. For SARS in 2003 the estimated \(k\) was about 0.16: most cases infected no one, and a few infected dozens.

Web and sales counts

Visits per user, purchases per customer and messages per account are overdispersed, because a few heavy users dominate. Count regressions on such data default to the negative binomial.

When it fails

Some units can never have events at all, which adds zeros that a zero-inflated model handles better (see the Poisson tutorial). Very heavy tails, such as followers per account, can be too extreme even for the negative binomial.
