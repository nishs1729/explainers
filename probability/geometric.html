The Geometric Distribution

How many tries until it works? Shots until the first basket, rolls until the first six, retries until a message gets through. When every try has the same chance and the tries are independent, the count follows the geometric distribution.

01

Waiting for the first success

The binomial distribution fixes the number of trials and counts the successes. Turn the question around: keep making Bernoulli trials until the first success, and count how many trials that took.

Call that count \(X\). The first success comes on trial \(k\) exactly when the first \(k-1\) trials fail and trial \(k\) succeeds. With success probability \(p\) and \(q = 1 - p\), independence lets the probabilities multiply:

$$P(X = k) = \underbrace{q \cdot q \cdots q}_{k-1 \text{ failures}} \cdot\, p = q^{\,k-1}p$$

for \(k = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) This is the geometric distribution, written \(X \sim \text{Geometric}(p)\). The name comes from the bars: each one is \(q\) times the one before, a geometric sequence. There is no upper limit, since a long enough losing streak is always possible, but the bars still add to 1:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} q^{\,k-1}p = \frac{p}{1-q} = 1$$

Two conventions. This page counts trials, including the success, so \(X\) starts at 1. Many books and software libraries count the failures before the first success instead: \(Y = X - 1\), starting at 0, with \(P(Y = j) = q^{\,j}p\) and mean \(q/p\). Both describe the same experiment. Check which one a formula or a function uses before trusting a number from it.

The conditions are the binomial ones minus the fixed \(n\): two outcomes per trial, the same \(p\) every time, and independent trials. The hero at the top shows it in action. A player who makes 30% of their shots needs 1 shot 30% of the time, 2 shots \(0.7 \times 0.3 = 21\%\) of the time, 3 shots \(14.7\%\) of the time, and so on.

02

The shape, bar by bar

Every geometric distribution has the same shape: the tallest bar at \(k = 1\), then a steady decline in which each bar is \(q\) times the one before. The mode is always 1, whatever \(p\) is. Succeeding on the very first try is always the single most likely outcome, even when \(p\) is small, because every later outcome needs that first try to fail and something more.

The chance of still waiting after \(k\) trials has an even simpler form. It is just the chance that the first \(k\) trials all fail:

$$P(X > k) = q^{\,k}$$

That tail shrinks by the same factor \(q\) at every step. On a logarithmic scale a constant factor is a constant step, so the tail becomes a straight line with slope \(\log q\). This is the quickest way to recognize geometric data.

Small \(p\) gives a long, slow decline: at \(p = 0.05\) there is still a 36% chance of needing more than 20 trials. Large \(p\) gives a cliff: at \(p = 0.9\) almost everything is over by the second trial. At \(p = 1\) the first trial always succeeds, and the whole distribution is a single bar at 1.

03

On average, 1/p tries

The average wait has a neat derivation that uses no sums at all. Make the first trial. With probability \(p\) it succeeds and you are done after 1 trial. With probability \(q\) it fails, and you are back where you started, facing a fresh wait with the same average \(E\), but one trial down. So

$$E = 1 + q\,E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E[X] = \frac{1}{p}$$

This is first-step analysis: condition on what the first trial does. A die needs 6 rolls on average to show a six. A 1-in-100 chance needs 100 tries on average. The rule matches intuition: if one try in six succeeds in the long run, the successes are on average six tries apart.

The same trick applied to \(X^2\) gives the variance:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &= \frac{1-p}{p^2} = \frac{q}{p^2} \\ \text{SD}(X) &= \frac{\sqrt{q}}{p} \end{aligned}$$

For small \(p\) the standard deviation is almost as large as the mean. Waits are wildly variable: for the die, a typical wait is 6 rolls give or take 5.5. Because of the long right tail, the median is well below the mean. For the die it is 4: half of all waits for a six are over within 4 rolls, even though the average is 6. In general the median is the smallest \(m\) with \(q^{\,m} \le \tfrac12\).

The same results from the series

Differentiate the geometric series \(\sum_{k\ge0} q^k = 1/(1-q)\) with respect to \(q\):

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k\,q^{\,k-1} = \frac{1}{(1-q)^2} = \frac{1}{p^2}$$

so \(E[X] = p \cdot 1/p^2 = 1/p\). Differentiating once more gives \(\sum k(k-1)q^{k-2} = 2/p^3\), hence \(E[X(X-1)] = pq \cdot 2/p^3 = 2q/p^2\), and

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[X(X-1)] + E[X] - E[X]^2 \\ &= \frac{2q}{p^2} + \frac1p - \frac1{p^2} = \frac{q}{p^2} \end{aligned}$$

The running average wanders early, pulled up by every long run, and settles on \(1/p\). Small \(p\) settles slowly: the occasional very long run keeps yanking it upward, which is the large variance at work.

04

No memory: nothing is ever due

A roulette wheel has come up red 10 times in a row. Is black “due”? Many gamblers think so. The geometric distribution says no, and says it exactly. Given that the first \(s\) trials have failed, the chance that you need more than \(t\) further trials is

$$\begin{aligned} P(X > s + t \mid X > s) &= \frac{q^{\,s+t}}{q^{\,s}} \\ &= q^{\,t} = P(X > t) \end{aligned}$$

The \(s\) failures cancel out. After any losing streak, the remaining wait has exactly the same geometric distribution as a fresh start. This is memorylessness. The trials are independent, so the wheel cannot remember the streak, and nor can the distribution.

Is the next one due?

fresh wait: \(P(X = k)\)remaining wait after \(s\) failures: \(P(X = s + k \mid X > s)\)

The simulation plays many independent sequences, picks out the ones that began with \(s\) straight failures, and checks the very next trial. Its success rate stays at \(p\), however long the streak. A long streak is rare to see, but once you are in one it predicts nothing.

The geometric is the only distribution on \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) with this property. Its continuous counterpart, the exponential distribution, is the only memoryless waiting time in continuous time.

Why nothing else is memoryless

Write \(S(k) = P(X > k)\), with \(S(0) = 1\). Memorylessness with \(t = 1\) says \(S(s+1)/S(s) = S(1)\) for every \(s\): the chance of surviving one more trial never changes. Call it \(q = S(1)\). Then \(S(k) = q^{\,k}\) for every \(k\), and \(P(X = k) = S(k-1) - S(k) = q^{\,k-1}(1-q)\). That is the geometric distribution with \(p = 1 - q\).

05

From trials to smooth time

Many waits have no separate trials: a radioactive atom can decay at any instant, a customer can walk in at any moment. To reuse the geometric, chop time into short slices of width \(\Delta t\), and let each slice “succeed” with probability \(p = \lambda\Delta t\), where \(\lambda\) is the average number of events per unit of time. The slice holding the first event is geometric, and the wait is about \(\Delta t\) times that count. Its average is \(\Delta t/p = 1/\lambda\), whatever the slice width.

Thinner slices, rate λ = 0.8

first event in this slice (bar area = probability)\(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\)

As the slices shrink, the staircase melts into the smooth curve \(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\): the exponential distribution. The geometric is its discrete twin, and the memorylessness of section 4 survives the limit unchanged. The next tutorial starts exactly here.

06

Collecting a full set

A cereal box holds one of \(n\) different stickers, each equally likely. How many boxes until you have the whole set? This is the coupon collector’s problem, and it is a chain of geometric waits.

The first box always gives a new sticker. Once you have \(i\) different ones, a box is new with probability \(p_i = (n - i)/n\), so the wait for the next new sticker is \(\text{Geometric}(p_i)\), with mean \(n/(n-i)\). Adding the stages up:

$$\begin{aligned} E[\text{boxes}] &= \frac{n}{n} + \frac{n}{n-1} + \dots + \frac{n}{1} \\ &= n\,H_n \approx n\ln n + 0.577\,n \end{aligned}$$

where \(H_n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1n\) is the \(n\)-th harmonic number. For 10 stickers that is 29.3 boxes on average, for 50 stickers 225.

The stages are wildly uneven. Early on almost every box is new. At the end you need, on average, \(n\) boxes for the last sticker alone. That is more than the whole first half of the set, which takes only about \(n \ln 2 \approx 0.69\,n\). The last few stickers are where the money goes, which is exactly why sticker albums are a good business.

07

Going backwards: what is p?

Suppose you watch \(m\) independent runs and they take \(x_1, \dots, x_m\) trials, \(S = \sum x_i\) trials in all. Each run contributes one success and \(x_i - 1\) failures, so the likelihood is

$$L(p) = p^{\,m}\,q^{\,S - m}$$

It peaks at the success rate you actually saw, successes over trials:

$$\hat p = \frac{m}{S} = \frac{1}{\bar x}$$

One over the average run length, the mirror image of \(E[X] = 1/p\). It slightly overestimates \(p\) for small \(m\), because \(1/\bar x\) is pulled up by short runs more than it is pulled down by long ones.

For an exact 95% interval, use the binomial again. Needing at most \(S\) trials for \(m\) successes is the same event as getting at least \(m\) successes in the first \(S\) trials. So the interval is the set of \(p\) under which the observed total \(S\) is not in either 2.5% tail of its distribution, and both tails are binomial probabilities. It is the analogue of the Clopper–Pearson interval, and it covers the true \(p\) at least 95% of the time.

With one run the interval is huge: a single success on the first try is consistent with almost any \(p\) above a few percent. The width shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{m}\), so pinning \(p\) down to ±0.02 near 0.2 takes a few hundred runs.

PMF

\(q^{\,k-1}p,\ k = 1, 2, \dots\)

\(P(X > k) = q^{\,k}\)

\(1\)

Median

\(\left\lceil -1/\log_2 q \right\rceil\)

Skewness

\((2-p)/\sqrt{q}\)

Excess kurtosis

\(6 + p^2/q\)

PGF

\(E[z^X] = \dfrac{pz}{1 - qz}\)
for \(|z| < 1/q\)

MGF

\(\dfrac{p\,e^{t}}{1 - q\,e^{t}}\)

Entropy

\(\dfrac{-q\log_2 q - p\log_2 p}{p}\)

The median formula takes the smaller value when \(-1/\log_2 q\) is a whole number (then \(P(X \le m)\) is exactly \(\tfrac12\)). The entropy is the Bernoulli entropy of one trial times the average number of trials, \(1/p\).

  1. Built from Bernoulli trials. Each trial is \(\text{Bernoulli}(p)\). The geometric counts how many it takes to get the first 1, and the binomial counts the 1s in a fixed number of them.
  2. Counting failures instead. \(Y = X - 1 \sim\) failures before the first success, with \(P(Y = j) = q^{\,j}p\), mean \(q/p\) and the same variance \(q/p^2\).
  3. The only memoryless discrete distribution. \(P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)\), and no other distribution on \(1, 2, \dots\) does this (section 4).
  4. Its continuous twin is the exponential. With \(p = \lambda\Delta t\) and \(\Delta t \to 0\), \(\Delta t \cdot X\) becomes \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\). In reverse, rounding an \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) wait up to a whole number gives a geometric with \(p = 1 - e^{-\lambda}\).
  5. The first of several. If independent geometric waits with \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) run side by side, the first to finish is geometric with \(p = 1 - q_1q_2\): a trial “succeeds” when either one does.
  6. Sums wait for later successes. Adding \(r\) independent \(\text{Geometric}(p)\) waits gives the number of trials until the \(r\)-th success, the negative binomial distribution. With different \(p\)s, as in the coupon collector, the sum has no special name.
  7. Maximum entropy. Among all distributions on \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) with a given mean, the geometric has the largest entropy. It is the least informative, most “random” way to have that average wait.
09

Where you will meet it

Retries

A network packet lost with probability \(\ell\) is resent until it arrives. The number of sends is geometric with \(p = 1 - \ell\), so a 20% loss rate means 1.25 sends on average, and timeouts are planned from the geometric tail.

Testing until failure

Inspect items off a line until the first defect. With a defect rate of 1 in 500, the count is geometric with mean 500, a simple way to monitor whether the rate has changed.

Proof of work

Each hash a bitcoin miner computes wins with a tiny fixed probability. The number of hashes until a valid block is geometric, and so, in continuous time, the gaps between blocks are close to exponential.

Streaks

The length of a run of identical outcomes, such as heads in a row or wins in a row, is geometric when the trials are independent. Real streaks that are much longer than the geometric allows hint that the trials are not independent.

Genetics

Along a DNA sequence, the distance to the next occurrence of a given base or short pattern, or the length of a run of one base, is roughly geometric when the bases are close to independent.

When it fails

If the chance changes from try to try, the geometric is wrong: a learner improves with practice, a machine wears out, a salesperson gets more desperate. Plot the tail \(P(X > k)\) on a log scale: a straight line means geometric, a bend means \(p\) is changing.
