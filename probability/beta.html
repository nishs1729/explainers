The Beta Distribution

A coin lands heads with some probability \(p\), but nobody has told you what it is. After a few flips you have a rough idea; after a few hundred, a sharp one. The beta distribution describes that kind of knowledge: a distribution for a probability, living between 0 and 1.

01

A probability of a probability

Every distribution so far took its probability as known. The Bernoulli trial succeeds with probability \(p\), the binomial counts successes at that \(p\). In practice \(p\) is usually the thing you don’t know: the click-through rate of an ad, the share of voters who agree, a player’s true batting skill.

Being unsure about \(p\) means spreading belief over its possible values, from 0 to 1. Some values are more plausible than others, so the belief is a density on \([0, 1]\). The standard family of such densities is the beta distribution with two positive parameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\):

$$f(p) = \frac{p^{\alpha - 1}(1 - p)^{\beta - 1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}, \qquad 0 < p < 1$$

The top is the important part. It looks like the probability of a run of flips, \(p^{\text{heads}}(1-p)^{\text{tails}}\), with \(\alpha - 1\) heads and \(\beta - 1\) tails. The beta function \(B(\alpha, \beta)\) on the bottom just makes the area 1. It is built from the gamma function:

$$\begin{aligned} B(\alpha, \beta) &= \int_0^1 p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}\,dp \\ &= \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\,\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)} \end{aligned}$$

The mean is \(\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)\), the share of the pseudo-flips that came up heads. The sum \(\alpha + \beta\) acts like the number of flips: the bigger it is, the narrower the curve.

Shapes

Two parameters and one formula give a surprising range of shapes. Try the presets, then drag the sliders.

Shape a beta

\(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) densitymiddle 95%mean
  • \(\alpha = \beta = 1\): flat. \(\text{Beta}(1, 1)\) is the uniform distribution: every \(p\) equally plausible.
  • Both below 1: U-shaped. The density piles up at 0 and 1. The belief is “the coin is almost always heads or almost always tails, but I don’t know which”. \(\text{Beta}(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) is called the arcsine distribution.
  • Both above 1: a hump with its peak at \((\alpha - 1)/(\alpha + \beta - 2)\). Equal parameters give a symmetric hump around ½; unequal ones lean toward the smaller parameter’s end.
  • One below 1, one above: J-shaped, rising without limit at one end.

The variance is \(\mu(1 - \mu)/(\alpha + \beta + 1)\), where \(\mu\) is the mean. For a given mean, the spread depends only on \(\alpha + \beta\), often called the concentration. Doubling both parameters keeps the center and narrows the curve by about \(\sqrt 2\).

03

The k-th smallest of n

Beta distributions also turn up with no unknown coin at all. Drop \(n\) points at random, uniformly, on the interval from 0 to 1, and look at the \(k\)-th smallest. It follows

$$U_{(k)} \sim \text{Beta}(k,\ n - k + 1)$$

The reason is a counting argument. For the \(k\)-th smallest to sit at \(x\), one point must land at \(x\), \(k - 1\) to its left (each with probability \(x\)) and \(n - k\) to its right (each with probability \(1 - x\)). That gives a density proportional to \(x^{k-1}(1-x)^{n-k}\), which is the beta with \(\alpha = k\) and \(\beta = n - k + 1\). The constant counts the ways to choose which points go where, \(n!/\big((k-1)!\,(n-k)!\big)\), and it equals \(1/B(k, n-k+1)\).

other pointsthe \(k\)-th smallestits value over many drops\(\text{Beta}(k, n - k + 1)\)

With \(n = 1\) there is one point and it is uniform: \(\text{Beta}(1, 1)\). The smallest of \(n\) leans toward 0, the largest toward 1, and the middle one, the sample median when \(n\) is odd, is a symmetric hump around ½. On average the \(k\)-th smallest sits at \(k/(n + 1)\): the \(n\) points split the interval into \(n + 1\) gaps of equal average length. The largest, \(k = n\), is \(\text{Beta}(n, 1)\); how the largest of many values behaves for other sources is the subject of extreme value distributions.

The same fact explains the binomial’s link to the beta: \(Y \le k\) successes in \(n\) trials happens exactly when the \((k+1)\)-th smallest of \(n\) uniforms is above \(p\).

04

From two gammas

A third route goes through waiting times. Let \(X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\) and \(Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\beta, \lambda)\) be independent, with the same rate. Then the share of the total that \(X\) makes up is a beta:

$$\frac{X}{X + Y} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$$

Picture one Poisson process. \(X\) is the time to its first \(\alpha\) events and \(Y\) the time to the next \(\beta\), so \(X + Y\) is the time to event \(\alpha + \beta\). The fraction \(X/(X + Y)\) says where the \(\alpha\)-th event falls inside that stretch, and given the total, the earlier events are scattered like uniform points: this is the order statistic of section 3 again. The rate cancels, and remarkably the share is independent of the total \(X + Y\).

Split a gamma total

\(X/(X+Y)\), 20,000 pairs\(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\)

This is how software draws beta random numbers: draw two gammas and take the ratio. It is also where \(B(\alpha, \beta) = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)\) comes from (see section 6). With more than two gammas, the shares \(X_i/\sum X_j\) follow the Dirichlet distribution, the beta’s generalization to several proportions that add up to 1.

05

Learning from flips

Back to the unknown coin. Describe what you believe about \(p\) before flipping as \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\). Then flip it \(n\) times and see \(h\) heads and \(t = n - h\) tails. Bayes’ rule says the new belief is proportional to the old one times the chance of the data, \(p^h(1-p)^t\). The exponents just add:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{before: } &\text{Beta}(\alpha,\ \beta) \\ \text{after: } &\text{Beta}(\alpha + h,\ \beta + t) \end{aligned}$$

So \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) work like imaginary earlier flips: a \(\text{Beta}(10, 10)\) belief is as firm as having already seen 10 heads and 10 tails. Real flips are added on top.

A coin with a hidden bias

belief before flippingbelief after the flips95% intervaltrue bias (once revealed)

The center of the new belief, \((\alpha + h)/(\alpha + \beta + n)\), is a compromise between the starting guess \(\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)\) and the observed share \(h/n\), weighted by \(\alpha + \beta\) against \(n\). With few flips the starting belief matters; with many, the data swamp it and different starting beliefs end up in nearly the same place. Starting from \(\text{Beta}(1, 1)\), the chance that the next flip is heads is \((h + 1)/(n + 2)\), Laplace’s rule of succession: even after 3 heads in 3 flips it is 4/5, not certainty.

The shaded interval is a credible interval: given the starting belief and the flips, \(p\) lies inside it with probability 95%. That reads the way people often wrongly read a confidence interval, and with a flat start and plenty of data the two are close. This is only a preview: a later series on Bayesian inference will build these ideas properly, starting from this beta–binomial model.

Where the formulas come from

The beta function from two gammas

Multiply two gamma integrals and change variables to the total \(s = x + y\) and the share \(u = x/s\), so \(x = us\), \(y = (1-u)s\) and \(dx\,dy = s\,ds\,du\):

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta) &= \int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1}y^{\beta-1} \\ &\quad\times e^{-x-y}\,dx\,dy \\ &= \int_0^\infty s^{\alpha+\beta-1}e^{-s}\,ds \\ &\quad\times \int_0^1 u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}\,du \\ &= \Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\,B(\alpha, \beta) \end{aligned}$$

The integral splits into a part in \(s\) alone and a part in \(u\) alone. That is the independence of the total and the share in section 4, and the \(u\) part is the beta density.

Mean and variance

Moments are ratios of beta functions. Using \(\Gamma(z + 1) = z\,\Gamma(z)\),

$$\begin{aligned} E[X] &= \frac{B(\alpha + 1, \beta)}{B(\alpha, \beta)} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} \\ E[X^2] &= \frac{B(\alpha + 2, \beta)}{B(\alpha, \beta)} \\ &= \frac{\alpha(\alpha + 1)}{(\alpha + \beta)(\alpha + \beta + 1)} \end{aligned}$$

and \(E[X^2] - E[X]^2\) simplifies to \(\alpha\beta/\big((\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)\big) = \mu(1-\mu)/(\alpha+\beta+1)\).

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Uniform is a beta. \(\text{Beta}(1, 1)\) is the uniform distribution on \([0, 1]\).
  2. Order statistics. The \(k\)-th smallest of \(n\) uniforms is \(\text{Beta}(k, n - k + 1)\) (section 3).
  3. Ratios of gammas. \(X/(X+Y) \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) for independent \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\) and \(\text{Gamma}(\beta, \lambda)\), independent of \(X + Y\) (section 4).
  4. Conjugate to the binomial. A \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) belief about \(p\) becomes \(\text{Beta}(\alpha + h, \beta + t)\) after \(h\) successes and \(t\) failures (section 5).
  5. Mirror image. \(1 - X \sim \text{Beta}(\beta, \alpha)\).
  6. Large parameters: normal. With \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) both large, the beta is close to a normal with the same mean and variance.
07

Where you will meet it

Click-through rates

An ad shown 10 times with 3 clicks has an observed rate of 30%, but that says little. Fitting a beta to the rates of many ads and updating it with each ad’s own clicks pulls small samples toward the typical rate.

Batting averages

Early in a season a player batting .450 after 20 at-bats is almost certainly not that good. A beta belief built from league-wide averages, updated with the player’s hits, gives a far better forecast.

Project estimates (PERT)

Given an optimistic, a most likely and a pessimistic duration \(a\), \(m\), \(b\), PERT fits a beta stretched over \([a, b]\) and estimates the mean as \((a + 4m + b)/6\).

A/B testing

Two versions of a page each get a beta belief about their conversion rate. The chance that B beats A is the chance that a draw from one beta exceeds a draw from the other.

Genetics and ecology

Allele frequencies that drift between populations around a common value are modeled as beta (the Balding–Nichols model), and so are many other proportions: cover, survival, shares of time.

Ranks and order statistics

The value at a given rank in a random sample, such as the median of 11 readings, has a beta distribution on the probability scale. This underlies distribution-free intervals for quantiles.

When it fails

Proportions that can be exactly 0 or 1, or that are bunched in ways a two-parameter curve can’t follow (several separate humps), need mixtures or other models. More than two categories need the Dirichlet.
