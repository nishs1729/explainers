Extreme Value Distributions

Dams, sea walls and bridges are not built for the average day. They are built for the worst one. The largest of many random values has its own laws, and just as sums settle into the bell curve, maxima settle into a small family of shapes.

01

Take the biggest

Draw \(n\) independent values \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), each with cumulative distribution function \(F\), and keep the largest, \(M_n = \max_i X_i\). The largest is at most \(x\) exactly when every value is at most \(x\), so

$$P(M_n \le x) = F(x)^n$$

That one line contains everything, but it hides how differently maxima behave from averages. An average settles down: by the square-root law it closes in on the mean. The maximum never settles. It keeps creeping up as \(n\) grows, and how fast it creeps depends only on the tail of \(F\), on how rare very large values are.

How the biggest grows

uniform on 0 to 1standard normalexponential, mean 1Pareto, tail exponent 2

The median of the largest of \(n\) values, \(F^{-1}\big(2^{-1/n}\big)\), for \(n\) from 1 to 100,000 on a log scale.

  • A bounded source such as the uniform can never pass its upper end. The maximum crowds up against it.
  • The normal’s tails thin out so fast that its maximum grows only like \(\sqrt{2\ln n}\): typically about 4.8 standard deviations for a million values, and about 6.1 for a billion.
  • The exponential’s maximum grows like \(\ln n\): ten times the data adds a fixed amount, about 2.3.
  • A heavy, power-law tail is different in kind. With tail exponent 2 the maximum grows like \(\sqrt n\), without limit, and a single value can dwarf all the others put together.
02

Three families

The maximum drifts, so to see its shape it has to be shifted and rescaled, just as the central limit theorem standardizes a sum. The extremal types theorem of Fisher and Tippett (1928), made rigorous by Gnedenko (1943), says that if the rescaled maximum settles into any shape at all, that shape is one of only three:

  • Gumbel, for tails that thin out exponentially or faster: normal, exponential, gamma, lognormal.
  • Fréchet, for heavy, power-law tails, where \(P(X > x)\) falls like \(x^{-\alpha}\).
  • Weibull (in its reversed form), for sources with a hard upper end, such as the uniform.

All three are one family, the generalized extreme value (GEV) distribution, with location \(\mu\), scale \(\sigma\) and a shape \(\xi\):

$$\begin{aligned} G(x) &= \exp\!\big(-(1 + \xi z)^{-1/\xi}\big) \\ z &= (x - \mu)/\sigma \end{aligned}$$

A positive \(\xi\) is Fréchet (for a power tail \(\xi = 1/\alpha\)), a negative \(\xi\) is Weibull, and the limit \(\xi \to 0\) is Gumbel, \(G(x) = \exp(-e^{-z})\).

Each block’s maximum is standardized by the median and the spread (the distance between the quartiles) of the maximum’s own distribution, so the chart shows shape only. The exponential reaches its Gumbel limit almost at once. The normal is famously slow: its maximum is less skewed than the Gumbel, most visibly for small blocks, and the gap closes only at the pace of \(1/\ln n\), because the normal tail keeps getting thinner. Compare the dashed exact curve with the gold limit at \(n = 10\). The uniform turns into a reversed exponential pressed against its upper end, and the Pareto keeps a long right tail, the Fréchet.

Why the exponential gives the Gumbel

For \(\text{Exp}(1)\) values, \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-x}\). Shift the maximum by \(\ln n\):

$$\begin{aligned} P(M_n - \ln n \le x) &= \big(1 - e^{-x - \ln n}\big)^n \\ &= \Big(1 - \frac{e^{-x}}{n}\Big)^n \\ &\to \exp\!\big(-e^{-x}\big) \end{aligned}$$

No rescaling is needed at all, only the shift: the exponential’s maximum is \(\ln n\) plus a Gumbel wobble.

03

The weakest link

Minimums are maximums upside down: \(\min_i X_i = -\max_i(-X_i)\), and \(P(\min > x) = (1 - F(x))^n\). The classic case is a chain. It breaks at its weakest link, so its strength is the minimum of its links’ strengths.

Strengths are positive, so the minimum is pressed against 0, and what decides its shape is how often a link is very weak. If \(P(\text{strength} \le x)\) behaves like \(c\,x^k\) near 0, the strength of a long chain approaches the Weibull distribution with shape \(k\):

$$\begin{aligned} &P(\text{chain strength} > x) \\ &\quad\approx \exp\!\big(-(x/\lambda)^k\big) \end{aligned}$$

Waloddi Weibull proposed it in 1939 for the strength of materials, which fail at their worst flaw. The same reasoning makes it the standard model for failure times of systems that fail when their first part does.

Every link has average strength 1, yet a chain of 100 is far weaker. This is the size effect: a longer chain, a longer cable or a bigger block of ceramic has more chances to contain a bad flaw. Under the Weibull model, making the chain 10 times longer divides its typical strength by \(10^{1/k}\). Flawed materials (small \(k\)) are punished hardest by size; uniform ones (large \(k\)) barely notice.

With \(k = 1\) the Weibull is the exponential: a system whose weakest part fails at a constant rate is memoryless. Shapes above 1 describe wear-out, where the failure rate rises with age.

04

Return periods

Engineers describe rare levels by their return period. The “100-year flood” is the river level whose chance of being exceeded in any given year is 1 in 100: the 99th percentile of the yearly maximum. It is an average spacing, not a schedule. Floods don’t wait their turn, and the chance of at least one 100-year flood in \(m\) years is

$$\begin{aligned} &P(\text{at least one in } m \text{ years}) \\ &\quad= 1 - \Big(1 - \frac{1}{T}\Big)^{m} \end{aligned}$$

With \(T = 100\): about 26% over a 30-year mortgage, and in a full century only \(1 - 0.99^{100} \approx 63\%\). More than a third of centuries see no 100-year flood at all, and some see two or three.

The yearly peaks here follow a Gumbel distribution with location 200 and scale 40 (in cubic meters per second), so the \(T\)-year level is \(200 - 40\ln\!\big(-\ln(1 - 1/T)\big)\). In practice nobody knows the true distribution. It is fitted to a few decades of records and then stretched to 100 or 1,000 years, far beyond the data, and the uncertainty of that stretch is often larger than people expect. A changing climate adds another problem: the theory assumes every year is drawn from the same distribution.

05

Over the threshold

Keeping only one maximum per year throws away the second-worst flood of a bad year. The alternative is to keep every value above a high threshold \(u\) and study the excess \(Y = X - u\). For a high enough threshold, the theorem of Balkema and de Haan (1974) and Pickands (1975) says the excesses follow the generalized Pareto distribution:

$$P(Y > y) = \Big(1 + \frac{\xi\,y}{\sigma}\Big)^{-1/\xi}, \qquad y > 0$$

with the same shape \(\xi\) as the GEV for that source. With \(\xi = 0\) it is \(e^{-y/\sigma}\), the exponential: for light-tailed sources, how far a value overshoots a high bar is memoryless. With \(\xi > 0\) it is a power-law tail, and with \(\xi < 0\) the excess runs into a hard upper limit.

This peaks-over-threshold approach is the workhorse of insurance and finance, where the size of the largest losses matters more than how often they occur. The catch is choosing \(u\): too low and the approximation fails, too high and too few values are left.

Where the formulas come from

Why only three shapes: max-stability

Suppose the rescaled maximum \((M_n - b_n)/a_n\) of some source tends to a distribution \(G\). Split \(nk\) values into \(k\) blocks of \(n\): the maximum of all of them is the maximum of \(k\) block maxima. So \(G^k\) must itself be \(G\) after a shift and rescale:

$$G(x)^k = G(A_k x + B_k) \quad \text{for every } k$$

Distributions with this max-stability are exactly the GEV family. The cases \(A_k = 1\), \(A_k > 1\) and \(A_k < 1\) give the Gumbel, Fréchet and Weibull types. The normal plays the same role for sums: it is the only shape that stays the same when independent copies are added and rescaled.

Return levels

The \(T\)-year level \(x_T\) solves \(G(x_T) = 1 - 1/T\). Inverting the CDF gives

$$\begin{aligned} x_T &= \mu + \frac{\sigma}{\xi}\Big(y_T^{-\xi} - 1\Big) \\ y_T &= -\ln(1 - 1/T) \approx 1/T \end{aligned}$$

For the Gumbel, \(x_T = \mu - \sigma\ln y_T \approx \mu + \sigma\ln T\): each tenfold longer return period adds the same amount, \(\sigma\ln 10\). For a Fréchet the levels grow like \(T^{\xi}\), much faster.

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. The maximum’s CDF. \(P(\max \le x) = F(x)^n\) and \(P(\min > x) = (1 - F(x))^n\) for independent values.
  2. Three limits, one family. Rescaled maxima can only tend to a Gumbel, Fréchet or Weibull: the GEV.
  3. The tail decides. Exponential-type tails give Gumbel, power tails with exponent \(\alpha\) give Fréchet with \(\xi = 1/\alpha\), a finite upper end gives Weibull.
  4. Max-stable. The maximum of independent GEV values is GEV with the same shape.
  5. Weakest links. Minimums of positive strengths tend to the Weibull, whose shape is set by how often a part is very weak (section 3).
  6. Threshold excesses. Values above a high level follow a generalized Pareto with the same \(\xi\) (section 5).
  7. The uniform’s order statistics. The largest of \(n\) uniforms is \(\text{Beta}(n, 1)\), and \(n(1 - \max)\) tends to an exponential: the reversed Weibull in its simplest form.
07

Where you will meet it

Floods and sea walls

Dams, levees and storm-surge barriers are designed for return periods of 100 to 10,000 years. After the 1953 North Sea flood, the Netherlands set the safety standard for its most populated coast at a 1-in-10,000-year storm surge.

Wind and snow loads

Building codes specify design wind speeds and snow loads as return levels, often the 50-year value, estimated from records of yearly maxima at weather stations.

Insurance and reinsurance

The largest claims, from hurricanes or industrial disasters, drive the cost of cover. Insurers fit generalized Pareto tails to losses above a threshold to price them.

Strength of materials

The strength of brittle materials such as ceramics and glass is described by a Weibull distribution, and its shape (the Weibull modulus) is a standard quality measure.

Reliability

Lifetimes of bearings, electronics and other components are routinely fitted with Weibull distributions, whose shape tells early failures (below 1) from wear-out (above 1).

Finance

Risk managers study the worst daily losses with extreme value methods, because a normal model badly understates how big they get.

When it fails

The theory assumes independent values from an unchanging distribution. Climate trends, clustered storms and short records all break that, and extrapolating to 1,000-year events from 30 years of data is risky.
