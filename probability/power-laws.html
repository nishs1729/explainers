A visual introduction

Power Laws

Nobody is ten times taller than average, but plenty of cities are ten times bigger than average, and a few are a thousand times bigger. When size breeds size, there is no typical value: a handful of giants and a long tail of small ones. These are power laws.

01

No typical size

Ask for the height of a typical adult and the question has a good answer: heights follow roughly a normal distribution, and nobody is even twice the average. Ask for the size of a typical city, the wealth of a typical person or the number of followers of a typical account, and the question falls apart. Most cities are small, a few are enormous, and the biggest are bigger than any “typical” scale would suggest.

The fingerprint is in the tail. For heights, the chance of exceeding a level dies off faster than exponentially. For city sizes, wealth and many other things, the chance of exceeding \(x\) falls only like a power of \(x\):

$$P(X > x) \approx C\,x^{-\alpha}$$

The number \(\alpha\) is the tail exponent. Doubling \(x\) always cuts the chance by the same factor \(2^{-\alpha}\), whether you go from 10 to 20 or from a million to two million. There is no scale at which the tail starts to die, which is why these distributions are also called scale-free.

Vilfredo Pareto noticed this pattern in income and wealth data in the 1890s. His name is now attached to the 80/20 rule: roughly 80% of a quantity held by 20% of the people (or customers, or files). That split is not a law of nature; it is what a power law with \(\alpha \approx 1.16\) gives, and other exponents give other splits.

02

The Pareto distribution

The simplest power law is the Pareto distribution: values start at a minimum \(x_m\) and above it the tail is exactly a power,

$$\begin{gathered} P(X > x) = \Big(\frac{x}{x_m}\Big)^{-\alpha}, \quad x \ge x_m \\ f(x) = \frac{\alpha\,x_m^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha + 1}} \end{gathered}$$

The exponent controls everything. The smaller \(\alpha\), the heavier the tail and the more the total is dominated by the largest few values. The mean \(\alpha x_m/(\alpha - 1)\) exists only for \(\alpha > 1\), and the variance only for \(\alpha > 2\).

Turn the tail exponent

Pareto: \(P(X > x)\)exponential with the same median

On log–log axes the Pareto tail is a straight line with slope \(-\alpha\), while an exponential tail with the same median plunges off the chart. That is the practical difference: under the exponential a value 100 times the minimum essentially never happens; under a Pareto with \(\alpha = 1.2\) it happens to about 1 value in 250, so any sizable sample contains some.

The share held by the top fraction \(q\) of a Pareto population is \(q^{1 - 1/\alpha}\) (for \(\alpha > 1\)). Setting the top 20% to hold 80% gives \(\alpha = \log_4 5 \approx 1.16\). The Pareto is also a close relative of the exponential: if \(X\) is Pareto then \(\ln(X/x_m)\) is exponential with rate \(\alpha\). A power law is an exponential on a logarithmic scale.

03

Straight lines on log–log plots

Because a power-law tail is a straight line on log–log axes, the classic way to “find” a power law is to plot the data that way and look for a line. The trouble is that many distributions that are not power laws look nearly straight too, especially the lognormal with a large spread. Over two or three decades of data, the gentle downward bend of a lognormal is easy to miss.

Spot the power law

fraction of the 5,000 values above \(x\)straight line fitted to the top 10%

On linear axes both samples are an unreadable L: almost everything is squashed against the left, and a few huge values stretch the axis. On log–log axes the Pareto is a line all the way out. The lognormal is nearly a line too, and a line fitted to its top 10% looks convincing; only its steady downward bend gives it away, and with real, noisy data even that is hard to see.

Clauset, Shalizi and Newman made this point forcefully in 2009. They recommend estimating \(\alpha\) by maximum likelihood rather than by fitting a line, choosing the lower cut-off \(x_{\min}\) with care, testing whether a power law fits at all, and comparing it with alternatives such as the lognormal. When they applied these tests to two dozen data sets that had been described as power laws, a power law could be ruled out for some, and for many others a lognormal fitted about as well. A straight-ish line on a log–log plot is a hint, not a proof.

04

Rank and size: Zipf

Another way to plot the same data is to sort it from largest to smallest and plot each value against its rank. The linguist George Kingsley Zipf did this for word counts in the 1930s and 1940s and found that the frequency of the \(r\)-th most common word falls roughly like \(1/r\): the second most common word appears about half as often as the first, the third about a third as often. The same rough pattern turns up in city sizes within a country.

$$\text{size of the } r\text{-th largest} \;\propto\; r^{-1/\alpha}$$

The rank plot is the survival plot turned on its side, so a tail exponent \(\alpha\) gives a rank slope of \(-1/\alpha\). Zipf’s law, slope \(-1\), is a power law with \(\alpha = 1\): the borderline where the mean just fails to exist.

Size against rank

2,000 simulated sizes, largest firstslope \(-1/\alpha\)

Notice how ragged the top of the plot is. The few largest values carry the most weight and are the least predictable: with \(\alpha = 1\), the largest of 2,000 values can easily be several times the second largest.

05

Where they come from

The rich get richer

In 1925 the statistician George Udny Yule explained why a few biological genera contain many species and most contain one or two, and in 1955 Herbert Simon turned the idea into a simple model. Items arrive one at a time. Each new item starts a new group with a small probability \(p\); otherwise it joins an existing group, chosen with probability proportional to the group’s size. Big groups attract more newcomers because they are big. Simon showed that the group sizes then follow a power law with tail exponent \(\alpha = 1/(1 - p)\).

The rich get richer

fraction of groups with at least \(k\) itemsslope \(-1/(1 - p)\)

Raise \(p\) and newcomers spread out over more groups: the tail steepens and the giants shrink. Lower it and a few early groups run away with nearly everything. In 1999 Barabási and Albert used the same mechanism, which they called preferential attachment, for networks: new web pages tend to link to pages that already have many links, which makes the number of links per page heavy-tailed. Derek de Solla Price had described the same effect for citations of scientific papers in the 1960s and 1970s.

Multiplying, with a floor

A second road runs through multiplication. A quantity that grows by a random factor each year becomes lognormal, as the normal tutorial shows. That alone is not a power law, but small changes make it one. If growth keeps going at a steady rate for a random, exponentially distributed length of time, the final size \(e^{gT}\) is exactly Pareto, with \(\alpha = \lambda/g\). Random growth with a lower barrier, or with units that are born and die, gives power-law tails too. This is one reason city sizes and firm sizes look the way they do.

06

When averages break

The tail exponent decides which averages make sense. With \(\alpha > 2\) a Pareto has a mean and a variance, and the central limit theorem works as usual. With \(1 < \alpha \le 2\) the mean exists but the variance is infinite: sample averages do converge, but slowly and in jumps, and the bell curve never appears. With \(\alpha \le 1\) the mean itself is infinite, and the average of a sample keeps growing, without limit, as the sample grows.

Running averages

run 1run 2run 3true mean

With \(\alpha = 3\) the three runs settle quickly onto the true mean of 1.5. With \(\alpha = 1.2\) the true mean is 6, but each run spends long stretches below it, then leaps up when a giant value arrives; the runs disagree with each other even after 100,000 samples. With \(\alpha = 0.8\) there is no mean to converge to, and the averages climb for ever. This is the extreme value story again: under a heavy tail the largest single value is of the same order as the sum of all the others.

What this means in practice. For heavy-tailed quantities such as insurance losses, wealth or the sizes of disasters, the sample average of past data understates the true mean most of the time, because the giants that drive it are rare. Medians, tail exponents and quantiles are more reliable summaries than means and standard deviations.

Where the formulas come from

The share held by the top

The top fraction \(q\) of the population are the values above \(x_q = x_m q^{-1/\alpha}\), since \(P(X > x_q) = q\). Their share of the total is the part of the mean that comes from above \(x_q\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\int_{x_q}^\infty x f(x)\,dx}{E[X]} &= \frac{x_q^{\,1-\alpha}}{x_m^{\,1-\alpha}} \\ &= q^{\,1 - 1/\alpha}, \qquad \alpha > 1 \end{aligned}$$

For \(\alpha \to 1\) the exponent goes to 0 and the top fraction holds nearly everything, however small \(q\) is.

Estimating the exponent

Because \(\ln(X/x_m)\) is exponential with rate \(\alpha\), the maximum likelihood estimate of \(\alpha\) is one over the average of \(\ln(x_i/x_m)\). This is the Hill estimator, used on the values above a chosen cut-off \(x_{\min}\). Its standard error is about \(\hat\alpha/\sqrt n\). It assumes a power law; it does not test for one, which is why the comparison with alternatives in section 3 matters.

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Straight on log–log axes. \(\ln P(X > x) = -\alpha\ln x + \text{const}\).
  2. Scale-free. Conditioned on exceeding any level, the excess looks the same, rescaled.
  3. The exponent sets the moments. No mean for \(\alpha \le 1\), no variance for \(\alpha \le 2\).
  4. An exponential in disguise. \(\ln(X/x_m) \sim \text{Exp}(\alpha)\), and \(e^{gT}\) with exponential \(T\) is Pareto.
  5. Zipf’s law. Rank plots have slope \(-1/\alpha\); Zipf’s \(1/r\) is \(\alpha = 1\).
  6. Extremes. Maxima of power-law values follow the Fréchet distribution, and excesses over a high threshold are again Pareto-like (extreme values).
08

Where you will meet it

Cities

The sizes of the largest cities in many countries follow Zipf’s rule roughly: the second largest about half the size of the first, and so on. The fit is rough and varies from country to country.

Wealth and income

The top tails of wealth and income distributions are close to Pareto, which is how inequality researchers estimate the share of the richest. The bulk of incomes is not a power law.

Words

In almost any long text the word frequencies follow Zipf’s law roughly: a few words such as “the” and “of” are everywhere, and most words occur once or twice.

Networks

Links between web pages, followers on social networks and citations of papers are heavy-tailed: a few hubs collect a large share of all connections.

Earthquakes

The Gutenberg–Richter law says that each step up of one in magnitude means roughly ten times fewer earthquakes. Since magnitude is logarithmic in energy, quake energies have a power-law tail.

Insurance and finance

Large insurance losses and stock market crashes have heavy, roughly power-law tails, which is why risk models fit tail exponents rather than variances.

When it fails

Many claimed power laws do not survive careful testing, and lognormals or stretched exponentials fit as well or better. Real data also have finite upper limits: no city can be larger than its country.
