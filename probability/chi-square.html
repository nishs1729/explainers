A visual introduction

The Chi-Square Distribution

Take a few random numbers from the bell curve, square each one and add them up. The total follows the chi-square distribution. It measures squared distances and sums of squared errors, and it is the yardstick for how much a sample’s variance can wander.

01

Square and add

Let \(Z_1, \dots, Z_k\) be independent standard normal numbers. The sum of their squares

$$Q = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \dots + Z_k^2$$

follows the chi-square distribution with \(k\) degrees of freedom, written \(Q \sim \chi^2_k\). The name is just the Greek letter χ (“kai”), squared.

One square already tells you the mean and the variance. A standard normal has variance 1, so \(E[Z^2] = 1\). Its fourth moment is 3, so \(\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) = 3 - 1 = 2\). Independent squares add both:

$$E[Q] = k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Q) = 2k$$

Square and add k normals

simulated sums \(Q\)\(\chi^2_k\) densitymean \(k\)

With \(k = 1\) most squares are tiny: a normal number is usually below 1 in size, and squaring shrinks it further. The density shoots up at 0. With \(k = 2\) it falls away from 0 like an exponential. From \(k = 3\) on there is a hump, and it slides right as \(k\) grows, one unit per degree of freedom.

02

Degrees of freedom

The number of squares \(k\) is the only parameter. The density is

$$f(x) = \frac{x^{k/2 - 1}\, e^{-x/2}}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)}, \qquad x > 0$$

with the peak at \(k - 2\) (at 0 when \(k \le 2\)). The skewness is \(\sqrt{8/k}\), so the curve becomes more symmetric as \(k\) grows, and since \(Q\) is a sum of \(k\) independent pieces the central limit theorem makes it approximately \(\mathcal N(k, 2k)\) for large \(k\).

The normal approximation is poor in the tails until \(k\) is large: the chi-square has a long right tail and no left one. A much better approximation, due to Wilson and Hilferty (1931), takes cube roots: \((Q/k)^{1/3}\) is close to normal with mean \(1 - \tfrac{2}{9k}\) and variance \(\tfrac{2}{9k}\). The readout compares both with the exact 95th percentile.

Why “degrees of freedom”? Each square is one independent direction in which the total can vary. Section 6 shows a case where a constraint removes one of them.

03

A gamma in disguise

Compare the density with the gamma distribution’s, \(\lambda^\alpha t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda t}/\Gamma(\alpha)\): they match with shape \(\alpha = k/2\) and rate \(\lambda = \tfrac12\).

$$\chi^2_k = \text{Gamma}\big(\tfrac{k}{2},\ \tfrac12\big)$$

Everything about gammas carries over. One square is \(\text{Gamma}(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\): from \(P(Z^2 \le x) = P(-\sqrt x \le Z \le \sqrt x)\), differentiating gives the density \(x^{-1/2}e^{-x/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\), which is the formula above with \(k = 1\) and \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\).

  • Two degrees of freedom is an exponential. \(\chi^2_2 = \text{Gamma}(1, \tfrac12)\), the exponential with mean 2. That is why the exponential tutorial can say \(2\lambda T \sim \chi^2_2\).
  • Degrees of freedom add. Gammas with the same rate add their shapes, so for independent pieces \(\chi^2_a + \chi^2_b = \chi^2_{a+b}\). This is just “more squares”.
  • Any positive \(k\) works. The formula makes sense for fractional \(k\), but in practice \(k\) is almost always a whole number of squares.

Scaling is the one thing to watch. \(\sigma^2\chi^2_k\), a sum of squares of normals with standard deviation \(\sigma\), is a gamma with rate \(1/(2\sigma^2)\) but not a chi-square. Chi-square means “squares of standard normals”.

04

Distance from the center

Put the \(k\) normal numbers together as the coordinates of a random point \((Z_1, \dots, Z_k)\). Its squared distance from the origin is \(Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_k^2\), by Pythagoras. So chi-square is the squared distance of a random normal point from the center.

In two dimensions this is a dart thrower whose horizontal and vertical errors are independent standard normals. The chance of landing within radius \(r\) of the bullseye is \(P(\chi^2_2 \le r^2) = 1 - e^{-r^2/2}\).

Darts with normal errors

inside the circleoutsidecircle of radius \(r\)

Half of the darts land within \(r = \sqrt{2\ln 2} \approx 1.18\) standard deviations, the starting radius. Navigation engineers call this radius the circular error probable: for a GPS fix or a guided shell with independent normal errors of size \(\sigma\) in each direction, half the shots land within \(1.18\,\sigma\) of the target.

05

The chi distribution

The distance itself, not squared, is \(R = \sqrt{Q}\). It follows the chi distribution with \(k\) degrees of freedom, with density

$$f(r) = \frac{r^{k-1}\,e^{-r^2/2}}{2^{k/2 - 1}\,\Gamma(k/2)}, \qquad r > 0$$

Three cases are famous enough to have their own names:

  • \(k = 1\): the half-normal. The size \(|Z|\) of a single normal error.
  • \(k = 2\): the Rayleigh distribution. The miss distance of a dart or a GPS fix, the size of the wind’s velocity when its two components are normal, and the strength of a radio signal that reaches you along many scattered paths (Rayleigh fading).
  • \(k = 3\): the Maxwell distribution. In a gas each velocity component of a molecule is normal, so its speed is chi with 3 degrees of freedom, scaled by \(\sqrt{k_B T/m}\). That is the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of molecular speeds.

Lengths of random vectors

lengths of simulated vectorschi density\(\sqrt k\)

Push \(k\) up and something strange happens. The lengths pile up just below \(\sqrt k\), and their spread does not grow: it settles at about \(1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.71\) however many dimensions there are. A random normal point in high dimensions almost never lies near the center, where the density is highest. It lies in a thin shell of radius about \(\sqrt k\). This concentration of measure is why distances behave strangely in high-dimensional data: random points are all at nearly the same distance from the center, and from each other.

06

The spread of a sample

The most common place to meet the chi-square is inside a sample variance. Take \(n\) independent draws \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) from \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), with sample mean \(\bar X\) and sample variance

$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \bar X)^2$$

Then the sum of squared deviations, measured in units of \(\sigma^2\), is chi-square, with one degree of freedom fewer than you might expect:

$$\frac{(n - 1)\,s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$$

and it is independent of \(\bar X\), a special property of normal samples. With the true mean \(\mu\) in place of \(\bar X\) it would be \(\chi^2_n\), \(n\) standardized squares. Using \(\bar X\) costs one degree of freedom: the deviations \(X_i - \bar X\) always add to 0, so once \(n - 1\) of them are known the last one is fixed. Only \(n - 1\) directions are free.

Sample variances from a normal

\((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2\), 20,000 samples\(\chi^2_{n-1}\)\(\chi^2_{n}\), one too many

Since \(E[\chi^2_{n-1}] = n - 1\), the average of \(s^2\) is exactly \(\sigma^2\): dividing by \(n - 1\) rather than \(n\) makes \(s^2\) unbiased. Its square root is not. The square root bends large values down more than small ones, so \(E[s] = c_4\,\sigma\) with \(c_4 = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{n-1}}\,\Gamma(\tfrac n2)/\Gamma(\tfrac{n-1}{2})\), which is below 1: about 0.80 for \(n = 2\), 0.94 for \(n = 5\) and 0.97 for \(n = 10\). Quality-control charts divide by \(c_4\) to correct for it.

The same fact gives exact confidence intervals for \(\sigma^2\), and sums of squared differences like this one are behind the chi-square tests that compare observed counts with expected ones (used in the Poisson tutorial). Both belong to statistical inference, which is planned as a later series. Unlike the central limit theorem, the result here needs normal data: for heavy-tailed data the sample variance varies much more than \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) says.

Where the formulas come from

The MGF of one square

Complete the square in the exponent:

$$\begin{aligned} E\big[e^{tZ^2}\big] &= \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(1 - 2t)z^2/2}\,dz \\ &= (1 - 2t)^{-1/2}, \qquad t < \tfrac12 \end{aligned}$$

since the integrand is a normal density with variance \(1/(1 - 2t)\), short of that factor. Independent squares multiply their MGFs, giving \((1 - 2t)^{-k/2}\), the MGF of \(\text{Gamma}(k/2, \tfrac12)\). Its cumulants are \(\kappa_j = k\,2^{j-1}(j-1)!\): mean \(k\), variance \(2k\), and skewness \(\kappa_3/\kappa_2^{3/2} = 8k/(2k)^{3/2} = \sqrt{8/k}\).

Why the sample variance loses one degree of freedom

Rotate the coordinates of the sample \((X_1, \dots, X_n)\) so that one axis points along \((1, 1, \dots, 1)/\sqrt n\). The coordinate along that axis is \(\sqrt n\,\bar X\). The other \(n - 1\) coordinates describe the deviations from the mean, and \(\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2\) is their squared length. A rotation of independent normals with equal variance gives independent normals again, so those \(n - 1\) coordinates are independent \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\) and independent of \(\bar X\). Dividing by \(\sigma^2\) gives \(\chi^2_{n-1}\).

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Squares of standard normals. \(Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_k^2 \sim \chi^2_k\) (section 1).
  2. A gamma. \(\chi^2_k = \text{Gamma}(k/2, \tfrac12)\); with \(k = 2\) it is the exponential with mean 2 (section 3).
  3. Degrees of freedom add. \(\chi^2_a + \chi^2_b = \chi^2_{a+b}\) for independent variables.
  4. Large \(k\): normal. \(\chi^2_k \approx \mathcal N(k, 2k)\), and \((\chi^2_k/k)^{1/3}\) is closer still.
  5. Square root: the chi distribution. Half-normal, Rayleigh and Maxwell for \(k = 1, 2, 3\) (section 5).
  6. Sample variance. \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\) for normal samples, independent of \(\bar X\) (section 6).
08

Where you will meet it

Statistical tests

Pearson’s goodness-of-fit and contingency-table statistics, and likelihood-ratio tests in general, are referred to chi-square distributions. A later page on chi-square tests will cover them.

Uncertainty of a spread

Confidence intervals for a variance, and the limits of control charts for process variability, come straight from \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\).

Navigation and targeting

Position errors in two or three dimensions give Rayleigh and Maxwell miss distances, and the circular error probable is a chi-square quantile.

Physics

Molecular speeds in a gas follow the Maxwell distribution, and the energy in a normal noise signal, a sum of squared amplitudes, is chi-square.

Wireless signals

A radio signal that reaches a phone along many reflected paths has a Rayleigh-distributed strength, and its power is exponential, which is \(\chi^2_2\) up to scale.

High-dimensional data

Squared distances between random points in many dimensions are chi-square, which is why they bunch together and why distance-based methods struggle in high dimensions.

When it fails

All of this rests on normal pieces. Squares of heavy-tailed or skewed numbers, and variances of non-normal samples, spread out much more than the chi-square predicts.
