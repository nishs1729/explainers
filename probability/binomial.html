01

From one trial to many

The Bernoulli distribution describes a single yes/no trial. Its outcome \(X\) is \(1\) (a “success”) with probability \(p\), and \(0\) (a “failure”) with probability \(1-p\). Throughout this page we write \(q = 1 - p\) for the probability of failure.

Most real questions involve many trials, not one:

  • How many of 20 free throws will a player make?
  • How many of 500 website visitors will click “Buy”?
  • How many of 100 light bulbs from a batch are defective?

In each case you repeat a Bernoulli trial \(n\) times and count the successes:

$$Y = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n$$

Each \(X_i\) is 0 or 1, so adding them up counts the 1s. The count \(Y\) can be any whole number from \(0\) to \(n\). This is the payoff of encoding outcomes as 0 and 1 that the Bernoulli tutorial promised.

The count \(Y\) follows a binomial distribution when four conditions hold. A common memory aid is BINS:

  1. Binary outcomes. Each trial ends in one of two ways: success or failure.
  2. Independent trials. The result of one trial does not change the chances of any other.
  3. Number of trials is fixed. \(n\) is decided in advance, not by what happens along the way.
  4. Same probability. Every trial has the same success probability \(p\).

We then write \(Y \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)\). The two numbers \(n\) and \(p\) are its parameters. A single Bernoulli trial is the special case \(n = 1\).

Binomial or not?

Drawing without replacement is the most common way to break the rules. Each draw changes what is left, so \(p\) shifts from trial to trial. The exact count then follows the hypergeometric distribution. When the population is much larger than the sample, such as polling 1,000 people out of millions, the shift is tiny and the binomial is an excellent approximation.

02

Counting the paths

Toss a coin 4 times. Which sequences give exactly two heads? Writing 1 for heads: 1100, 1010, 1001, 0110, 0101, 0011. That is six sequences.

The Bernoulli tutorial showed that the probability of one specific sequence depends only on how many 1s and 0s it contains. A sequence with \(k\) ones and \(n-k\) zeros has probability \(p^k q^{n-k}\), whatever the order. So the only remaining question is: how many sequences have exactly \(k\) ones?

That number is the binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{k}\), read “\(n\) choose \(k\)” and also written \(C(n,k)\). It counts the ways to choose which \(k\) of the \(n\) positions hold the 1s:

$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$$

Here \(n! = n \times (n-1) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1\) is “\(n\) factorial”, with \(0! = 1\). For example, \(\binom{4}{2} = \frac{24}{2 \cdot 2} = 6\), matching the list above.

Every possible sequence, grouped by its number of 1s

Click a group to highlight it in Pascal’s triangle below. ● = 1 (success), ● = 0 (failure).

Pascal’s triangle

Write the counts \(\binom{n}{0}, \binom{n}{1}, \dots, \binom{n}{n}\) in a row for \(n = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) and you get Pascal’s triangle, known centuries before Pascal in India, Persia and China. Each number is the sum of the two numbers above it:

$$\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$$

The reason is simple. A sequence of length \(n\) with \(k\) ones either ends in a 1 (and the first \(n-1\) positions hold \(k-1\) ones) or ends in a 0 (and the first \(n-1\) positions hold all \(k\) ones).

03

The formula: paths × probability per path

Put the two pieces together. There are \(\binom{n}{k}\) sequences with exactly \(k\) ones, and each one has probability \(p^k q^{n-k}\). So

$$P(Y=k) = \binom{n}{k}\,p^{k}\,q^{\,n-k}$$

for \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\). Read it from left to right as number of paths × probability of each path. This is the probability mass function (PMF) of the binomial distribution.

Do these probabilities add up to 1? Yes. The binomial theorem from algebra says that expanding \((p+q)^n\) gives exactly these terms, so

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^kq^{n-k} = (p+q)^n = 1$$

because \(p + q = 1\). That is where the distribution gets its name.

Pick a bar

Tap or drag across the bars to pick \(k\). Horizontal axis: \(k\), the number of successes.

Two things to try:

  • Set \(n = 60\) and look at the tallest bar. Even the most likely count has a probability of only about 0.1. With many trials the probability is spread over many possible counts, so no single count is very likely.
  • Pick \(k = 0\). The probability of no successes at all is \(q^n\): there is only one such path (all zeros). It shrinks quickly as \(n\) grows.
04

How \(n\) and \(p\) set the shape

Every binomial distribution is a row of bars. The two parameters decide where the bars pile up and how wide the pile is.

Shape explorer

P(Y = k)most likely count (mode)mean \(np\)mean ± 1 standard deviationBinomial(n, 1−p)
  • Center. The bars balance at the mean, \(np\). With 20 trials at \(p = 0.2\) you expect about 4 successes.
  • Peak. The most likely count, the mode, is \(\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor\), the whole-number part of \((n+1)p\). When \((n+1)p\) is itself a whole number there is a tie: \((n+1)p - 1\) and \((n+1)p\) are equally likely. Try \(n = 9\), \(p = 0.5\).
  • Symmetry. At \(p = \tfrac12\) the bars are symmetric. For \(p < \tfrac12\) they bunch up on the left with a longer tail to the right (right-skewed), and for \(p > \tfrac12\) it is the mirror image. Counting failures instead of successes turns \(\text{Binomial}(n, p)\) into \(\text{Binomial}(n, 1-p)\). Use the overlay button to see the mirror.
  • More trials. As \(n\) grows the skew fades. Skewness is \((q-p)/\sqrt{npq}\), which shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\). Even with \(p = 0.1\), at \(n = 100\) the shape is nearly symmetric.
Why the mode is \(\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor\)

Compare each bar with the one before it. Most of the factorials cancel:

$$\frac{P(Y=k+1)}{P(Y=k)} = \frac{n-k}{k+1}\cdot\frac{p}{q}$$

The next bar is taller when this ratio is greater than 1, that is when \((n-k)p > (k+1)q\). Expanding and using \(p + q = 1\), this simplifies to \(k + 1 < (n+1)p\). So the bars rise as long as \(k+1\) stays below \((n+1)p\) and fall after that. The peak is the largest whole number not exceeding \((n+1)p\). If \((n+1)p\) is a whole number \(m\), the ratio at \(k + 1 = m\) is exactly 1, and bars \(m-1\) and \(m\) tie.

05

Mean and variance, the easy way

You could compute the mean as \(\sum_k k\,P(Y=k)\) straight from the formula, but there is a much quicker route. \(Y\) is a sum of \(n\) Bernoulli variables, and each one has mean \(p\) and variance \(pq\).

Expected values always add, so

$$E[Y] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[X_i] = np$$

Variances add when the variables are independent, so

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = npq$$

and the standard deviation is \(\sigma_Y = \sqrt{npq}\). For example, in 60 rolls of a die you expect \(60 \times \tfrac16 = 10\) sixes, give or take \(\sqrt{60 \cdot \tfrac16 \cdot \tfrac56} \approx 2.9\).

More trials: a bigger spread in the count, a smaller spread in the fraction

\(\sigma_Y = \sqrt{npq}\) grows with \(n\). With more trials, the count wanders further from \(np\) in absolute terms. But divide by \(n\) to get the fraction of successes, \(\hat p = Y/n\), and its standard deviation shrinks:

$$\sigma_{\hat p} = \frac{\sqrt{npq}}{n} = \sqrt{\frac{pq}{n}}$$

This is the same \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) funnel from the Bernoulli coin laboratory. Multiply \(n\) by 4 and the spread of the count doubles while the spread of the fraction halves.

06

The bean machine

In the 1870s Francis Galton built a board to show this distribution in action. A ball drops through \(n\) rows of pegs. At each peg it bounces right with probability \(p\) or left with probability \(q\). Its final bin is the number of right bounces: a sum of \(n\) Bernoulli trials. So the bins fill up in the shape of \(\text{Binomial}(n, p)\).

Galton board

balls landed in each binexpected: total × P(Y = k)

Nothing pushes balls toward the middle. The middle bins fill up because many more paths lead there. With 10 rows there is exactly one path to bin 0 (left every time) but \(\binom{10}{5} = 252\) paths to bin 5. Each path is one of the sequences from section 2.

Tilt the board with \(p\) and the pile slides and becomes lopsided, just as in the shape explorer.

07

The bell curve appears

With many rows the bean-machine pile looks like a bell. That is no accident. The Central Limit Theorem says that a sum of many independent random variables is approximately normally distributed, and \(Y\) is exactly such a sum. For large \(n\):

$$Y \approx \mathcal{N}(np,\; npq)$$

Here \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) is the normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the famous bell curve. Abraham de Moivre found this special case for coin tossing in 1733, long before the general theorem, and it is known as the de Moivre–Laplace theorem.

The continuity correction

The binomial lives on whole numbers; the normal curve lives on the whole number line. Draw each bar with width 1, centered on its value \(k\). Bar \(k\) then covers the stretch from \(k - \tfrac12\) to \(k + \tfrac12\). To approximate \(P(Y \le k)\) with the area under the curve, you should take the area up to \(k + \tfrac12\), not up to \(k\), or you cut bar \(k\) in half. This half-unit shift is called the continuity correction:

$$P(Y \le k) \approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{k + \frac12 - np}{\sqrt{npq}}\right)$$

where \(\Phi\) is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution: \(\Phi(z)\) is the area under the standard bell curve to the left of \(z\).

Binomial versus normal

bars counted in \(P(Y\le k)\)normal-curve area used as the approximationimpossible counts

When it fails. If \(p\) is close to 0 or 1, the binomial is lopsided and squashed against the wall at 0 (or at \(n\)). The normal curve is symmetric and spills over into impossible negative counts. A common rule of thumb asks for \(np \ge 10\) and \(n(1-p) \ge 10\) before trusting the normal approximation (some textbooks use 5). For small \(p\) and large \(n\) there is a better approximation, which is the next section.

08

Rare events: the Poisson limit

Sometimes \(n\) is huge and \(p\) is tiny, but the expected count \(\lambda = np\) is moderate. Think of the number of typos in a 2,000-word essay, the number of calls to a help line in a minute, or the number of houses on a street hit by lightning this year. Let \(n \to \infty\) while keeping \(np = \lambda\) fixed, and the binomial PMF turns into something simpler:

$$\binom{n}{k}p^kq^{n-k} \;\to\; \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$

The right-hand side is the Poisson distribution with mean \(\lambda\). It needs only one number, \(\lambda\), instead of two, and you don’t need to know \(n\) at all.

Binomial(n, λ/n) versus Poisson(λ)

Binomial(n, λ/n)Poisson(λ)

The readout gives the total variation distance between the two distributions: the largest difference between the probabilities they give to any event. Le Cam’s inequality guarantees it is at most \(np^2 = \lambda p\). So what makes the Poisson approximation good is that \(p\) is small, not merely that \(n\) is large. The variances tell the same story: the binomial’s \(npq = \lambda(1-p)\) approaches the Poisson’s \(\lambda\) as \(p \to 0\).

The gaps between Poisson events have a distribution of their own, the exponential. It is the subject of the next tutorial, The Exponential Distribution. The Poisson count itself, with real data, estimation and what to do when it fails, has a tutorial of its own: The Poisson Distribution.

Why the limit holds

Substitute \(p = \lambda/n\) and look at the pieces one at a time. First,

$$\binom{n}{k}p^k = \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\cdot r_n$$

where

$$r_n = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}$$

is a product of \(k\) factors \(\frac{n}{n}, \frac{n-1}{n}, \dots, \frac{n-k+1}{n}\), each of which tends to 1 when \(k\) is fixed and \(n\) grows. So \(r_n \to 1\). Second,

$$q^{n-k} = \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k} \to e^{-\lambda}$$

using the classic limit \((1 - \lambda/n)^n \to e^{-\lambda}\); the extra \(-k\) in the exponent does not matter in the limit. Multiply the two pieces to get \(\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}/k!\).

09

Going backwards: what is \(p\)?

So far we knew \(p\) and predicted the count. In practice it is usually the other way around: you see \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials and want to learn \(p\). As in the Bernoulli tutorial, the best single guess is the observed fraction \(\hat p = k/n\). It is unbiased, and from section 5 its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{pq/n}\).

How sure can you be? Confidence intervals

A 95% confidence interval is a range computed from the data by a recipe that captures the true \(p\) in 95% of experiments. The textbook recipe, the Wald interval, plugs \(\hat p\) into the standard deviation formula:

$$\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}$$

The 1.96 comes from the normal approximation: 95% of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 standard deviations of its mean.

The trouble starts at the edges. When \(k = 0\) or \(k = n\), \(\hat p(1-\hat p) = 0\) and the interval shrinks to a single point. See 0 defects in 5 items and the Wald interval says the defect rate is exactly 0, with no uncertainty at all. That is clearly wrong: a 20% defect rate produces 0 defects in 5 items about a third of the time, since \(0.8^5 \approx 0.33\).

The Wilson interval fixes this by asking a better question: which values of \(p\) would make the observed \(\hat p\) unsurprising? Solving that for \(p\) gives an interval centered at

$$\tilde p = \frac{\hat p + z^2/(2n)}{1 + z^2/n}$$

with half-width

$$h = \frac{z}{1 + z^2/n}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}$$

where \(z = 1.96\). The extra \(z^2\) terms keep the interval from collapsing, and they matter less and less as \(n\) grows.

Two intervals for the same data

How often does each recipe actually capture the true \(p\)? For this \(n\), across every possible true \(p\):
WaldWilsontarget 95%

The coverage chart is computed exactly: for each true \(p\), it adds up the probabilities of every \(k\) whose interval contains \(p\). The Wald curve sags far below 95% near the edges and is jagged everywhere. Wilson stays close to the target. Statisticians Brown, Cai and DasGupta made this point forcefully in 2001, and Wilson (or a similar method) is now the standard recommendation.

A handy shortcut: if you see 0 successes in \(n\) trials, a rough 95% upper bound for \(p\) is \(3/n\), the “rule of three”.

Is this coin fair? A hypothesis test

Flip a coin 100 times and get 60 heads. Is the coin biased, or was that luck? Start from the boring explanation, the null hypothesis \(p = p_0\), here \(p_0 = 0.5\). Then ask: if that were true, how often would a count land at least as far from the expected \(np_0\) as ours did? That probability is the p-value.

The p-value, shaded

at least as far from \(np_0\) as the observed countcloser to \(np_0\)

With 60 heads in 100 flips the p-value is about 0.057: a fair coin does something this lopsided roughly one time in 18. That is suggestive but not conclusive. The same 60% rate over 200 flips (120 heads) gives about 0.006, and over 1,000 flips about \(3 \times 10^{-10}\). The same fraction becomes more convincing with more data, because the spread of \(\hat p\) shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\).

A p-value is not the probability that the coin is fair. It is the probability of data at least this extreme if the coin were fair. A small p-value says the data would be surprising under \(p_0\). Press “Simulate a coin with p = p₀” a few times: even for a perfectly fair coin, a few runs in every hundred give a p-value below 0.05. (With whole-number counts the rate is a bit under the nominal one in 20.)

Where the formulas come from

Mean and variance, through indicators

Write \(Y = X_1 + \cdots + X_n\), where \(X_i\) is 1 if trial \(i\) succeeds. Each \(X_i\) is an indicator with \(E[X_i] = p\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = pq\). Expectation is linear, so \(E[Y] = np\) with no conditions at all. For the variance, the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus twice the sum of all pairwise covariances. Independent trials have zero covariance, which leaves

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = n \cdot pq$$

The same indicator trick works far beyond the binomial: whenever you need the expected value of a count, write the count as a sum of indicators.

Median, mode and mean are always close

The mode \(m = \lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor\) satisfies \((n+1)p - 1 < m \le (n+1)p\), which rearranges to \(np - q < m \le np + p\). So the mode is always within 1 of the mean \(np\). The median is always \(\lfloor np \rfloor\) or \(\lceil np \rceil\); when \(np\) is a whole number, mean, median and mode all equal \(np\). One special case: when \(F(m) = \tfrac12\) exactly (for example \(n\) odd and \(p = \tfrac12\)), every number between \(m\) and \(m+1\) is a median.

Skewness and kurtosis, via cumulants

Cumulants \(\kappa_r\) are a sequence of numbers describing a distribution: \(\kappa_1\) is the mean, \(\kappa_2\) the variance, and \(\kappa_3\), \(\kappa_4\) are the third and fourth central-moment-like quantities. Their key property is that cumulants of independent variables add. A single Bernoulli has \(\kappa_2 = pq\), \(\kappa_3 = pq(q-p)\) and \(\kappa_4 = pq(1-6pq)\), so the binomial has \(n\) times each. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\gamma_1 &= \frac{\kappa_3}{\kappa_2^{3/2}} = \frac{npq(q-p)}{(npq)^{3/2}} \\ &= \frac{q-p}{\sqrt{npq}}\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\gamma_2 &= \frac{\kappa_4}{\kappa_2^{2}} = \frac{npq(1-6pq)}{(npq)^2} \\ &= \frac{1-6pq}{npq}\end{aligned}$$

Both shrink as \(n\) grows (like \(1/\sqrt n\) and \(1/n\)). The normal distribution has skewness 0 and excess kurtosis 0, so this is another view of the bell curve taking over.

MGF and PGF

The moment generating function of a sum of independent variables is the product of their MGFs. Each Bernoulli has \(M_X(t) = q + pe^t\), so

$$M_Y(t) = \big(M_X(t)\big)^n = (q + pe^t)^n$$

The probability generating function \(G_Y(z) = E[z^Y] = (q + pz)^n\) works the same way. Expanding it with the binomial theorem gives \(\sum_k \binom{n}{k}p^kq^{n-k}z^k\): the coefficient of \(z^k\) is exactly \(P(Y = k)\). For example \(G_Y(0) = q^n = P(Y=0)\). Multiplying PGFs also proves that \(\text{Binomial}(n_1, p) + \text{Binomial}(n_2, p) = \text{Binomial}(n_1+n_2, p)\) for independent counts.

The CDF and the incomplete beta function

There is no simpler closed form for \(F(k) = P(Y \le k)\) than the sum, but it equals a regularized incomplete beta function:

$$P(Y \le k) = I_{q}(n-k,\; k+1)$$

One way to see it: generate each trial by drawing a uniform random number \(U_i\) between 0 and 1 and calling it a success if \(U_i \le p\). Then \(Y \le k\) means that fewer than \(k+1\) of the \(U_i\) fall below \(p\), i.e. the \((k+1)\)-th smallest \(U_i\) is above \(p\). That order statistic has a Beta distribution, which gives the formula. In practice this is how software computes binomial tails for large \(n\), and it is the basis of the exact Clopper–Pearson confidence interval.

Fisher information and the best estimate

Each trial carries Fisher information \(1/(pq)\) about \(p\) (see the Bernoulli tutorial), and information from independent trials adds, so \(I(p) = n/(pq)\). The estimate \(\hat p = k/n\) has variance \(pq/n = 1/I(p)\), which is the Cramér–Rao lower bound. No unbiased estimator can do better. The likelihood depends on the data only through \(k\), which is why the count alone is a sufficient statistic: once you know how many successes there were, their order tells you nothing more about \(p\).

Entropy

The exact entropy \(H = -\sum_k P(Y=k)\log_2 P(Y=k)\) has no closed form. For large \(n\) the binomial is close to a normal distribution with variance \(npq\), whose entropy is \(\tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\,\sigma^2)\). The card shows both the exact value and this approximation. Note that it grows only like \(\tfrac12\log_2 n\): doubling the number of trials adds just half a bit of uncertainty about the count.

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Bernoulli is the case \(n = 1\). \(\text{Binomial}(1, p) = \text{Bernoulli}(p)\), and every binomial is a sum of \(n\) independent Bernoullis.
  2. Counts with the same \(p\) add. If \(Y_1 \sim \text{Bin}(n_1, p)\) and \(Y_2 \sim \text{Bin}(n_2, p)\) are independent, then \(Y_1 + Y_2 \sim \text{Bin}(n_1 + n_2, p)\). With different \(p\)s the sum is not binomial; it is called a Poisson binomial distribution.
  3. Failures are binomial too. \(n - Y \sim \text{Bin}(n, q)\). This is the mirror image from section 4.
  4. Thinning keeps it binomial. If each success is independently kept with probability \(r\), the kept count is \(\text{Bin}(n, pr)\).
  5. The pieces of a Poisson total are binomial. If \(A \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)\) and \(B \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)\) are independent, then given \(A + B = m\), \(A \sim \text{Bin}\big(m, \lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\big)\).
  6. Large \(n\): normal. \(Y \approx \mathcal N(np, npq)\) when \(np\) and \(nq\) are both reasonably large (section 7).
  7. Small \(p\): Poisson. \(\text{Bin}(n, \lambda/n) \to \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) (section 8).
  8. Without replacement: hypergeometric. Sampling from a finite population without replacement gives the hypergeometric distribution, which approaches the binomial when the population is large.
  9. Fix the successes instead: negative binomial. Count trials until the \(r\)-th success and you get the negative binomial distribution (the geometric for \(r = 1\)). The two are linked: at least \(r\) successes in \(n\) trials means the \(r\)-th success came by trial \(n\).
  10. Conjugate prior: Beta. If your uncertainty about \(p\) is a \(\text{Beta}(a, b)\) distribution, then after \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials it becomes \(\text{Beta}(a+k,\; b+n-k)\).
  11. A recurrence for computing it. \(P(Y=k+1) = P(Y=k)\cdot\frac{n-k}{k+1}\cdot\frac{p}{q}\). Starting from \(P(Y=0) = q^n\), you can get every bar without computing a single factorial.

11

Where you will meet it

A/B tests

Each visitor clicks or doesn’t, so the clicks on each version of a page are binomial. Deciding whether a new design is really better means comparing two binomial proportions.

Polls

Ask \(n\) random people a yes/no question. The margin of error is about \(1.96\sqrt{0.25/n} \approx 1/\sqrt n\): roughly ±3 points for 1,000 people. Halving it takes four times as many people.

Quality control

Inspect 50 items from a large batch and count defects. Acceptance sampling rejects the batch when the count exceeds a threshold chosen from the binomial tail.

Genetics

When both parents carry one copy of a recessive gene, each child has the trait with probability ¼. Among 4 children, the number with the trait is \(\text{Bin}(4, \tfrac14)\). In Mendel’s pea crosses, the number of plants showing the dominant trait was \(\text{Bin}(n, \tfrac34)\), which is why his counts hovered around 3 : 1.

Reliability

A system of 5 independent parts keeps working if at least 3 of them do. If each part works with probability \(r\), the system works with probability \(P(Y \ge 3)\) for \(Y \sim \text{Bin}(5, r)\).

Machine learning

A classifier’s number of correct answers on \(n\) test examples is binomial. An accuracy of 0.92 on 200 examples carries an uncertainty of about ±0.04, which is often larger than the gap between two models being compared.
