A visual introduction

Is That Pattern Real?

Chance is a brilliant forger. It draws trends, clusters and streaks that look exactly like discoveries. Before you can trust any pattern in data, you need to know what chance alone can draw.

01

The line-up

The plots at the top come from a notebook Francis Galton filled in the 1880s: the heights of 205 couples and their 934 grown-up children. One plot shows 30 real sons against their parents’ height. The other 19 use the same 30 sons, shuffled among the families at random. Shuffling keeps every height but breaks any link between parent and son, so whatever pattern a shuffled plot shows, chance drew it.

That is a line-up, the same idea as a police line-up: hide the suspect among innocent look-alikes and see if a witness can pick it out. If you could find the real plot, the link between parents’ and sons’ heights is strong enough to stand out from chance. If you couldn’t, then 30 families aren’t enough to show it, however convincing the real plot looks on its own.

Now try it with a pattern whose strength you control. In each round one plot has a real effect built in and the other 19 are shuffles of it. Turn the signal down and see where your eye gives up.

Three things usually happen. At strong signals the real plot jumps out and you wonder why anyone needs statistics. At faint signals you can’t beat guessing, even though one plot really does contain an effect. And in between, you often feel sure about a shuffled plot. Chance doesn’t make random-looking plots; it makes plots with slopes, gaps and trends, just not consistent ones.

Try the None setting, where the “real” plot has no effect at all. Some plot still looks like the obvious winner, every time.

Why this matters. Most of the time you see only one plot: the real one, with no line-up around it. It will always show some pattern. The line-up asks the fair question: would this pattern stand out among the patterns chance draws? Andreas Buja, Hadley Wickham and colleagues proposed line-ups as a working tool for statisticians (2009, 2010), and the same logic sits under every statistical test in this series.

Noise has a size

To spot a real pattern you need a feel for how big chance patterns get. Start with the simplest random thing there is: a fair coin, flipped 20 times. Before flipping, make a guess.

A fair coin gives exactly 10 heads in 20 less than 1 time in 5. About 96% of runs land anywhere from 6 to 14, so “14 heads out of 20” sounds like a lopsided coin but is well within what chance does. Streaks are bigger than most people expect too: about 3 runs in 4 contain a streak of 4 or more, and almost half contain one of 5 or more.

People who invent “random” sequences know streaks exist but don’t believe they are this long, so they switch sides too often. Try it.

A real coin switches sides on about half of its flips: about 9 or 10 times in 20 flips, and between 5 and 14 times in 95% of runs. Invented sequences often switch 13 or more times with no streak longer than 3, which a real coin does in only about 1 run in 15. Teachers use this as a party trick: half the class flips a real coin, half invents results, and the teacher sorts the papers by looking for long streaks.

The lesson for patterns. Our eyes expect randomness to look evenly mixed. Real randomness is lumpier: runs, clusters and gaps are normal. So the gut test “that can’t be chance” is badly calibrated. To judge a pattern you have to compare it with what chance actually produces, by simulating chance, not by imagining it.

03

Meet the cast

Three datasets will come back again and again in this series. Each one has a pattern, and each time the first question is the same: could chance alone have done this? Every widget below has a button that builds chance worlds, so you can see the answer for yourself before any formulas arrive.

The button test

A website tests its gray “Sign up” button against a new orange one. Is orange really better?

gray (A): 50 of 1,000 visitors clicked = 5.0% · orange (B): 64 of 1,000 = 6.4%

gap in a world where the buttons are identicalgap at least as big as the real onereal gap: +1.4 points

Orange beat gray by 1.4 percentage points, which sounds like a win. But give 2,000 visitors two identical buttons (both clicked 5.7% of the time, the overall rate) and one button comes out at least 1.4 points ahead about 1 time in 5. A gap this size is ordinary for chance. That doesn’t prove orange is no better; it means this test can’t tell. (The button test is made up but typical; real A/B tests face exactly this problem.)

The sleep trial

Ten patients each tried two sleeping drugs. Does drug 2 give more extra sleep than drug 1?

Extra hours of sleep, per patient

Average gain, drug 2 minus drug 1

These are real numbers, from a 1905 study by Arthur Cushny and Arthur Peebles, and they became famous three years later when William Gosset, writing as “Student”, used them to show off his new method for small samples. Every patient slept at least as well on drug 2 as on drug 1, and on average 1.58 hours better.

If the two drugs were really the same, the labels “drug 1” and “drug 2” would be arbitrary for each patient, and swapping them would change nothing real. With 10 patients there are exactly 210 = 1,024 ways to swap. Only 4 of them give an average gain as large as 1.58 hours in either direction. Chance alone almost never does this.

Parents and children

Tall parents tend to have tall children. How strong is the link?

Correlation in each shuffle

correlation after shuffling sons among familiesreal correlation: 0.48

This is Galton’s full set of sons: 481 of them, each plotted against the “mid-parent” height Galton used, the father’s height averaged with 1.08 times the mother’s (his way of putting women and men on one scale). The cloud tilts upward. Shuffle the sons among the families and the tilt vanishes. Shuffled correlations pile up around 0 and rarely pass 0.15, far short of the real 0.48; in 1,000 shuffles nothing comes close. With 481 families, the link is far beyond what chance draws. The interesting question here is not “is it real?” but “how strong, and why do children of very tall parents tend to be less tall than their parents?” That puzzle gave regression its name, and it gets its own page.

04

The one question

Look back at what you just did, three times over. It was always the same three steps:

  1. Boil the pattern down to a number.The gap between click rates, the average extra sleep, the tilt of the cloud.
  2. Build worlds where nothing is going on.Identical buttons, interchangeable drug labels, sons shuffled among families. These worlds share everything with the real data except the effect.
  3. See where the real number lands.Among the chance numbers it is ordinary (the button test), or far out in the tail (the sleep trial and the heights).

That is the core of statistical inference, and the rest of this series builds on it. The famous tests with their tables and formulas (t-tests, chi-square tests, analysis of variance) are fast shortcuts for step 2, invented when building thousands of chance worlds by hand was impossible. A computer can do it directly, so this series will usually simulate first and bring the shortcut in afterwards.

Ruling out chance is not the end. If the sleep data are not chance, something real is going on, but what? Maybe drug 2 works better. Maybe patients always got drug 2 second, after they had adjusted to the ward. Chance is only the first suspect. Later pages deal with the others: biased samples, hidden third variables, and the many ways an analyst can fool themselves.

The series has three parts. Learning from data asks how much an estimate wobbles, how to test and how to measure uncertainty, all by simulation first. Bayesian inference turns the question round, from “how surprising is this data?” to “what should I now believe?”. Relationships and regression asks how one quantity moves with another, and when that movement is real.

05

Where you will meet it

Cancer clusters

Draw a circle around the town with the most cases and it looks alarming. With thousands of towns, some will have several times the average by chance alone. The fair question is whether there are more such towns than chance makes.

The hot hand

Basketball fans see shooters “on fire”. A 1985 study found streaks no longer than chance makes; a 2018 reanalysis found a subtle flaw in that method and some evidence for a modest hot hand. Even experts needed careful comparisons with chance to settle it.

Star fund managers

Give 1,000 managers coins instead of skill, and about 31 will beat the market five years running. A winning streak alone can’t tell skill from luck.

A/B tests

Websites compare versions of a page all the time. Most small differences between versions are chance, which is why the button test needs far more than 2,000 visitors.

Stock charts

Random walks draw “head and shoulders”, “support levels” and trends that look tradeable. Shown a real chart among simulated ones, most people can’t pick the real one out.

Checking models

Statisticians use line-ups to check their own work: hide the real residual plot among plots simulated from the model. If it stands out, the model is missing something.

Real or chance?

Every pattern in this page came with a question: how much does a number computed from data move around by chance? That wobble has a shape and a size, and it is the subject of the next page, Sampling Distributions.
