A visual introduction

Who’s Missing?

Data can only show you what made it into the data. When something filters out part of the population on its way to you, the sample misleads, and collecting more of it doesn’t help. A bigger biased sample is just more confidently wrong.

01

The planes that didn’t come back

The holes you can count are on the planes that came back. That makes them a sample of all the hits, but not a random one: every hole on a returning plane is a hit that the plane survived. A part of the plane can look clean for only two reasons. Either it is rarely hit, or a hit there brings the plane down, so the planes hit there aren’t around to be inspected.

In this toy squadron the bullets land evenly all over the plane, which rules out the first reason. How deadly a hit is in each part is set in advance and kept hidden. To compare parts of different sizes fairly, count holes per square foot:

Holes per 100 square feet, by part of the plane

“Hits home” is the share of hits in that part that were on a plane that made it back.

planes that came backevery plane sent, including the lost ones

On the returning planes, the engines and cockpit carry only a fraction of the holes per square foot that the wings carry. Add the lost planes and the shortfall fills in: every part of the plane was hit about equally often, and the missing holes were on the missing planes. So the parts with the fewest holes on the survivors are the parts where a hit is deadliest, and that is where the armor belongs. (Even the wings don’t bring all their hits home, because a plane with a harmless wing hit can still be brought down by a second hit elsewhere.)

The trap has a name: survivorship bias. It happens when you study only what got through a filter, and whether something got through depends on the very thing you are measuring.

What really happened. The story is told about Abraham Wald, a mathematician in the Statistical Research Group at Columbia University during the Second World War. The popular version, with an officer pointing at a diagram covered in red dots, is a later simplification, and the famous diagram is a modern illustration. Wald’s real contribution was a series of eight technical memos showing how to estimate the chance that a hit on each part would down a plane, using only the damage on the planes that returned. Mangel and Samaniego (1984) explain his method. The heart of it is the idea above: if hits would have landed evenly, the shortfall of holes on a part measures how deadly a hit there is.

The question to ask of any dataset is the same one: what had to happen for this observation to reach me?

02

Bigger doesn’t help

On the previous page a bigger sample always helped. The wobble of an average shrinks like σ/√n, so with enough data the estimate homes in on the truth. That promise came with a condition: the sample has to be random. A sample that passes through a filter also homes in as it grows, but on the wrong value.

The most famous demonstration is the US presidential election of 1936. The Literary Digest, a popular magazine that had called the winner of every election since 1916, mailed mock ballots to 10 million people. The addresses came mainly from telephone directories, car registrations and its own subscriber lists. Nearly 2.4 million ballots came back, and the final count was 1,293,669 for the Republican, Alf Landon, against 972,897 for President Franklin Roosevelt: 57% for Landon.

In November Roosevelt won 60.8% of the vote and every state except Maine and Vermont. Of the votes for the two men, he took 62.5%; the Digest’s ballots gave him 42.9%. George Gallup, with about 50,000 interviews, correctly called Roosevelt’s win.

Which would you trust more to forecast the election?

random sampleDigest-style samplethe Digest’s real resultRoosevelt’s true share

Both kinds of sample settle down as they grow, and their error bars shrink in the same way. The random sample settles on the truth. The biased one settles on 43%, with an error bar so narrow it hides behind the dot. Its margin of error is honest about chance and blind to bias, because the formula assumes the sample is random.

Error = bias + wobble. More data shrinks only the wobble. The bias, how far the sample’s target sits from the truth, stays exactly where it is, however large the sample. Slide the bias to 1 and the two samples behave identically.

Why the over-count? In the middle of the Great Depression, people with telephones and cars were better off than average and leaned Republican. On top of that, the people who bothered to mail a ballot back leaned toward Landon. Studies of the poll disagree about how to split the blame between the mailing list and the response (Squire 1988; Lusinchi 2012; Lohr and Brick 2017), but agree that both played a part. Comparing the Digest’s split with the real one, a Landon voter was about 2.2 times as likely as a Roosevelt voter to end up in the count. That is the default on the slider.

03

Kinds of missing

Bombers and ballots are two cases of one problem: whether a value reaches you depends on the value itself. It turns up anywhere people or things can drop out before they are counted. Pick an example below and change how strongly the value decides who is seen.

Who gets seen

seenmissingaverage of everyoneaverage of those seen
  • Who answers is nonresponse bias. People who answer surveys differ from people who don’t: they tend to be more interested in the topic, more trusting and more helpful. Surveys overstate how much people volunteer, for instance, because the people who give up their time for a survey also tend to give it to good causes (Abraham, Helms and Presser 2009).
  • Who finishes is attrition, or dropout. In a study that follows people over time, the ones it isn’t working for are the most likely to stop showing up, so the final measurements describe the successes.
  • Which funds survive is survivorship bias again. Funds that do badly are closed or merged into others, and often vanish from the databases. Averaging the funds that still exist makes the whole industry look better than it was.

A fourth kind needs no filter at all, just volunteers: self-selection. Online polls, product reviews and phone-in shows hear from the people who chose to speak, and those people usually feel strongly. However many of them there are, they speak only for themselves.

04

Random sampling is the cure

There is one reliable way to stop the values from deciding who gets into the sample: let chance decide. In a random sample everyone has the same chance of being picked, whatever their vote, height or bank balance. The sample can still be unlucky, but only by chance, and chance is exactly what the previous page learned to measure: its size is the standard error, and it shrinks as the sample grows. Random sampling turns an error you can’t measure (bias) into one you can (wobble).

That is why 1,000 people picked at random beat 2.3 million who came through a filter.

The catch is that you can choose people at random, but you can’t make them answer. Pew Research Center’s telephone surveys had a response rate of 36% in 1997 and 6% in 2018. Every modern poll is a random sample passed through a second, non-random filter, and pollsters fight back as best they can:

The same cure works for comparisons. When a coin decides which visitors see the orange button, or which patients get the new drug, nothing about the people can sneak into the comparison. That is random assignment, and the 1954 polio vaccine trial at the end of this part of the series shows what it buys.

05

Where you will meet it

Medicine

In the Coronary Drug Project (1980), patients who took most of their placebo pills had a five-year death rate of 15.1%, against 28.3% for those who didn’t. The sugar pills did nothing; people who stick with treatment differ from those who don’t.

Opinion polls

A poll’s “± 3 points” covers chance only. Who refused to answer is a separate error, handled by weighting, and it doesn’t shrink with a bigger sample.

Investing

Fund performance studies use “survivorship-bias-free” databases that keep every fund that ever existed, because the funds still around are the ones that did well.

Lending and AI

A bank only learns whether a loan was repaid if it approved the loan. A model trained on past loans never sees the applicants who were turned down.

Astronomy

Far away, only bright stars and galaxies are visible, so a catalog makes distant objects look brighter on average than nearby ones. Astronomers call it Malmquist bias.

Everyday life

“They don’t build them like they used to.” The old buildings, cars and fridges still around are the ones that lasted; the flimsy ones were thrown away long ago.
