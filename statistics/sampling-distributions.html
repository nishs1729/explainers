Sampling Distributions

Any number you compute from a sample would have come out differently with a different sample. It is a random draw with a distribution of its own, and once you can see that distribution you know how far to trust the number.

01

Many samples, one distribution

On the first page the question was always: could chance alone have done this? To answer it you need to know how much a number computed from data moves around by chance. The polls above show it directly. Every pollster did the job properly, yet their answers spread over several points, just because each one happened to ask different people.

The same is true of any number computed from a sample: an average, a median, a percentage, the largest value. Draw another sample and you get another number. Draw thousands of samples and the numbers pile up into a distribution, called the sampling distribution of that statistic. It is the distribution of the answers you could have got.

The machine below draws samples from a population you choose. The top chart is the population, the middle row is the latest sample, and the bottom chart collects one number from every sample. To start, the population is Galton’s 481 sons from the first page, and each sample picks 25 of them at random. Each time you choose a new population or statistic, the machine asks for a fresh guess first.

Most people’s first band is far too wide. They expect the averages to spread as much as the heights themselves do, from about 64 to 74 inches. In fact 95% of the averages of 25 sons land within about 1 inch of the population average. One son can easily be 5 inches above average; for 25 sons to average 5 inches above, nearly all of them would have to be tall at once, and that almost never happens. Highs and lows cancel.

Now switch to Waiting times, a population piled up near zero with a long tail to the right (an exponential distribution). At sample size 1 the bottom chart just copies that skewed shape. By 25 the averages make a fairly symmetric bell. That is the central limit theorem from the Normal page, seen from the statistician’s side: whatever the population looks like, averages of reasonably large samples have a roughly normal sampling distribution. The Geyser population, with two humps, and one you draw yourself make the same point.

Three distributions, not one. The population is everything you could sample. A sample is the handful you actually got. The sampling distribution is how a number computed from samples would vary over many repeats. In real life you see one sample and one number, never the other two charts. Statistics is the craft of working out the bottom chart without being able to draw it.

02

How much it wobbles

The spread of a sampling distribution is so useful that it has its own name, the standard error. It is simply the standard deviation of the numbers in the bottom chart: how far one sample’s answer typically lands from the average of all the answers. For the mean, that average is the truth itself, so the standard error is also the typical distance from the truth. That isn’t true of every statistic, as section 4 shows.

For averages there is a formula. If the population has standard deviation σ, the average of n independent values has standard error

standard error of the mean = σ / √n

The square-root law on the Normal page explains why: variances add, so the sum of n values has a spread of σ√n, and dividing by n leaves σ/√n. Check it against brute force:

Wobble against sample size

measured: SD of 3,000 sample meansformula: σ/√n

The dots land on the curve for every population, skewed or not. Read it at sample sizes 1, 4, 16 and 64: each time the sample grows four times, the wobble halves. Precision gets expensive. To halve the uncertainty you need four times the data; to cut it to a tenth you need a hundred times.

Back to the pollsters

A poll is an average too: score each “yes” as 1 and each “no” as 0, and the share who said yes is the average of the scores. A yes/no population with share p has σ = √(p(1−p)), which is 0.5 for a near-even split. So a poll of 1,000 has a standard error of 0.5/√1000 ≈ 0.016, or 1.6 percentage points. About 95% of honest polls land within two standard errors, roughly 3 points, of the truth. That is the “margin of error” newspapers print, and it is why 52% and 49% can both come from good polls.

Notice what is missing from the formula: the size of the population. A random sample of 1,000 is about as precise for a city of 100,000 as for a country of 300 million. What matters is how many people you ask and whether they were picked at random, not what fraction of the population they are. (When the sample is a large share of the population, say half of it, the standard error does shrink further. Real polls are nowhere near that.)

There is a catch. The formula needs σ, the spread of the whole population, which you usually don’t know. In practice you plug in the sample’s own standard deviation instead. For large samples that works fine. For small ones it understates the uncertainty a little, and fixing that is the job of Student’s t, later in the series.

03

Spread of data, spread of an estimate

Two different spreads are now in play, and mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes in reading statistics. The standard deviation describes how much individuals vary. The standard error describes how much an estimate varies. More data shrinks one of them but not the other.

Slide the sample size up. On the left, the sample fills in the population’s shape more and more faithfully, but it doesn’t get any narrower: sons vary by about 2.6 inches whether you measure 10 of them or 250. On the right, the averages squeeze in toward the true mean. A bigger sample tells you the average more precisely; it does not make people more alike.

Error bars. Charts in papers and reports often show a bar around each average without saying which spread it is. An SD bar shows how much individuals vary and stays wide however much data there is. An SE bar shows how precisely the average is known and shrinks as data grows. With 100 people per group an SD bar is 10 times longer than an SE bar, so the same data can look either convincing or hopeless. Always check which one you are looking at.

04

Not just the mean

Everything so far has been about averages, because averages behave so well: centered on the truth, with a simple formula for the spread and a bell shape for large samples. Other statistics have sampling distributions too, and they are not always so tidy. Here are four of them, from the same 2,000 samples:

  • The median wobbles more than the mean for smooth bell-shaped data, about 25% more in large samples. Galton’s heights are less smooth than they look: most were written down to the whole inch, so the median keeps landing on the same few values and jumps between them. In the widget that makes it wobble anywhere from about 15% more than the mean (samples of 3) to about 45% more (samples of 50). For skewed data like the waiting times, small-sample medians also land a little off-center, above the population median on average. The median earns its keep with messy data, where a few wild values would drag the mean around.
  • The largest value is always at or below the population’s largest value, so on average it falls short. Its distribution is lopsided, with a long tail toward big values; the Extreme Values page is about exactly this shape. For waiting times the population has no largest value at all, so the sample maximum keeps growing with the sample size.
  • The standard deviation is skewed to the right for small samples and on average comes out a little below σ, even with the usual n − 1 divisor. Its square, the sample variance, has the shape of a stretched chi-square, at least for normal data.

So a sampling distribution can be lopsided, and it can be centered somewhere other than the truth. A statistic whose sampling distribution is off-center is called biased. Whether that matters, and how to choose between a steady statistic and an unbiased one, is the subject of What makes a good guess. And for statistics with no handy formula, like the median, the bootstrap will estimate the sampling distribution from a single sample. Both pages are coming up in this series.

05

Where you will meet it

Opinion polls

The “± 3 points” in a poll report is about two standard errors of a sample of roughly 1,000. It covers chance only: it says nothing about who refused to answer or how the question was worded.

A/B tests

The button test’s 5.0% and 6.4% each wobble with a standard error of about 0.7 points. Their gap wobbles more, about √2 × 0.7 ≈ 1.0 point, because both rates move. So the 1.4-point gap is only about 1.35 standard errors, well inside the ±2 that chance alone produces 95% of the time.

Measuring things

Labs repeat a measurement and report the average, because the average of 4 readings has half the random error of one. That is σ/√n at work.

Quality control

Factories plot the average of small batches over time with limits at about 3 standard errors from the target. A point outside the limits is unlikely to be chance.

Clinical trials

Trial sizes are planned from the standard error: how many patients are needed before the wobble in the average effect is smaller than the effect worth detecting.

Machine learning

A model’s accuracy on a test set is a sample statistic. On 500 test cases, an accuracy of 80% has a standard error of about 1.8 points, so a “1-point improvement” may be noise.

Check yourself

All of this assumed that each sample was picked at random. Real samples often aren’t: the people who answer polls differ from those who don’t, and the planes that come back differ from those that don’t. A biased sample stays wrong however large it gets. That is the next page, Who’s Missing?
