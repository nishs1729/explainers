A visual introduction

Errors and Decisions

Every decision made from noisy data can go wrong in two ways: raising a false alarm, or missing something real. You can trade one mistake for the other, but no rule avoids both.

You’re the quality inspector. 50 batches of coffee, some of them faulty. Pass or reject each one.

A filling machine in good order puts 100 g of coffee in a bag on average; one that has drifted puts in only 97 g. Single bags vary by a few grams either way, so from each batch you weigh 4 bags. About 2 batches in 5 have drifted.

An inspector who sees only the bags can’t sort every batch correctly: even the best single rule, chosen after the fact with every answer known, gets some wrong. The mistakes aren’t carelessness. They come with the noise, and they will come with any decision made from a sample.

02

Two kinds of mistake

The red dots come in two kinds, and they are different mistakes with different costs.

Every decision, sorted

A false alarm is rejecting a good batch: acting when nothing is wrong. A miss is passing a drifted batch: failing to act when something is. Statisticians call them type I and type II errors, after Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, who around 1930 named them errors “of the first kind” and “of the second kind”. The share of good batches that get rejected is written α (alpha), and the share of drifted batches that get passed is β (beta).

Every test on the earlier pages is an inspector of this kind:

The inspectorA test
The batch is goodNothing is going on: chance alone, the null hypothesis, is true
The batch has driftedSomething real is going on
Reject the batchReject chance alone: a “significant” result
Pass the batchNot significant: chance alone can’t be ruled out
False alarm rate αThe significance level. As the rain on p-values showed, when chance alone is at work p lands below 0.05 one time in 20, so the rule “p < 0.05” has α = 5% by construction.
Miss rate βSet by how big the real effect is and how much data there is. 1 − β is the test’s power: the next page.

Both rates count mistakes among the batches that really were good or really had drifted, not among your decisions. “α = 5%” does not mean that 5% of rejected batches were good: the last line of the readout above shows the difference with the game’s own numbers. That second share depends on how many drifted batches there were to find, which is why a “significant” result can still be a false alarm more often than 1 time in 20.

A memory aid. In the fable of the boy who cried wolf, the villagers make both mistakes in order. First they run to a wolf that isn’t there: a false alarm, type I. Later they ignore a wolf that is: a miss, type II.

Moving the threshold

A rule of the form “reject if the average is below some weight” fixes both rates in advance. The humps below show where the averages of all good and all drifted batches land, not just these 50. Drag the cutoff and watch the two shaded areas, and the point on the right.

Slide the cutoff left and you reject less: false alarms shrink, misses grow. Slide it right and the reverse happens. The point on the trade-off chart runs along its curve but can’t leave it. With 4 bags per batch, holding false alarms to 5% lets about 36% of drifted batches through. The game’s dot sits near the 4-bag curve; with only 50 batches it can land a little off it on either side, just by luck.

To shrink both mistakes at once, the humps must overlap less. That takes clearer evidence: more bags per batch, or a bigger drift to catch. Try 16 bags: the same 5% false alarm rate now misses fewer than 1 drifted batch in 100. How much data is enough is the subject of the next page.

04

The costs decide

Statistics can tell you how many false alarms and misses each cutoff will produce. It can’t tell you which mistake is worse; that comes from outside the data. Say scrapping a good batch costs one unit. Set what a drifted batch costs once it reaches customers, and how common drift is:

When misses are expensive, the cheapest cutoff moves right and accepts plenty of false alarms. When false alarms are expensive it moves left. Rare drift pushes it left as well: if almost every batch is good, most rejections would be false alarms. Every real decision rule makes this trade, whether or not anyone wrote the costs down:

DecisionFalse alarmMissSo the rule…
Smoke alarmIt beeps at burnt toastIt stays silent in a fireis set to be very sensitive. A miss can kill; a false alarm costs a wave of a towel.
Criminal trialAn innocent person is convictedA guilty person goes freedemands proof “beyond reasonable doubt” and accepts many misses. William Blackstone (1769): “better that ten guilty persons escape, than that one innocent suffer.”
Medical screeningA healthy person is sent for more testsA disease goes unnoticedcasts a wide net first, then sorts out the false alarms with a second, more exact test.
Spam filterA real email lands in the spam folderSpam reaches the inboxlets some spam through, because losing a real email is worse.

Science usually works the other way round: it fixes the false alarm rate first and lets the miss rate fall where it may. The 5% goes back to Ronald Fisher, who in 1925 called a 1-in-20 chance a “convenient” limit. It is a convention, not a law of nature. Fields where a false discovery is very costly ask for more: particle physicists announce a discovery only at “5 sigma”, a false alarm rate of about 1 in 3.5 million.

05

Where you will meet it

Medical tests

Sensitivity is 1 − the miss rate; specificity is 1 − the false alarm rate. A test report quotes both because no test maximizes both.

Machine learning

A classifier’s ROC curve is the trade-off chart from section 3 flipped upside down: it plots hits (1 − β) against false alarms (α) for every threshold.

A/B testing

Shipping a button that doesn’t help is a false alarm; dropping one that would have helped is a miss. Teams pick α and plan the sample size to keep the miss rate down.

Factory quality control

Walter Shewhart’s control charts, from Bell Labs in the 1920s, are this page’s game: sample a few items, and stop the line only when the average strays past a limit.

Security and fraud

Airport scanners and card-fraud systems flag far more innocent bags and purchases than guilty ones, by design: a miss is the costly mistake.

Science

α = 5% is the usual false alarm rate; the miss rate often goes unreported. The next page shows why that matters.

Check yourself
