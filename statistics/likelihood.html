The bridge

Likelihood

Probability runs forward, from a coin to the flips it might produce. Likelihood runs backward, from the flips you saw to the coins that might have made them. Almost every method in statistics, the classical tests and the Bayesian ones alike, is built on it.

01

Same formula, read backwards

The binomial formula gives the chance of k heads in n flips of a coin that lands heads with probability p:

P(k heads | p) = C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n − k

It has two inputs, the coin and the result, and you can hold either one still. Hold the coin still and vary the result, and you get the chances of every possible result: a probability distribution. Hold the result still, the seven heads you actually saw, and vary the coin, and you get a score for every possible coin. That score is the likelihood. The numbers are the same; only the question changes.

One table, two readings

fix the coin: chance of each resultfix the result: likelihood of each coin

Click anywhere in the table. A column, read down, is a probability distribution: its 11 bars always add up to 1, because the coin must produce some result. A row, read across, is a likelihood curve, and its area is never 1. For ten flips it is 1/11 for every row. The likelihood is not a probability that the coin is this or that. It says how well each coin explains what happened, and only comparisons between coins mean anything: p = 0.7 makes seven heads 0.267 likely and p = 0.5 makes it 0.117 likely, so the first explains the data 2.3 times as well.

Ronald Fisher drew this distinction as a young statistician. He presented the method of picking the best-explaining value in 1912, as a third-year undergraduate, named the score “likelihood” in 1921, and gave the method its name, maximum likelihood, in 1922. His definition:

“The likelihood that any parameter (or set of parameters) should have any assigned value (or set of values) is proportional to the probability that if this were so, the totality of observations should be that observed.”R. A. Fisher, “On the mathematical foundations of theoretical statistics”, 1922, p. 310, as quoted by J. Aldrich in Statistical Science, 1997

“Proportional to” matters. Multiply a likelihood curve by any constant and nothing changes, which is why from here on the curves are scaled so the peak is 1, the relative likelihood.

02

The likelihood curve

Here is how the curve is made, one flip at a time. Pick a candidate coin, say p = 0.4. Each head you saw scores 0.4, the chance that coin gives a head, and each tail scores 0.6. Multiply the scores together, since the flips are independent. That product is the likelihood of p = 0.4. Now slide the candidate across every value from 0 to 1 and plot the product each time.

Add flips and watch the raw product. After 100 flips it is around 10−30; after 1,000 it is around 10−300, near the smallest number a computer can store, and a poorer candidate falls below even that. Taking logarithms fixes both problems: the product of scores becomes a sum of log-scores, and since the logarithm only rises, the peak stays in the same place. Statisticians nearly always work with the log-likelihood.

The peak, the coin that explains the data best, is the maximum likelihood estimate, written p̂. For coin flips it is exactly the fraction of heads, p̂ = k/n (setting the slope of the log-likelihood to zero gives it in two lines; the Bernoulli page does the algebra). That is what anyone would have guessed. The machinery earns its keep when the answer is not obvious, as in section 4, and because the curve says more than its peak.

03

More data, sharper peak

Ten flips with seven heads and 1,000 flips with 700 heads peak in the same place, at 0.7. They do not say the same thing. The second curve is far narrower: it rules out every coin outside a thin band. How wide the peak is tells you how precise the estimate is.

The width of the peak

log-likelihoodparabola with the same curvaturewithin 1.92 of the peak
estimates from 1,000 repeatspredicted from one curve’s curvature

Near its peak, any smooth log-likelihood looks like an upside-down parabola. How sharply it bends, its curvature, measures how much the data pin the answer down; statisticians call it the Fisher information. For coin flips it is n / (p̂(1 − p̂)), and one over its square root is

SE = √(p̂(1 − p̂) / n)

the same standard error that set the width of every poll interval on the confidence intervals page. Repeat the experiment and the 1,000 estimates spread out by just this much: a single curve, from a single dataset, predicts how much its own peak would wobble from sample to sample. Since curvature grows like n, the width shrinks like 1/√n, the √n law again.

The shaded band, every coin whose log-likelihood is within 1.92 of the peak, is a likelihood interval; section 5 explains the 1.92. With many flips it matches p̂ ± 1.96 SE. With few flips, or a share near 0 or 1, the curve is lopsided and the two disagree. Set the share to 0: no heads in 10 flips. The parabola has infinite curvature there, and the shortcut claims p is exactly 0, give or take nothing. The curve itself says any coin up to about 0.17 still explains the data reasonably well.

04

Horse kicks and tanks

The recipe never changes: write down the chance of the data for each possible setting, then find the setting that makes it largest. It works for any model, not just coins.

Horse kicks

Bortkiewicz’s 1898 count, from the Poisson page: 10 Prussian army corps watched for 20 years, 200 corps-years, with 109 having no deaths from horse kicks, 65 one, 22 two, 3 three and 1 four, 122 deaths in all. If deaths come at random at some rate λ per corps-year, the Poisson formula gives the chance of each count, and the likelihood of λ is the product of those chances over all 200 corps-years.

The peak is at λ̂ = 0.61, which is 122 deaths over 200 corps-years: the plain average again. The curvature gives a standard error of √(0.61/200) = 0.055, and the likelihood interval runs from 0.51 to 0.72. Away from the peak the fit gets visibly worse: at λ = 1 the model expects 74 empty corps-years instead of 109, and the likelihood has fallen to 2 in 100 million of its peak.

Tanks

The German tank problem from page 4: four captured tanks carry serial numbers 19, 40, 42 and 60. If the enemy made N tanks, numbered 1 to N, every set of four serial numbers is equally likely to be captured, so the chance of exactly this set is one over the number of possible sets. That is zero if N is below 60, since tank 60 exists, and shrinks steadily as N grows above 60.

The likelihood of every factory size

estimates from 1,000 capturestrue number, 100▲ average estimate

The likelihood peaks at N = 60, the largest serial number seen. That is the maximum likelihood estimate, and it is clearly too low: the largest of four captured tanks is almost never the last one made. From a factory of 100 it averages 80.8. Maximum likelihood picks the setting that makes this sample most probable, which is not the same as a setting that is right on average. The “largest + gap” recipe from page 4 adds the average gap between serial numbers, giving 74 here, and averages exactly 100.

Where maximum likelihood goes wrong. It can be biased in small samples, as with the tanks. The same happens with the spread of a normal distribution: the maximum likelihood estimate of the variance divides by n, and on average comes out too small by a factor (n − 1)/n, which is why the usual s divides by n − 1 (the Chi-square page shows why). The bias fades as samples grow. A deeper limit is that the likelihood only ranks settings inside the model you chose. If the horse-kick rate differed from corps to corps, the Poisson curve would still have a peak, but its width would understate the uncertainty, and nothing in the curve would warn you. Checking the model, as the χ² test did for the horse kicks, is a separate job.

05

Where it goes next

The likelihood curve is where the two big schools of statistics part ways. Both start from it.

Road one: compare two settings. To test whether the coin is fair, compare the best coin with the fair one. The ratio of their likelihoods, L(p̂) / L(0.5), is the likelihood ratio. In 1938 Samuel Wilks showed that, when the fair coin is the truth, twice the log of this ratio follows, in large samples, the chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom (one for each setting the claim fixes). So the ratio becomes a p-value: the likelihood ratio test. The 1.92 of section 3 is half of 3.84, the chi-square cut-off for 5%; the likelihood interval is every coin this test would not reject.

Road two: weigh every setting. Bayesian statistics treats the coin itself as uncertain. Start with a prior, how plausible each coin seemed before the flips, multiply it point by point by the likelihood, and rescale so the area is 1. The result is the posterior, how plausible each coin is now. A flat prior gives back the likelihood curve itself, now with area 1, so it can be read as probabilities.

Two roads from one curve

likelihoodpriorposterior

With 7 heads in 10, the best coin explains the data 2.3 times as well as a fair one. Wilks’s curve turns that into p = 0.20 (the exact binomial test, better for 10 flips, gives 0.34): no evidence against fairness. A flat prior puts 89% of the posterior above 0.5; a prior that expects coins to be near fair pulls the posterior toward 0.5 and gives 72%. With 70 heads in 100, every route agrees the coin is biased. Both roads also lead further: likelihood ratios to comparing whole models, posteriors to the whole Bayesian half of this series.

06

Where you will meet it

Machine learning

Training a classifier by minimizing “cross-entropy loss” is maximizing a log-likelihood. The loss is the negative log-likelihood of the labels.

Regression for yes/no outcomes

Logistic regression has no least-squares shortcut; its S-shaped curve is fitted by climbing the likelihood, as the horse-kick widget did.

Survival studies

Patients still alive when a study ends contribute “survived at least this long” to the likelihood, so nobody’s data is thrown away.

Genetics

Linkage studies report LOD scores, the base-10 log of a likelihood ratio, and evolutionary trees are routinely chosen by maximum likelihood.

Physics

Particle discoveries are declared from likelihood ratio tests comparing “signal plus background” with “background only”.

Bayesian inference

Every posterior is a prior times a likelihood. The next pages build it up from there.
