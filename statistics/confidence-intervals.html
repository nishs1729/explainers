Confidence Intervals

An estimate with a range around it: how big the effect might be, and how sure you can be. “95% confident” is a promise about the method that made the range: used again and again, it catches the truth 95% of the time.

01

Estimate ± wobble

On Sampling Distributions, honest polls of 1,000 people scattered around the true support with a standard error of about 1.6 points. Like any bell, 95% of that scatter lies within 1.96 standard errors of the center; that is where the 1.96 comes from (see the Normal page). So 95% of polls land within about 3.1 points of the truth.

Now flip it around. If the poll is within 3.1 points of the truth, then the truth is within 3.1 points of the poll. So “estimate ± 3.1” reaches back to the truth exactly when the poll landed in the middle 95%, which happens in 95% of polls. Drag the poll result below to see the flip.

The flip: from wobble to interval

middle 95% of pollsyour poll (drag it)true support

That is the whole recipe for a confidence interval: estimate ± 1.96 standard errors. In practice the standard error itself is estimated from the sample, as √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n) for a percentage, or s/√n for an average, and for statistics with no formula the bootstrap provides it.

02

Coverage

The 95% is a property of the recipe, so the way to see it is to use the recipe many times. Each bar below is one poll’s interval; the newest appears at the top. The truth never moves; the intervals do.

The interval rain

catches the truthmissesnewest

Over many polls, the share of intervals that catch the truth, the coverage, settles at the advertised level: about 95 in 100 for a 95% interval. Any single interval either contains the truth or it doesn’t, and in real life you never find out which. The 95% is your confidence in the method, not a probability attached to one particular range.

With very small polls the simple recipe falls a little short of its promise: at 25 people per poll, its 95% intervals catch the truth about 93% of the time. Section 5 shows why, and what to do about it.

03

Width

Two dials set how wide the interval is. Try both in the rain above.

  • Confidence level. To catch the truth more often, cast a wider net: 99% intervals use 2.58 standard errors instead of 1.96. To be right 100% of the time you would need an interval from 0% to 100%, which tells you nothing. There is always a trade between confidence and precision.
  • Sample size. The standard error shrinks like 1/√n, so to halve the width you need four times the people. That square-root law is why national polls rarely go much beyond a few thousand people: each extra point of precision costs more than the last.

04

Intervals and tests are the same thing

Back to the button test: 50 clicks from 1,000 visitors on grey, 64 from 1,000 on orange. The gap is 1.4 points, and its 95% confidence interval runs from about −0.6 to +3.4 points. The interval contains 0, so “no difference” is among the plausible values, and that is exactly the same conclusion as the p-value of about 0.18 on the previous page. Move orange’s click count and watch the two agree:

One result, two readings

Every value inside a 95% interval is one a test at the 0.05 level would not reject; every value outside it is one the test would. When 0 drops out of the interval, p drops below 0.05 at the same moment. (Both here use the same standard error; other formulas can disagree in the third decimal place.)

The interval says more than the test, though. “p = 0.18” says only that the data can’t rule out no difference. “−0.6 to +3.4 points” says that too, and adds that orange could plausibly be better by as much as 3.4 points: worth testing again with more visitors. That is why careful reports give intervals, not just p-values.

When the simple interval fails

“Estimate ± 1.96 standard errors” leans on the bell shape, and for percentages near 0% or 100% in small samples the bell is a poor fit. The Binomial page met the problem from the other side. Here is the coverage, worked out exactly, of the simple interval and of a better one, the Wilson interval, for every true percentage:

Does “95%” really mean 95%?

Coverage (up) for every true percentage (across), worked out exactly.

simple interval (estimate ± 1.96 SE)Wilson intervalthe promised 95%

The simple interval collapses near the edges. With 20 people and a true rate of 5%, it catches the truth only about 64% of the time while claiming 95%. At the extreme, if 0 of 20 customers complain, it reports 0% ± 0%, as if a sample of 20 could prove that nobody ever complains. The Wilson interval, from 1927, stays close to 95% almost everywhere and gives 0% to 16% for that case. The jagged lines are real: because counts are whole numbers, coverage jumps as the true rate shifts (Brown, Cai and DasGupta 2001). The lesson generalizes. A confidence level is a promise that rests on assumptions, and it is worth checking, by simulation or exact calculation, when the data are small or lopsided.

06

Misreadings

A poll reports 52%, with a 95% confidence interval of 49% to 55%. Which of these readings hold up?

ReadingVerdict
“The method behind this range catches the true support in 95% of polls.”Right. That is the definition.
“Support between 49% and 55% fits these data; values far outside it don’t.”Right. A fair everyday summary.
“There is a 95% probability the true support is between 49% and 55%.”Not quite. In this framework the truth is fixed and this range either contains it or not. A statement of that form needs a prior and Bayes’ theorem: see Credible intervals, later in the series. (With lots of data and no strong prior, the numbers often come out similar.)
“95% of voters have a view between 49% and 55%.”Wrong. The interval is about the uncertainty in one number, the overall percentage, not about the spread of individuals.
“A repeat poll will land inside 49% to 55% 95% of the time.”Wrong. The repeat wobbles too. Only about 83% of repeat estimates land inside the original 95% interval.
“The truth is more likely to be near 52% than near 49%.”Careful. Values near the middle fit the data better, but the confidence interval itself only marks a range, not a probability for each value inside it.
07

Where you will meet it

Polls

The “margin of error” is half the width of a 95% interval. It covers only the wobble of random sampling, not who refused to answer or how the question was worded.

Medicine

Trial reports give the treatment effect with a 95% interval. In a meta-analysis, a “forest plot” lines up the intervals from many studies, one above another, exactly like the rain on this page.

A/B testing

Good dashboards show the lift with an interval, so a team can see both whether it might be zero and how big it might plausibly be.

Science graphs

Error bars may be standard deviations, standard errors or 95% intervals, which differ a lot. A good caption says which.

Official statistics

Monthly figures from surveys, such as job counts and unemployment rates, come with confidence intervals, and the month-to-month change is often inside them.

Machine learning

A model’s accuracy on a test set is a proportion, so it deserves an interval. On a small test set, two models a point apart may be indistinguishable.

Check yourself

An interval that includes 0 means the data couldn’t tell. Any time a decision is made from data, two kinds of mistake are possible: acting on a fluke, and missing something real. Balancing them is the next page, Errors and decisions (coming soon).
