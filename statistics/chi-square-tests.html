A visual introduction

Chi-Square Tests

You expected so many of each kind and got something else. Is the difference real, or the ordinary unevenness of chance? Add up how far the counts you got are from the counts you expected, cell by cell, and compare the total with what chance alone produces. One recipe tests dice, theories and tables of counts.

Gregor Mendel’s pea counts fit his theory almost perfectly. In 1936 Ronald Fisher asked whether they fit too perfectly. To judge that, you first need to know how uneven honest counts are.

Roll a fair die 60 times. Each face should come up about 10 times. How often does every face land between 8 and 12?

01

Observed vs expected

Fair dice are lumpier than intuition says, so “it looks uneven” is no evidence at all. What’s needed is one number that says how uneven a set of counts is, and a yardstick for how large that number gets by chance alone. Karl Pearson supplied both in 1900.

His number is built one cell at a time. For each face, take the gap between the count you got and the count you expected. Step through the three versions below and watch the total.

Built cell by cell

The plain gaps always add up to zero: every extra six is a missing something else. Squaring stops the cancelling. The last step divides each square by the count expected in that cell, because a gap of 5 is huge when you expect 10 and nothing when you expect 1,000:

ExpectedObservedGapGap²Gap² ÷ expected
10155252.5
1,0001,0055250.025

Counts wobble by about the square root of what you expect (the Poisson rule), so dividing by the expected count puts every cell on the same footing. The sum is Pearson’s chi-square statistic:

χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² ÷ expected, added over every cell. Zero means a perfect match; the bigger it is, the further the counts are from what you expected.

02

Goodness of fit

A χ² of 6 means nothing until you know what χ² fair dice produce. So build the world where chance alone is at work: roll a fair die the same number of times, again and again, compute χ² each time, and see where the real value lands. Then try it as a game. The page hides a die that is either fair or loaded on one face. Roll it as often as you like and decide.

The gray histogram is the yardstick, and its shape barely depends on how many times you roll. That is what makes the shortcut possible. Pearson showed that when expected counts are not too small, χ² follows the chi-square distribution, the sum of squared bell-curve draws, with one degree of freedom for every count that is free to vary. Six counts that must add up to the number of rolls leave five free, so a die is judged against the 5-df curve. Switch it on: it lies along the simulated histogram, and its tail beyond 11.07 holds 5%.

A loaded die is easy to catch with 600 rolls and almost impossible with 10. The test can only find what the data are able to show: the power lesson again.

Horse kicks and the Poisson

The same recipe checks a whole theory. The Poisson page showed Bortkiewicz’s 1898 count of Prussian cavalrymen killed by horse kicks: 10 army corps watched for 20 years, 200 corps-years, 122 deaths, an average of 0.61. If deaths strike at random, the Poisson formula says how many corps-years should have 0, 1, 2 or more deaths.

Deaths per corps-year against the Poisson

observed corps-yearsexpected if Poisson (average 0.61)

The rare classes are merged so that no class is tiny (3 and 4 deaths become “3+”, which expects 4.8), leaving 4 cells. That is 3 free counts, minus one more because the average 0.61 was estimated from the same data, so the curve has 2 degrees of freedom. That extra subtraction is Fisher’s: Pearson’s method, from his 1900 paper on, did not take a degree of freedom off for quantities estimated from the data, for fitted averages or for the row and column totals of a table, and Fisher corrected it in 1922–24. A χ² of 0.32 is right in the middle of what chance produces: the Poisson fits.

03

Tables of counts

The button test is a table of counts too. Each of 2,000 visitors saw one button and either clicked or didn’t. If clicking has nothing to do with the color, both buttons should get the overall click rate, 114 of 2,000 = 5.7%, so each button’s 1,000 visitors should produce 57 clicks and 943 non-clicks. Those are the expected counts, and the recipe is unchanged.

The world where nothing is going on is the shuffle from Shuffling to Test: keep the 114 clicks, deal them out to visitors at random, and recompute χ². Edit any count in the table, or add a button.

For the button test, χ² = 1.82, and about 21% of shuffles reach it. The 1-df curve gives p = 0.18, which is exactly the p of the two-proportion z-test on p-values: for a table with two rows and two columns, χ² is z² (1.35² = 1.82), and the two tests are the same test. The shuffle’s slightly larger 0.21 comes from counts being whole numbers: the shuffle counts every table exactly as lopsided as the real one in full, and the smooth curve only half-counts that step. Yates’s 1934 “continuity correction” nudges the curve to match and gives 0.21 too.

With the same click rates and 4,300 visitors per button, χ² = 7.8 and p = 0.005. Nothing about the gap changed, only how much evidence there is. Add a third button and the curve has 2 degrees of freedom: in general a table with r rows and c columns has (r − 1)(c − 1), the number of cells you can fill freely once the row and column totals are fixed.

Note what χ² does not say: which button is better, or by how much. A big χ² only says the table is far from “no connection”. The contribution bars show where the gaps are; the size of the effect needs a confidence interval.

Too good to be true

Back to the hero’s question. In 1866 Mendel reported crossing peas and counting the offspring. His theory said that for each of seven traits, the dominant and recessive forms should appear in a 3 : 1 ratio. Here are his counts and how far each is from 3 : 1:

No single row is suspicious. But a χ² can be too small: honest counts wobble, and seven experiments in a row that all land this close to 3 : 1 happen only about 1 time in 20. Replay Mendel’s seven experiments with honest 3 : 1 chance and see how rarely the total is that small. Then switch to Fisher’s full analysis, which combined 84 comparisons from the whole paper; the replays there are 84 honest yes-or-no experiments of Mendel’s own sizes, from 19 seeds to 8,023.

honest replaysreplays fitting at least as wellMendel’s totalχ² curve

Fisher’s total for 84 comparisons was χ² = 41.6, where honest data average 84. The widget splits Mendel’s data into 84 yes-or-no counts, as Edwards (1986) and Pires and Branco (2010) did, which gives 41.3. A fit that good or better happens only 2 or 3 times in 100,000, both in 2 million honest replays and on the χ² curve (Fisher’s printed tables gave 7 in 100,000). So 1,000 replays will almost never show one; you would need tens of thousands. This is a test in the left tail: instead of asking whether the data are too far from the theory, it asks whether they are too close.

Fisher concluded that the data had been adjusted, suggesting Mendel “was deceived by some assistant who knew too well what was expected”. The argument has run ever since (see Franklin and colleagues’ 2008 book Ending the Mendel–Fisher Controversy). Proposed explanations include stopping a count when the ratio looked right, discarding plants suspected of contamination, and repeating experiments and reporting the better one; Pires and Branco (2010) found that the last of these could produce the pattern. Fisher’s sharpest single point was about the 2 : 1 ratios. Mendel sorted plants into pure and hybrid by growing 10 seeds from each, and a hybrid shows no recessive among 10 seeds (3/4)¹⁰ = 5.6% of the time, so the honest expectation is about 1.7 : 1, yet his counts fit 2 : 1. Others have argued that his procedure may have differed from Fisher’s reading. Few historians think Mendel simply invented numbers, and his theory was right. But the lesson stands: honest data are noisy, and data with too little noise are a warning sign of a human hand.

05

When it breaks

The χ² curve is a smooth stand-in for a lumpy reality. With large expected counts the lumps are fine-grained and the curve is close. With tiny ones, the statistic can only take a handful of values, and the curve misjudges how often each one occurs. Below, the jagged line is the exact answer from every possible shuffle of the table, which is Fisher’s exact test; the smooth one is the curve. (Fisher’s test ranks tables by how likely they are rather than by χ², but with equal-sized groups, as here, the two rankings agree.)

every possible shuffle (exact)χ² curve, 1 dfthis table

With 20 visitors per button and 0 against 4 clicks, the curve says p = 0.035, “significant”; every possible shuffle says 0.11. Only 4 clicks can be dealt out, so there are only three distinct χ² values, and the curve smears them into a smooth tail. By 1,000 per button the two lines run close together, and what is left is the whole-number gap from section 3 (0.18 against 0.21). The usual rule of thumb is to trust the curve when every expected count is at least 5; the 50-per-button table sits right at that line and the curve still halves the p-value. When counts are small, merge sparse categories (as with the horse kicks) or use the exact shuffle, which a computer does instantly.

  • Counts, not percentages. χ² must be computed on counts. The same percentages from 10 times as many people give 10 times the χ².
  • One count per independent unit. If one visitor can click many times, or one patient contributes several measurements, the counts are not independent and χ² overstates the evidence.
  • Huge samples find everything. With millions of rows, trivial differences give enormous χ². Look at the size of the gaps, not just p.
  • Direction is lost. Squaring throws away the sign. χ² says the table departs from “no connection” but not how; read the cells.
06

Where you will meet it

Genetics

Mendelian ratios, and whether a population’s genotype counts match the Hardy–Weinberg proportions, are classic goodness-of-fit tests.

A/B tests on rates

Clicks, sign-ups and conversions by variant form a table of counts; χ² with more than two variants tests them all at once.

Surveys

Cross-tabulations such as answer by age group or region are tested for any connection with χ².

Fraud checks

First digits of genuine financial figures follow Benford’s law; a χ² against those proportions flags books that don’t.

Quality control

Defects by shift, machine or supplier: are they spread as their share of production would predict?

Checking a model

Does a Poisson, a binomial or a fitted curve really describe the data? Bin it and compare counts, as with the horse kicks.

Check yourself

χ² compares counts across groups. Comparing averages across many groups at once needs a different yardstick, the spread between groups against the spread within them: the next page, ANOVA.
