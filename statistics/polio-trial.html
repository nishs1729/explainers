A case study

The 1954 Polio Vaccine Trial

In 1954 about 1.8 million American schoolchildren, and some 70,000 more in Canada and Finland, took part in what the statistician Paul Meier called “the biggest public health experiment ever”, to find out whether Jonas Salk’s polio vaccine worked. Everything in this series so far was needed to get the answer right.

Polio struck only a few dozen children per 100,000 in a year. How do you tell if a vaccine works?

The trial ran two designs side by side. In some areas, second-graders whose parents agreed got the vaccine, and first- and third-graders were watched for comparison. In others, children whose parents agreed got either the vaccine or an injection of salt water, chosen at random, with nobody told which. Which design showed the bigger effect of the vaccine?

Make your guess, then see the results.

01

The problem

Polio epidemics came every summer, crippling and sometimes killing children, and parents dreaded them. It struck hardest, oddly, among the better-off: children raised in cleaner homes met the virus later in life, when it did more damage. By 1954 Jonas Salk had a vaccine made from killed virus, and the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis, the charity behind the March of Dimes, decided to test it on a vast scale. The evaluation was run independently by Thomas Francis Jr. at the University of Michigan.

The difficulty was rarity. Even in the groups that got salt water, about 71 children in 100,000 caught polio that year. Here is what that looks like.

A test on 1,000 children would expect less than one case in either group. To see a difference between 71 and 28 per 100,000 at all, the trial needed hundreds of thousands of children in each group, and section 5 shows why. It also needed a design that nobody could argue with, because whatever it found would decide whether millions more children were vaccinated.

02

Two designs, side by side

The Foundation’s first plan was the observed-control design: offer the vaccine to second-graders and compare them with the first and third grades, who got nothing. It was simple and needed no fake injections. Critics pointed to two flaws. Doctors diagnosing a borderline case would know whether the child had been vaccinated. And only children whose parents volunteered got the vaccine, while the comparison grades included everyone. Volunteers, Paul Meier noted in his account of the trial, tend to be better educated and better off, and polio hit the better-off harder.

Each state’s health officer chose the design, and in 91 of the 217 U.S. areas they chose the randomized, double-blind one: among children whose parents consented, a random half got the vaccine and the rest got salt water* from identical-looking vials, and neither the families nor the diagnosing doctors knew which. When consent doesn’t depend on income, both designs land on the truth (with blind diagnosis). When it does, the volunteer design compares higher-risk volunteers with a mixed comparison group, and its estimate shrinks. If the vaccine did nothing at all, the volunteers would get more polio than the comparison grades, and the vaccine would look harmful. The randomized design is right in every setting, because the coin decides who gets the vaccine, so the two groups match in income and in everything else, measured or not. This is the selection problem from Who’s missing?, and randomization is its cure.

Randomization fixes who gets the vaccine, not who judges the cases. Switch diagnosis to doctors know: now even the randomized design overstates the vaccine, by about 8 cases per 100,000 in this model. In the volunteer design, at these settings, the two biases pull opposite ways and roughly cancel, which is luck that nobody could have counted on. In the real observed-control areas the doctors could know, because only the vaccinated grade-2 volunteers had been injected. That is why the randomized areas were double-blind as well.

The real results show the same signature. Within each design, children whose parents refused had lower rates than the children they were compared with: 46 per 100,000 against 71 for salt water in the randomized areas, and 44 against 54 for grades 1 and 3 in the observed-control areas, because refusing families were on average less well-off. And the observed-control design understated the vaccine’s effect: 29 cases prevented per 100,000 against 42.

03

Is the difference chance?

In the randomized areas, 199 children caught polio: 57 who had the vaccine and 142 who had salt water. Could a useless vaccine produce a split that lopsided? Ask it the way Shuffling to Test did: if the vaccine does nothing, the 199 cases would have struck whoever they struck regardless, and the vials were assigned at random. So deal the 199 cases out at random among the 401,974 children and count how many land in the vaccine group.

Shuffled, the vaccine group gets about 99 of the 199 cases, give or take 7. The real trial had 57, six standard deviations below. No shuffle comes close, and the formula puts the p-value at about 2 in a billion. Chance is not the explanation.

04

How sure, how big?

A tiny p-value says the effect is real, not how large it is. For that, a confidence interval for the difference in rates: 70.6 − 28.4 = 42 cases prevented per 100,000 children, give or take about 14. As a share, the vaccine prevented about 60% of polio cases (95% interval 45% to 70%) and about 71% of paralytic cases (58% to 81%).

The observed-control design gives an even narrower interval, 20 to 37, because its groups are bigger, and it doesn’t reach the randomized estimate of 42. An interval measures how much an estimate wobbles from chance. It says nothing about bias: a biased design gives a precise answer to the wrong question. Only the design can fix that.

05

Why it had to be so big

Before the trial, nobody knew how bad the 1954 season would be or how well the vaccine would work. Suppose it halved a placebo rate of 71 per 100,000. How many children per group would a trial need to detect that? This is the power calculation, with a very rare outcome.

Trial size and power

1954: the real trial, about 200,000 per group
100 simulated trials of this size (green = effect detected)

With 10,000 children per group, the salt-water group expects about 7 cases and the vaccine group 3 or 4, and the trial finds a real halving only about 1 time in 5. Reaching 80% power takes about 67,000 per group, and 90% about 89,000. The real trial used about 200,000 per group in the randomized areas, enough to give a clear answer even in a mild polio year.

06

What it teaches

On April 12, 1955, Francis announced the results: the vaccine was safe and effective, and mass vaccination began. Polio cases in the United States fell steeply over the following years. The trial used nearly every idea in this series:

Acting on a right answer still carries risk: within weeks, about 120,000 doses from Cutter Laboratories turned out to contain live virus. Vaccinated children and their contacts caught polio from it: about 200 were left paralyzed (164 severely) and 10 died. The lots were withdrawn on April 27, 1955 (the Cutter incident; P. Offit, The Cutter Incident, 2005).
PowerA rare outcome needs enormous groups; tens of thousands per group would have risked missing the effect.
Forking pathsThe outcome, the diagnosis rules and the analysis were fixed in advance, and diagnosis was blind.
