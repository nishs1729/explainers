01

The tea test

The story comes from the statistician Ronald Fisher, who used it to explain the logic of experiments in his book The Design of Experiments (1935). The lady was Muriel Bristol, a colleague at an agricultural research station in England. Fisher’s point was not about tea. It was about how to decide, before pouring a single cup, what result would count as evidence.

His reasoning: suppose she can’t taste the difference at all. Then her choice of 4 cups is just one of the ways to pick 4 cups out of 8, and every way is equally likely. There are exactly 70 of them (the counting from the Binomial page: 8 × 7 × 6 × 5 ÷ (4 × 3 × 2 × 1) = 70). Only one of them is all correct. So a pure guesser gets all 4 right 1 time in 70.

Be the guesser yourself. The cups below have been poured at random; you can’t taste anything, so just pick 4.

The 70 strips form a histogram made of every possible guess: 1 way to get none right, 16 ways to get 1, 36 ways to get 2, 16 to get 3, and 1 to get all 4. Getting 3 or more right happens 17 times in 70, about 1 in 4, which is far too often to convince anyone. Only a perfect score is rare enough. According to a colleague who was there, Muriel Bristol got all eight cups right; the story was retold in David Salsburg’s book The Lady Tasting Tea (2001).

02

Shuffle the labels

The tea test had only 70 possibilities, so Fisher could list them all. Most data have far too many, but the same idea works by random sampling. Take the button test from the first page: 1,000 visitors saw the grey button and 50 clicked (5.0%); 1,000 saw the orange one and 64 clicked (6.4%). A gap of 1.4 points.

If the color makes no difference, each visitor would have clicked or not whichever button they saw. Then the labels “grey” and “orange” are arbitrary: deal the same 2,000 visitors, with their 114 clicks, into two new groups of 1,000 at random, and you get a gap that chance alone produced. Do that many times and you see how big chance’s gaps get.

About 21% of shuffles produce a gap at least as big as the real one, in one direction or the other. Chance alone does this roughly 1 time in 5, so the button test can’t show that orange is better. (On the first page, a slightly different chance world, two identical buttons each shown to 1,000 fresh visitors, gave about 19%. Both say the same thing.)

This is a permutation test, also called a randomization test. The fraction of shuffles at least as extreme as the real data is the famous p-value, the subject of the next page.

Why shuffling is allowed. The shuffle is a fair stand-in for chance because the visitors were assigned to the buttons at random in the first place. If color does nothing, the real split was just one of the many random splits the shuffle explores. Random assignment, the cure from Who’s Missing?, is what licenses the test.

03

Sign flips for paired data

The sleep trial is different. Each of the 10 patients took both drugs, so the data come in pairs, and shuffling patients between groups would break those pairs apart. Here the shuffle happens inside each patient. If the two drugs work the same, it was a coin toss which of the two nights came out better, so each patient’s difference could just as well have had the opposite sign. Flipping signs is how this data gets shuffled.

With 10 patients there are 210 = 1,024 ways to choose the signs, few enough to try every one. Click a patient to flip their sign, or try them all:

Flipping signs, patient by patient

Average gain under every set of signs tried

Of all 1,024 sign patterns, only 2 give an average gain of 1.58 hours or more, and 4 give an average that far from zero in either direction: 0.4%. (Each pattern is counted twice, because one patient’s difference is exactly 0 and flipping it changes nothing.) Unlike the button test, this is something chance alone almost never does. On the first page that was the bottom line; now you have seen exactly how it was computed.

04

Every test is this test

Three different problems, one recipe. The statistician Allen Downey summed it up as “there is only one test”:

  1. Compute a number from the data that measures the effect you care about: cups right, a gap, an average gain.
  2. Build the world where nothing is going on, by shuffling whatever would be arbitrary in that world: cup choices, group labels, signs.
  3. See where the real number lands among the shuffled ones. If it sits among them, chance is a fine explanation; if it is out beyond almost all of them, chance struggles.

The named tests later in this series, the t-test, the chi-square test, ANOVA, the test for a regression slope, all follow this recipe. They replace the shuffling with a formula for where the shuffled numbers would fall, which was essential before computers. Each of those pages will show its formula next to the shuffle it stands in for, and they will agree. The tea test itself survives under the name Fisher’s exact test.

05

Where you will meet it

A/B testing

Shuffling which users saw which version is a standard way to check a website experiment, and it works for any metric, from click rates to revenue per user.

Clinical trials

When patients are randomized, re-randomizing the treatment labels on the computer gives a test that rests on nothing but the trial’s own design.

Genetics and brain imaging

With thousands of genes or brain regions tested at once, shuffling the labels and rerunning the whole analysis shows how many “hits” chance alone produces.

Machine learning

Permutation importance shuffles one input column and measures how much a model’s accuracy drops. A feature whose shuffling costs nothing wasn’t helping.

Ecology and geography

Are species that live close together more alike? Shuffling which location goes with which sample builds the world where geography doesn’t matter.

Sports and streaks

Shuffling the order of a player’s hits and misses shows how streaky a player with no “hot hand” would look, exactly like the coin streaks on the first page.

Check yourself

Every test on this page ended with a fraction: 1 in 70, 21%, 4 in 1,024. That fraction has a name, a long history of misuse, and a page of its own: p-values (coming soon).
