Power and Sample Size

A study can only find what it is big enough to see. Too small, and a real effect usually slips past unnoticed; and on the rare occasions it is noticed, it looks bigger than it really is.

Power

On Errors and Decisions, the inspector’s miss rate depended on how much the good and drifted humps overlapped. A test is the same. Below are the two worlds for the button test’s gap (orange minus gray): one where the buttons are equal, one where orange really lifts clicks by 1.4 points. A gap beyond the dashed lines counts as significant. Slide the number of visitors and watch the humps narrow.

The chance that a test detects an effect that is really there is its power. It is 1 − β, one minus the miss rate. With 1,000 visitors per button, a gap has to reach about 2 points to count as significant, more than the 1.4-point lift itself. The test succeeds only when chance happens to stretch the gap, which it does about 27% of the time. With 4,300 per button both humps narrow, the cutoff moves in to 1 point, and 80% of the real-lift hump lies beyond it. A stricter α pushes the cutoffs back out and shrinks the shaded part again.

So the button test was never likely to find this lift. Even if orange is truly better by 1.4 points, “not significant” was the expected outcome, about 3 times in 4.

The three dials

Power is set by three things you could see in the picture: how big the effect is (how far apart the humps sit), how much data there is (how narrow they are), and α (where the cutoff goes).   • Effect size. Power climbs fast as the effect grows. A lift to 8% is found about 3 times in 4 with 1,000 per button (and 97 times in 100 with 2,000); a lift to 5.5% almost never.
  • Sample size. The humps narrow like 1/√n, the same square-root law as on Confidence Intervals. Halve the effect you want to see and you need four times the visitors.
  • α. A stricter cutoff, 0.01 instead of 0.05, means fewer false alarms and more misses: the trade from Errors and Decisions. At the same size, power drops.

The formula line is the shortcut: it is what the dots settle onto as you run more studies. It comes from the bell-curve approximation to the two humps, the same one behind the z-test on p-values.

03

Planning a study

Power is most useful before the data arrive. Decide the smallest effect that would be worth knowing about, then choose a size that gives a good chance of seeing it, usually 80% or 90%. The curves below are the formula for each lift at α = 0.05:

A handy rule of thumb (Lehr 1992): for 80% power at α = 0.05, each group needs about 16 × variance ÷ effect². For clicks the variance is about p(1 − p) = 0.057 × 0.943, and the effect is 0.014, which gives about 4,400 per button, close to the exact 4,300.

Two warnings. First, plan for the smallest effect that matters, not the one you hope for: optimism is the usual reason studies come out too small. Second, don’t compute power afterwards from the effect you happened to observe. That “observed power” is just the p-value in disguise: it is a fixed function of p. A result at exactly p = 0.05 always has observed power of about 50%, one at p = 0.01 about 73%, one at p = 0.5 about 10%, whatever the study. It repeats the p-value in other units and adds nothing (Hoenig and Heisey 2001). After the data are in, the confidence interval is the honest summary.

04

Absence of evidence

“Not significant” is often reported as “no effect”. With a low-powered test that is a serious misreading: the test would have said “not significant” most of the time even if the effect were real. As Douglas Altman and Martin Bland put it in the BMJ in 1995, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

The confidence interval shows the difference. Below is the real button test next to an imaginary bigger one that found exactly no gap. Suppose the team had decided that any lift under 1 point isn’t worth a redesign:

no differencesmallest lift worth having (drag it)

Both include 0, so neither is significant. But the first also includes lifts of 2 or 3 points: it can’t tell a useless button from a very good one. At the 1-point line, the second rules out every lift worth having; that one really is evidence that orange doesn’t matter. Drag the line down to 0.9 points or below and even the big test can no longer rule it out: “worth having” has to be decided before the data, not after. A non-significant result from a small study says “we don’t know yet”. Only a study with enough power, or a narrow interval, can say “there’s nothing here worth having”.

Checking that the whole interval sits below the smallest effect worth having is the idea behind an equivalence test. The standard version, two one-sided tests at α = 0.05, uses a 90% interval, a little narrower than the 95% ones drawn here.

05

The winner’s curse

Low power has a second, sneakier cost. Suppose the button test had come out significant. At 1,000 per button that only happens when chance stretched the gap past 2 points, so the tests that “succeed” are exactly the ones that overestimated. Run many tests and keep only the winners. First, a guess:

How much the significant tests exaggerate, by size

At 1,000 per button the significant tests report a lift of about 2.65 points on average, nearly twice the truth. At 250 per button they report more than three times the truth, and a few even get the direction wrong. At 4,300 or more, the winners are close to honest. The lower chart shows the whole pattern: the exaggeration falls toward 1× as power rises toward 100%. Andrew Gelman and John Carlin (2014) call these type M (magnitude) and type S (sign) errors: in an underpowered study, a significant result is likely to be too big, and sometimes backwards.

This is one reason exciting findings shrink when someone checks them. When the Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 published psychology studies, 97% of the originals had been significant but only 36% of the repeats were, and the repeats’ effects were on average about half as large. Whenever a small study reports a surprisingly large effect, expect the truth to be smaller.

06

Where you will meet it

Clinical trials

A trial protocol has to state its power calculation up front: the effect worth detecting, α, and the target power, typically 80% or 90%. Ethics committees reject trials too small to answer their question.

A/B testing

Sample size calculators ask for the current rate and the smallest lift worth finding. Stopping the moment p dips below 0.05 breaks the plan, a trap for the page on forking paths.

Replication

A repeat of a study should be bigger than the original, because the original effect was probably inflated by the winner’s curse.

Neuroscience

A 2013 survey (Button and colleagues) estimated the median power of neuroscience studies at about 21%, though it varies a lot between fields.

Safety and the environment

“No significant increase in side effects” or “no significant decline in the species” from a small study is weak comfort: check the interval.

Grant proposals

Funders ask for a power analysis to show that the money will buy a study able to see what it is looking for.

Check yourself

Everything so far used percentages, where the bell-curve shortcut works well with big samples. Measurements from tiny samples, like the 10 patients of the sleep trial, need a correction for the extra uncertainty of estimating their spread. That correction is the next page, Student’s t.
