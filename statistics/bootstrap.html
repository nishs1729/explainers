The Bootstrap

You have one sample and want to know how much a number computed from it would wobble, but there are no other samples to compare it with. So make some: draw new samples from your own data, and watch the number wobble.

01

Pretend your sample is the population

On Sampling Distributions the answer to “how much does this number wobble?” was easy: draw thousands of samples from the population, compute the number for each, and look at the spread. In real life that is impossible. You have one sample, and the population is exactly what you don’t know.

The bootstrap’s trick is to use the best picture of the population you have, which is the sample itself. Imagine your 10 patients copied a million times over into a stand-in population, then draw new samples of 10 from that. Each new sample is called a resample.

The idea comes from Bradley Efron (1979), who named it after the impossible feat of pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps. It seems too good to be true, and it does have limits, but in a wide range of problems the wobble in the bootstrap world closely matches the wobble in the real one.

02

Resample with replacement

Drawing from a population copied a million times is the same as drawing from your sample with replacement: pick a patient at random, note their value, put them back, and pick again, 10 times. Some patients get picked twice or more, and some not at all. That is the point: each resample is a slightly different mix of your data, the way a fresh sample would be a different mix of the population. (Without replacement, every resample would be the same 10 patients in a new order, with the same median.)

03

The bootstrap distribution

The pile of resample medians is the bootstrap distribution. It stands in for the sampling distribution you can’t see, and you read it the same way:

  • Its spread estimates how much the median would wobble from sample to sample: the bootstrap standard error. For the sleep data it is about 0.23 hours.
  • Its middle 95% gives a range of plausible values for the true median: the percentile interval. For the sleep data it runs from about 1.0 to 1.9 hours, 0.9 hours wide.

Draw a range in the opening chart, or skip the guess, and this paragraph will compare it with the bootstrap’s answer.

The histogram of medians is spiky, not a smooth bell. A median of 10 values is the average of the 5th and 6th values, and only a handful of such averages are possible, so the resample medians pile onto the same few numbers. Nearly a third of them land exactly on 1.30. The bootstrap is honest about that lumpiness; a formula assuming a smooth bell would hide it.

What the bootstrap can’t do. It doesn’t make your estimate more accurate, and it doesn’t create information. The resample medians center on your sample’s median, not on the truth. All the bootstrap tells you is how far off your estimate is likely to be. If the sample is biased, as on Who’s Missing?, the bootstrap will measure the wobble around the wrong answer, confidently.

The percentile interval is the simplest of several bootstrap intervals; better-behaved versions exist, and intervals in general get their own page, Confidence intervals, later in the series.

04

It matches the formula

For the median there is no simple formula, which is why the bootstrap is so useful. For the mean there is one: the standard error is s/√n. For the sleep data, s = 1.23 hours and n = 10, so the formula says 0.39 hours. Does the bootstrap agree?

Bootstrap means against the formula

10,000 bootstrap meansbell curve from the formula: mean 1.58, SE 0.39

The two agree closely. The bootstrap comes out a touch smaller, about 0.37, for a known reason: treating the sample as the whole population means dividing by n instead of n − 1 when measuring its spread, which shrinks the answer by a factor of √(9/10). With larger samples the difference vanishes. The histogram is also slightly lopsided, leaning right because of the patient who slept 4.6 hours longer; the formula’s bell can’t show that.

That is the general pattern: where a formula exists, the bootstrap reproduces it, and where none exists (medians, ratios, correlations, percentiles, anything computed by a long procedure), the bootstrap carries on regardless.

05

When it fails

You can only check the bootstrap where the truth is known, so go back to a population you control. Below, one sample is drawn from a known population. The top chart is the real sampling distribution, from 2,000 fresh samples; the bottom one is the bootstrap distribution, from 2,000 resamples of the one sample. If the bootstrap works, the two spreads match, even though the bottom one is centered on the sample’s value instead of the truth.

  • It works for the mean of 20 values from a bell curve. Draw several samples: the bootstrap’s spread usually lands within about 20% of the true one, sometimes above and sometimes below, because it can only be as good as the spread of the sample it starts from. Medians and skewed populations behave similarly once samples are moderately large.
  • Tiny samples: with 3 values there are only 10 different resamples, so the bootstrap distribution is a handful of spikes. Its answer swings wildly from one sample to the next, anywhere from a third of the true spread to nearly double. Three values are a poor picture of any population, and the bootstrap can’t know what it hasn’t seen.
  • The largest value: a resample can never exceed your sample’s largest value, and with 20 values about 64% of resamples contain it, so the bootstrap distribution is one tall spike. The real sampling distribution looks nothing like it. Extremes are the bootstrap’s classic failure.
  • Heavy tails: when rare, enormous values drive the mean, as with the power laws, most samples don’t contain one. The bootstrap only reshuffles what the sample has, so it misses the wild values that make the real mean so unstable. For this population it typically reports less than a fifth of the true spread.
06

Where you will meet it

Medicine and economics

Intervals for medians, ratios and cost-effectiveness figures, where no tidy formula exists, are routinely bootstrapped.

A/B testing

Product metrics such as median session length or the 95th-percentile page load time have no simple standard error; companies bootstrap them.

Machine learning

“Bagging” trains many models on bootstrap resamples and averages them; random forests are built this way. Resampling the test set also gives an error bar for a model’s accuracy.

Evolutionary trees

The support numbers on branches of a family tree of species are bootstrap percentages: how often the branch reappears when the genetic data are resampled (Felsenstein 1985).

Time series

Days in a row aren’t independent, so resampling single days would break the pattern. The block bootstrap resamples whole stretches of days instead.

Complicated pipelines

When an estimate comes out of a long chain of steps, rerunning the whole chain on resamples is often the only practical way to get an error bar.

Check yourself

The bootstrap resamples one group to see how much a number wobbles. Shuffling labels between two groups answers a different question: could the difference between them be chance alone? That is the next page, Shuffling to Test.
