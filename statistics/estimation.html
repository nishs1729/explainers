What Makes a Good Guess

Any recipe that turns data into a number is an estimate of something you can’t see. Some recipes aim true, some hold steady, and the best do both. The way to tell them apart is to try them where the answer is known.

01

Estimators are recipes

A recipe that turns a sample into a guess about something you can’t see directly is called an estimator, and its answer is an estimate. Here are three recipes for the tank count, each with a sensible reason behind it:

  • Largest: the biggest serial number seen. There are at least that many tanks. For the hook: 60.
  • Twice the average: the captured numbers should be spread evenly between 1 and the total, so their average should land about halfway. Double it and subtract 1: 2 × 40.25 − 1 = 79.5.
  • Largest + average gap: four captured numbers leave gaps below 60 that average 60 ÷ 4 = 15 numbers wide. The stretch above the largest is probably about as wide as a typical gap, so add it: 60 + 15 − 1 = 74.

Your own guess is a recipe too. If you said 90 when the largest number was 60, your rule is “largest × 1.5”. One capture can’t show which recipe is best, because any recipe can get lucky once. So build a tank factory where you know the true count, capture tanks from it again and again, and watch where each recipe’s guesses land.

This happened. During the Second World War, Allied economists estimated German tank production from the serial numbers on captured and destroyed tanks and their parts, using recipes like the ones above. 02

Aimed and steady

Each row in the factory is the sampling distribution of one recipe, the subject of Sampling Distributions. Two features of it decide how good the recipe is, and they are different things. Think of a dart player:

  • Where they aim. How far the center of their throws sits from the bullseye. For a recipe, it’s the gap between its average guess and the truth: the bias.
  • How steady the hand is. How widely the throws scatter around their own center. For a recipe, it’s how much the guesses wobble from one sample to the next: the variance. Its square root is the standard error.

The dartboard

throws+ where the player aimscenter of the throws

A steady hand doesn’t fix bad aim, and good aim doesn’t fix a shaky hand. In the factory, Largest is a steady player aiming low, Twice the average aims at the bullseye with a shaky hand, and Largest + average gap does both well. A recipe with no bias, whose guesses are right on average, is called unbiased. Notice that “right on average” says nothing about any single throw.

03

Mean squared error

To choose between a steady player who aims a little off and a shaky one who aims true, you need one number that counts both. The usual choice: square every miss, so big misses count extra, and average the squares. That is the mean squared error (MSE). Its square root is back in the original units: the typical miss. And the MSE splits exactly into the two parts from the dartboard:

mean squared error = bias² + variance

The readout under the dartboard was already doing this sum. Here it is for the tank recipes, from every capture you have made in the factory so far:

Where each recipe’s error comes from

bias²: aiming offvariance: shaky hand

With 4 tanks captured out of 100, Largest guesses about 19 too low on average, yet its typical miss, about 25 tanks, beats the unbiased Twice the average, about 28. A little bias bought a lot of steadiness. Largest + average gap beats both, with a typical miss of about 20, and for this problem no unbiased recipe can do better.

So bias isn’t automatically bad. The sample variance divides by n − 1 to remove its bias; for bell-shaped data, dividing by n + 1 instead gives a slightly biased estimate with a smaller mean squared error. Deliberately trading a little bias for a lot of steadiness is the idea behind shrinkage, later in the series.

04

Robustness

Recipes also differ in how they react to bad data. Take the sleep trial from the first page: the extra hours of sleep each of 10 patients got on drug 2 compared with drug 1. Three recipes for its center: the mean; the median, the middle value; and the trimmed mean, which drops the 2 highest and 2 lowest values and averages the rest.

Three recipes, one dataset

one patient (drag me)meanmediantrimmed mean

Drag any patient, or make the typo. A single wild value pulls the mean as far as it likes. The median doesn’t care how far out the value is, only which side of the middle it falls on. The trimmed mean sits between the two. An estimator that a few bad values can’t push around is called robust.

Robustness isn’t free, though. Race the three recipes on 2,000 samples of 10, drawn from a bell curve where a chosen share of the values are wild:

With clean, bell-shaped data the mean is the steadiest of the three, and the median wobbles noticeably more. Add even 5% wild values and the order flips: the mean’s spread balloons while the median and trimmed mean hardly notice. The trimmed mean is the usual compromise: nearly as steady as the mean on clean data, nearly as safe as the median on dirty data.

05

Getting there as n grows

One more question to ask of a recipe: does it get better with more data? A recipe whose guesses close in on the truth as the sample grows is called consistent. Here are the tank recipes in a factory of 1,000 tanks, plus a fourth, lazy one: twice the first tank’s number, minus 1, ignoring every other tank captured.

Typical miss against the number of tanks captured

Largest is biased but consistent: as more tanks are captured, the largest number creeps up toward the true total and the bias melts away. Twice the first tank is unbiased, right on average, yet it never improves, because it throws away everything but one tank. Unbiased is not the same as good. And Largest + average gap improves fastest of all: its typical miss shrinks roughly in proportion to 1/k, faster than the 1/√k of averages.

06

Where you will meet it

Counting from serial numbers

Wartime tank production, and anything else numbered in sequence: the same recipe estimates how many of something exist from the numbers on the few you have seen.

House prices

Reports quote the median price, not the mean, because a few mansions would drag the mean up. That is robustness at work.

Sensors and images

Median filters clean up noisy sensor readings and speckled photos: a single wild pixel or reading can’t move a median.

Machine learning

The “bias–variance tradeoff”: a simple model aims off but holds steady, a flexible one aims true but wobbles with every new dataset. Overfitting, later in the series, is variance winning.

Sports statistics

Early-season batting averages predict the rest of the season better when pulled toward the league average (Efron and Morris 1975): a little bias, much less variance.

Every formula with n − 1

The sample variance divides by n − 1 to remove a bias. Now you know that unbiased isn’t the only goal, and why other choices are sometimes made.
