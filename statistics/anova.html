A visual introduction

ANOVA

To compare many groups at once, don’t test them two at a time. Compare how far apart the group averages are with how much individuals inside each group vary: signal over noise, in one number called F.

01

Why not ten t-tests

The obvious plan is to compare the fertilizers in pairs with the t-test: A against B, A against C, and so on. Five groups make ten pairs. Each test has a 5% chance of a false alarm when nothing is going on, and ten chances add up. Below, all five groups are pure noise: the same fertilizer five times.

Ten t-tests on pure noise

The ten pairwise t-tests (p-values; red = “significant”)

About 29% of pure-noise experiments produce at least one “significant” pair. That is less than the 40% you would get from ten unrelated tests, because the tests share groups, but it is six times the promised 5%. And it grows fast:

(By simulation, 40,000 experiments each.) The fix is to ask one question first, “do these groups differ at all?”, with one test whose false-alarm rate really is 5%. That test is the analysis of variance, or ANOVA.

02

Between vs within

Look at any panel above. Two kinds of variation are mixed together. Within each group, plots given the same fertilizer still differ: soil, drainage, luck. That is pure noise. Between groups, the averages differ, partly from that same noise and partly, perhaps, from the fertilizers. If the fertilizers do nothing, the averages should be about as spread out as noise alone makes averages of 5 plots.

ANOVA turns that into a ratio. The between-group variance measures how far the group averages sit from the overall average (scaled up by the group size, so it is on the same footing as single plots). The within-group variance is the pooled spread of plots around their own group average. Their ratio is F: signal plus noise over noise. When nothing is going on, F hovers around 1.

Two ways to make F large: pull the averages apart (more signal), or squeeze the plots together (less noise). The same 2-kilogram gap between fertilizers is convincing when plots vary by 1 kg and invisible when they vary by 5. The shuffle builds the chance world as before: deal the 25 yields back out to the five fertilizers at random and recompute F. Your F is judged by where it lands among those.

F = between-group variance ÷ within-group variance. With k groups of n, between = n × Σ(group average − overall average)² ÷ (k − 1), and within = the pooled variance of plots around their group averages.

03

The F distribution

Ronald Fisher joined Rothamsted Experimental Station in 1919, where decades of crop records and new field trials needed analyzing. In 1923 he and W. A. Mackenzie published the first analysis of variance in its modern form, on how potato varieties responded to different manures, and his 1925 book Statistical Methods for Research Workers spread it to every field that runs experiments. In 1934 George Snedecor tabulated the distribution of the ratio and named it F in Fisher’s honor.

The shortcut works like χ²: if the data are bell-shaped with equal spreads, both variances are scaled chi-square quantities, and their ratio follows the F distribution with two degrees of freedom, k − 1 for the groups and N − k for the plots. Here it is against the shuffle for real data: R’s PlantGrowth experiment (from Dobson, 1983), the dried weight of 10 plants under a control and two treatments.

04

Which groups differ?

A significant F says the averages are not all equal. It doesn’t say which ones differ. Now the pairwise comparisons come back, but they have to pay for being several, or the problem of section 1 returns. Here are the three PlantGrowth pairs:

On its own, treatment 2 vs control looks “significant” at 0.047. But it was one of three comparisons, picked out after seeing which gaps were large. The simplest correction, Bonferroni, multiplies each p-value by the number of comparisons. A sharper one asks how often chance alone produces a gap this large somewhere among the pairs: shuffle the plants, record the largest of the three gaps, and compare each real gap with that. John Tukey’s “honestly significant difference” is the formula version of the same idea.

Only treatment 2 vs treatment 1 survives every correction. Treatment 2 vs control is worth a follow-up experiment, not a conclusion. The shuffle is a little more cautious than Tukey for the smaller gaps, because the real differences between groups are mixed into every shuffle, but both point the same way. The deeper version of this problem, where the comparisons you could have made are just as dangerous as the ones you did, is the next page.

05

When it misleads

The F curve assumes bell-shaped noise with the same spread in every group. Moderate departures from the bell shape matter little, especially with equal group sizes. Unequal spreads matter a lot, especially when the groups also differ in size. In these simulations every group has the same true average, so every “significant” F is a false alarm:

Five groups, plots per groupSpread (SD) per groupFalse alarms (promised 5%)
5, 5, 5, 5, 51, 1, 1, 1, 15%
5, 5, 5, 5, 51, 1, 1, 1, 310%
5, 5, 5, 5, 203, 3, 3, 3, 123%
20, 5, 5, 5, 53, 1, 1, 1, 10.3%

When the small groups are the noisy ones, F raises false alarms nearly five times too often; when the big group is the noisy one, it almost never fires. The shuffle is no cure here, because it too assumes the groups are interchangeable. Welch’s ANOVA, which lets each group keep its own spread, is the usual fix, as Welch’s t-test is for two groups.

  • A significant F is not “all different”. It means at least one average differs. Find which with corrected comparisons.
  • A non-significant F is not “all the same”. Small experiments miss real differences; look at the power and the size of the gaps.
  • Plots must be independent. Neighboring plots share soil. Fisher’s answer was to assign fertilizers to plots at random and to group plots into blocks of similar land, so that soil differences can’t masquerade as fertilizer effects.
  • One F per question. Running ANOVA after ANOVA on different subsets or outcomes brings the many-tests problem straight back.
06

Where you will meet it

Field trials

Varieties, fertilizers and irrigation schemes, laid out in randomized blocks: the setting ANOVA was invented for.

A/B/n tests

Several versions of a page compared on an average such as time on site or order value, before any pairwise comparison.

Psychology

Experiments with several conditions, and with two factors at once (two-way ANOVA), report F and its two degrees of freedom.

Medicine

Dose groups in a trial: does the response depend on the dose at all, before asking which doses differ?

Manufacturing

Do machines, shifts or suppliers produce different average dimensions, or is it all within-batch noise?

Regression

Every regression output has an ANOVA table: the variation the model explains against the variation it leaves, with an F test. A later page.

Check yourself

ANOVA protects one question from the many-tests problem. But the problem is bigger than the tests you run: every choice of which groups, outcomes and subsets to look at is another chance for noise to look like a finding. That is the next page, The garden of forking paths.
