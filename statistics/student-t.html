A visual introduction

Student’s t

When the spread has to be estimated from the data as well, small samples produce extreme results far more often than the bell curve says. Student’s t is the corrected curve, and the shortcut behind the most-used test in science.

A Guinness brewer can only afford a handful of samples. How far can he trust the bell curve?

Take 3 measurements from a bell-shaped population, and measure how far their average is from the true mean in units of its own estimated standard error, s/√3, worked out from the sample’s own spread. The bell curve says a result beyond ±3 happens about 3 times in 1,000. With samples of 3, how often does it really happen?

01

The Guinness story

William Sealy Gosset joined the Guinness brewery in Dublin in 1899, fresh from studying chemistry and mathematics at Oxford. The brewery wanted to choose barley, hops and processes by experiment, and Gosset was handed the numbers. His problem was that experiments in a brewery are expensive: he often had only a few measurements to go on, sometimes as few as two or three.

The statistics of the day, built by Karl Pearson and others for large biological surveys, treated the spread of the data as known. With hundreds of measurements that is harmless: the sample’s spread s is almost exactly the true spread σ. With 3 measurements it is not, and the standard formulas made Gosset’s small experiments look more conclusive than they were.

He worked out the curve, partly by educated guesswork he could not fully prove, checked it by simulation done by hand (writing the measurements of 3,000 criminals on cards, shuffling them, and dealing out 750 samples of 4), and published it in 1908 in Biometrika as “The probable error of a mean”. Guinness, wary of giving away trade secrets, did not let its scientists publish under their own names, so he signed it Student. Among his examples was the sleep trial used throughout this series. Ronald Fisher later proved the result, recast it in the form used today, called it t, and gave it its “degrees of freedom”.

02

Dividing by a wobbly s

On Sampling Distributions, an average was standardized by subtracting the true mean and dividing by its standard error σ/√n. The result follows the bell curve exactly when the data are bell-shaped, whatever n is. But σ is almost never known. In practice you divide by s/√n, using the spread of the same small sample, and s wobbles too.

Below, each sample is n measurements from a population with true mean 100 and spread σ = 10, bell-shaped to start with. Each sample is standardized twice: once with the true σ, once with its own s.

With the bell-shaped population, the left histogram fits the bell curve at every n. The right one has a lower middle and much fatter tails, and the reason is in the bottom chart. With 3 measurements, s comes out below half of σ about 22% of the time, and whenever it does, dividing by it at least doubles the result. Press Mark the small-s samples and those samples turn red in all three charts. On the left they are scattered through the middle; on the right they make up most of the tails, nearly two-thirds of the results beyond ±2 in a long run. An ordinary average divided by an unluckily small s becomes an “extreme” result.

Student worked out the shape of that ratio: a bell-shaped numerator divided by an independently wobbling s, whose spread is described by the chi distribution on the Chi-square page. Fisher later supplied the full proof. Switch on Student’s curve to see it fit.

Now switch the population to skewed: a long tail to the right, still with mean 100 and σ = 10. Student’s curve no longer fits. With samples of 3, about 23% of the results land below −2 and only about 3% above +2, where the curve promises about 9% on each side. Most samples miss the long tail: their values bunch just below 100 with a small s, so they land far below −2. A sample that catches one big value gets a big average and a big s, so it is rarely extreme above. The lopsidedness is still visible at 30 measurements (about 7% below −2 against the curve’s 3%). Student’s curve is exact only for bell-shaped data. With small, skewed samples, use a shuffle or the bootstrap instead.

03

Degrees of freedom

The more measurements, the steadier s is, and the closer the t curve comes to the bell. Its shape is set by one number, the degrees of freedom: for a single sample, n − 1. One is lost because s measures spread around the sample’s own average rather than the true mean; once the average is fixed, the last deviation is determined by the others, so only n − 1 of them carry information about spread.

With 3 measurements, an honest 95% interval needs 4.30 standard errors, not 1.96; the bell-curve version would catch the truth only 81% of the time. By 30 the difference is small, which is where the old rule “use the normal above 30” comes from.

04

A shortcut to the shuffle

On Shuffling to Test, the sleep trial was tested by flipping the sign of each patient’s difference in all 1,024 possible ways. Here are those 1,024 flips again, each turned into a t statistic (average ÷ (s/√10)) so they can sit on the same scale as Student’s curve. The t-test is the claim that the curve, with 9 degrees of freedom, is a good stand-in for the flips.

The lower chart shows the tails, where the answer lives. For the paired differences, the flips give p = 4 in 1,024 = 0.39%, and Student’s formula gives 0.28%: two routes to the same answer. The bell curve, used with s, would give 0.005% (see the p-values page for what these numbers mean), claiming 80 times more certainty than 10 patients can provide. That is the mistake Student’s curve exists to prevent. For drug 1 alone, the data are consistent with chance alone, and all three agree on that. As on Power, that is not evidence that drug 1 does nothing: its 95% t interval runs from −0.53 to +2.03 extra hours.

This is the pattern for the rest of the series: every classical test is a formula that approximates a shuffle, and it can be checked against one.

t-tests and t intervals

The same curve answers several everyday questions. In each, the statistic is an estimate divided by its estimated standard error.

Ten patients, two drugs

The two-sample line is a warning. The same 20 numbers give p = 0.003 when analyzed as pairs and p = 0.08 when analyzed as two separate groups. Pairing matters because some patients sleep much more than others whichever drug they take: on drug 1 alone they range from −1.6 to +3.7 hours. Yet 9 of the 10 lines go up and none goes down. Comparing each patient with themselves removes the variation between patients; seen as two separate groups, the same 20 numbers overlap heavily. Choose the test that matches how the data were collected, never the one that gives the smaller p.

When to prefer the shuffle

  • Small, skewed or outlier-prone data. The t curve assumes bell-shaped data. Thanks to the central limit theorem it is forgiving for averages of moderate samples, but with a handful of lopsided measurements a sign flip or permutation test is safer.
  • Statistics other than the mean. Medians, trimmed means and ratios have no t curve; shuffling and the bootstrap still work.
  • When you can. With a computer the exact answer is often cheap. The t-test remains the quick, standard summary, and when the two agree, as they do here, you can report either with confidence.
06

Where you will meet it

Medicine

Before-and-after measurements on the same patients, such as blood pressure or sleep, are compared with a paired t-test.

A/B tests on averages

Average order value or time on a page, rather than click rates, is compared with a two-sample t-test, usually Welch’s.

Lab science

Experiments with 3 to 6 replicates are everywhere in biology. That is exactly Gosset’s situation, and why the multiplier is 2.6 to 4.3, not 2.

Regression

Each slope in a regression table comes with a t statistic and a p-value from Student’s curve: a later page.

Manufacturing

Gosset’s own field: is this batch’s average on target? A handful of measurements and a t interval answer it.

Error bars

A 95% error bar from 3 replicates should be about 4.3 standard errors long on each side. Bars drawn at ±2 standard errors overstate the precision.

Check yourself

The t-test compares averages. Many questions are about counts instead: how many of each kind, against how many you’d expect. Adding up those gaps is the next page, Chi-square tests.
