A visual introduction

p-values

A p-value says how surprising your data would be if chance alone were at work. It is not the probability that chance is at work, and that one-word swap is behind a century of misreadings.

A drug trial reports p = 0.03. Is there a 97% chance the drug works?

Warm up first. Suppose a drug does nothing at all, and 1,000 separate trials test it. Each trial gets its own p-value. What does the histogram of those 1,000 p-values look like?

01

The fraction beyond the line

On Shuffling to Test, every test ended the same way: build the world where nothing is going on, and count how much of it is at least as extreme as the real data. That fraction is the p-value.

Here is the button test once more. The bars show every gap that shuffling the 2,000 visitors can produce, worked out exactly instead of by sampling. The real gap, +1.4 points, is the line. Drag it to see how the p-value depends on where the data lands.

Reading a p-value off the chance histogram

gaps chance can produceat least as extreme as the line: the p-value

The real gap gives p = 0.21: chance alone produces a gap this big, in one direction or the other, about 21% of the time. Drag the line out to 3 points and the shaded area all but vanishes. A small p-value means the data sit far out in a tail of the chance world; a large one means they sit in its crowded middle. (A common formula for comparing two percentages gives 0.18 for the button test. It approximates the shuffle, and the two tell the same story.)

Whether to count both tails is decided before seeing the data. If any difference would have been interesting, count both; that is the usual choice, and it doubles a one-sided p-value.

By a convention going back to Ronald Fisher in the 1920s, p below 0.05 is called statistically significant. Fisher meant it as a rough signal that a result was worth a closer look, not as a verdict.

02

What it is not

A p-value answers one narrow question: if chance alone were at work, how often would data look at least this extreme? It is very easy to read it as the answer to a different, more interesting question. Here are six sentences about a trial that reported p = 0.03. Tap the ones you think are wrong, then check.

The misreading checker

The central mistake flips the question around. The p-value is the chance of data like this given that chance alone is at work. The probability that chance alone is at work, given data like this, is a different number. It depends on something the p-value knows nothing about: how plausible the drug was before the trial. For example, if only 1 in 10 drugs tested actually works, and trials catch 80% of the ones that do, then about 36% of drugs that reach p < 0.05 are duds. Working that out properly is the job of Bayes’ theorem, later in the series.

The statisticians’ own warning. In 2016 the American Statistical Association published a statement on p-values (Wasserstein and Lazar 2016), its first on a specific statistical practice. Among its principles: p-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone; a p-value does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result; and decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.

03

Under chance alone, p is flat

The opening experiment is the key to everything else on this page, so here it is with dials. Each experiment compares two groups of patients; the dial sets how much the treatment truly helps, measured in standard deviations of the outcome. Every experiment’s p-value drops into the histogram.

The p-value rain

experimentsp < 0.05, “significant”level if p-values were flat

With the effect at zero, the histogram is flat. Any p-value between 0 and 1 is as likely as any other, so 5% of experiments land below 0.05, 10% below 0.10, and so on. That isn’t a coincidence; it is what a p-value means. “p = 0.03” says the data are as extreme as the most extreme 3% of chance worlds, and when chance is all there is, 3% of experiments are exactly that extreme.

So when a drug does nothing, 1 trial in 20 still comes out “significant”. Run 20 useless trials, or test 20 useless drugs, or look at 20 outcomes in one trial, and you should expect one success that means nothing. That is the engine behind the garden of forking paths, later in the series.

04

With a real effect, p piles up near 0

Now turn the effect up in the rain above. As soon as the treatment truly helps, small p-values become more common than large ones, and the histogram tilts toward 0. The bigger the effect, the steeper the pile. More patients per group do the same thing for a given effect: a bigger sample makes a real effect harder for chance to hide.

The share of experiments that land below 0.05 when there is an effect is called the power of the experiment. With a modest effect of 0.2 standard deviations, 50 patients per group catch it only about 17% of the time; 500 per group catch it about 89% of the time. Most of the time, a small study of a small effect comes back “not significant”, not because the effect isn’t there but because the study couldn’t see it. Planning for that is the subject of Power and sample size.

Notice what never happens: even with a large effect, some experiments still land well above 0.05. One non-significant result is not evidence that nothing is going on. The button test’s p = 0.21 doesn’t show that orange is no better. It shows that 1,000 visitors per button couldn’t tell.

05

Small p, tiny effect

The flip side: with enough data, a difference too small to matter becomes “highly significant”. Suppose the orange button really is better, by 0.1 percentage points: 5.1% against 5.0%. Slide the number of visitors.

One tiny effect, many sample sizes

The difference never changes; it is 0.1 points all the way along. Only the sample size grows. At a few thousand visitors per button the gap is invisible; at a million it is “significant” at p ≈ 0.001; at ten million it is around 10−24, a decimal point followed by 23 zeros. The p-value measures how sure you can be that the difference isn’t zero, which depends as much on the sample size as on the difference. Whether 0.1 points is worth anything is a separate question the p-value can’t answer.

That is why a careful report gives the size of the effect with an interval around it, not just a p-value. The next page, Confidence intervals, is about exactly that.

06

Where you will meet it

Medical research

Almost every trial reports p-values, usually against 0.05. Regulators look at the size of the benefit and its uncertainty too, not the p-value alone.

Particle physics

Physicists demand “5 sigma”, a one-sided p-value of about 3 in 10 million, before claiming a discovery. The Higgs boson was announced in 2012 at that level.

Genetics

Studies that scan a million genetic variants use a threshold of 5 × 10⁻⁸, because at p < 0.05 a million tests would turn up 50,000 flukes.

A/B testing

Dashboards show p-values that update as data arrives. Stopping the moment p dips below 0.05 inflates the false-alarm rate far above 5%.

The replication crisis

When 100 published psychology studies were repeated (Open Science Collaboration 2015), 97% of the originals had p < 0.05 but only about 36% of the repeats did.

News headlines

“Scientists find link between X and Y” often rests on one p-value just under 0.05. Now you know what it does and doesn’t say.

Check yourself

A p-value squeezes an experiment into one number about one question. A confidence interval keeps much more: how big the effect might be, and how sure you can be. That is the next page, Confidence Intervals.
