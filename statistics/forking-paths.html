A visual introduction

The garden of forking paths

A p-value promises that noise looks this impressive only 1 time in 20. The promise holds for one test, planned in advance. Run enough tests, or analyze one dataset enough ways, and pure noise will always hand you a “discovery”.

“According to their birth dates, people were nearly a year-and-a-half younger after listening to ‘When I’m Sixty-Four’ (adjusted M = 20.1 years) rather than to ‘Kalimba’ (adjusted M = 21.5 years), F(1, 17) = 4.92, p = .040.”Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, Psychological Science, 2011

Listening to a Beatles song can’t change your birth date. The authors produced this result on purpose, from real data, to show how easy it is. Start with the bluntest way: if every study you run is pure noise, how many studies until one gives p < 0.05?

Make your guess, then run the studies.

01

20 tests, one “discovery”

Nobody needs 20 separate studies. One study that measures 20 things will do: 20 foods checked against heart disease, 20 genes against a trait, 20 page designs against sign-ups. If none of them matters, each test still has a 5% chance of a false alarm, and the chance that at least one of the 20 fires is 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 64%. Report that one and stay quiet about the other 19, and you have a headline.

The two corrections answer different worries. Bonferroni divides the threshold by the number of tests (0.05 / 20 = 0.0025), so the chance of even one false alarm in the whole batch stays at 5%. It is safe and blunt: with 5 real effects it finds only about half of them. The false discovery rate method of Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) sorts the p-values and compares the smallest with 0.05 × 1/20, the next with 0.05 × 2/20, and so on, the rising staircase in the chart. It promises instead that, on average, no more than 5% of the things you call discoveries are false. It finds more of the real effects (about 3.3 of 5 against 2.5) and is the standard when thousands of genes or variants are tested at once.

02

The forking paths

Multiple testing is easy to spot when someone runs 20 tests. Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken (2013) pointed out that it happens just as surely when a researcher runs one test, but chose it after seeing the data. Which outcome to analyze, which participants count as outliers, whether to adjust for age, whether to look at men and women separately, when to stop collecting: each choice is a fork. Each one is defensible on its own, and taken in light of the data, the path that leads to p < 0.05 looks like the obvious one in hindsight. They called it the garden of forking paths, after a Borges story. No dishonesty is required.

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn measured the damage by simulation: two groups of 20, pure noise, and a researcher allowed a few common choices.

Four ordinary choices, and a false finding becomes more likely than not. The most innocent-looking fork is peeking: checking the p-value as data come in and stopping once it dips below 0.05. Here is one researcher who starts with 10 per group and tests after every new pair, up to 50.

About 22% of noise studies “work” this way (the paper found 22.1%), even though only 5% are significant at the end. A p-value wanders as data arrive, and stopping at a low point locks in the luck. Peeking is fine if it is planned: sequential designs used in clinical trials and some A/B testing tools set stricter thresholds for each look in advance.

03

The p-hacking sandbox

Your turn. This study is modeled on the Beatles one. Twenty students per song listened to “When I’m Sixty-Four”, “Kalimba” (the control) or “Hot Potato”, then gave their birth date, how old they feel, their father’s age and their gender. Nothing here can make anyone younger: every number is noise. Your job is to find p < 0.05 anyway. Every switch is a choice a real researcher could defend.

★ The p-hacking sandbox

The sandbox has 6 forks and 486 paths. On a typical dataset of pure noise a few dozen of them are “significant”, and in about 9 studies out of 10 at least one is. You only need to find one. Then press Publish: the same path, run on fresh data from the same noise, is significant only rarely. The path was fitted to the accidents of one dataset, and accidents don’t replicate.

One fork is worse than the others even when taken alone. Dropping “outliers” group by group, relative to each group’s own spread, makes both groups look tidier than they are, and the t-test then overstates the evidence. In this sandbox a single, pre-planned analysis that drops values beyond 2 SD is significant about 10% of the time on pure noise (beyond 2.5 SD, about 6.5%). Removing outliers should be decided in advance, for a reason other than the outcome, and reported both ways.

The real paper did all of this. Study 2 measured many more things than it reported, checked the result after about every 10 participants, quietly dropped the third song, and adjusted for father’s age. Without that adjustment, the “effect” had p = .33.

04

The fixes

None of the fixes is a cleverer formula. Each one restores the condition the p-value needs: that the test was chosen before the data could influence it, or that every test run is counted.

FixWhat it does
Pre-registrationWrite down the outcome, sample size, exclusions and analysis before collecting data, with a timestamp. The garden collapses to one path. Registered reports go further: the journal accepts the study on its plan, before the results exist.
CorrectionsWhen many tests are planned, count them: Bonferroni to protect against any false alarm, the false discovery rate to keep the share of false discoveries low.
ReplicationRun the same analysis on fresh data, as the sandbox’s Publish button does. When 100 psychology studies were repeated in 2015, 97% of the originals had been significant and 36% of the repeats were.
DisclosureReport every variable, condition and exclusion, and the results with and without covariates, so readers can see the garden. Simmons and colleagues’ six requirements for authors are listed below.
Explore, then confirmExploring is fine; it is how ideas are found. Split the data, explore one half and test the chosen path once on the other, or call the result a hypothesis rather than a finding.

The six requirements from the 2011 paper are short enough to quote: decide the rule for ending data collection in advance and report it; collect at least 20 observations per cell; list all variables collected; report all conditions, including failed manipulations; if observations are dropped, also report the results with them; if a covariate is used, also report the results without it. The Beatles study, reported this way, would have been “all but impossible to publish”, which was the point.

05

Where you will meet it

Genomics

Scans test hundreds of thousands of genetic variants at once, so they use far stricter thresholds or false-discovery-rate control as a matter of course.

Nutrition headlines

Food questionnaires × dozens of health outcomes × subgroups is a vast garden. A single surprising link from such data is a hypothesis.

A/B testing

Watching a dashboard and stopping when the test “wins” is peeking. Fixed sample sizes or sequential methods keep the 5% honest.

Clinical trials

Trials register their primary outcome in advance; switching to a secondary outcome that happened to work is a known warning sign.

Finance

Backtest enough trading rules on past prices and one will look brilliant. It was fitted to the accidents of the past.

Your own analyses

Notice the forks you take after seeing the data, and count them, or test the chosen path on data you haven’t looked at.

Check yourself

The cure for chance findings is to plan the analysis before the data arrive, and to let chance, not the researcher, decide who gets the treatment. The largest experiment of the twentieth century did both: the next page, 📖 The 1954 polio vaccine trial.
