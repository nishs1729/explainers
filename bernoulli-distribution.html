01

One try, two outcomes

Look at these questions:

  • Will this coin land heads?
  • Will a basketball player make this free throw?
  • Is the next email in your inbox spam?
  • Will a visitor click the “Buy” button?
  • Will a die show a six?

They are about different things, but they have the same structure:

  1. You do something once. This is called a trial.
  2. The trial ends in exactly one of two ways: yes or no.
  3. You can’t know in advance which way it will go, though one answer may be more likely than the other.

A random experiment like this is called a Bernoulli trial. It is named after the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli (1655–1705). His book Ars Conjectandi (“The Art of Conjecturing”), published in 1713 after his death, studied repeated yes/no experiments like these.

The die example shows that the experiment itself doesn’t have to have only two outcomes. A die has six faces. If the only question you ask is “six or not six?”, though, you get a Bernoulli trial.

02

Turning outcomes into numbers

Mathematics works with numbers, not with words like “heads” or “spam”. So the first step is to agree on a rule that turns each outcome into a number. For a yes/no experiment, the natural choice is:

$$X = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the outcome is a “success”} \\ 0 & \text{if the outcome is a “failure”} \end{cases}$$

A rule that assigns a number to each outcome of a random experiment is called a random variable. By convention it is written with a capital letter, here \(X\). A specific value it takes is written in lowercase, \(x\).

“Success” doesn’t have to mean something good. It only labels the outcome you are counting. For a spam filter, spam is the success.

Every scenario produces the same kind of output: a sequence of 0s and 1s. Once outcomes are turned into numbers, a coin, a basketball shot, and an email filter can all be analyzed with the same mathematics. The only thing that differs between them is how often the 1s show up.

Using 0 and 1 specifically has a big advantage. If you add up the values you get the number of successes, and if you average them you get the fraction of successes. That will matter later.

03

What is a distribution?

A random variable can take several values. A probability distribution answers one question: how likely is each of those values?

Think of probability as a fixed amount of sand, exactly 1 unit, that has to be poured into buckets. There is one bucket for each value the variable can take. A distribution is a particular way of splitting the sand. Two rules always apply:

  • No bucket can hold a negative amount: every probability is \(\ge 0\).
  • All the sand gets used: the probabilities add up to exactly \(1\).

A Bernoulli variable has only two buckets, \(X=0\) and \(X=1\). If a fraction \(p\) of the sand goes into bucket 1, the remaining \(1-p\) must go into bucket 0. So a single number \(p\) fully describes the distribution. That number is called the parameter.

$$\begin{gathered} P(X=1) = p, \quad P(X=0) = 1-p, \\ 0 \le p \le 1 \end{gathered}$$

We write this as \(X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\), read as “\(X\) follows a Bernoulli distribution with parameter \(p\).” A fair coin is \(\text{Bernoulli}(0.5)\). The free-throw shooter is \(\text{Bernoulli}(0.7)\). “Roll a six” is \(\text{Bernoulli}(1/6)\).

Move the slider all the way to 0 or 1. Then one bucket holds everything and the outcome is certain. These are the degenerate cases: technically still Bernoulli distributions, but with no randomness left.

04

One formula for both cases

The function that gives the probability of each value is called the probability mass function, or PMF. For a Bernoulli variable you could just list the two cases, as above. There is also a compact single formula:

$$P(X = x) \;=\; p^{\,x}\,(1-p)^{\,1-x}, \qquad x \in \{0, 1\}$$

At first glance this looks unnecessarily complicated, but it relies on one simple fact: any number raised to the power 0 equals 1. The exponent works like an on/off switch. Choose a value of \(x\) to see what happens:

Why use this form? When we look at many independent trials together, their probabilities get multiplied, and with this notation the exponents simply add up. For example, the probability of the specific sequence \(1, 0, 1\) is

$$\begin{aligned} &p^1(1-p)^0 \cdot p^0(1-p)^1 \cdot p^1(1-p)^0 \\ &\quad= p^{1+0+1}\,(1-p)^{0+1+0} \;=\; p^2(1-p)^1. \end{aligned}$$

Only the number of 1s and the number of 0s matter, not the order they came in. We will use this in sections 8 and 9.

05

Let the computer flip

What does “\(P(X=1) = 0.3\)” actually mean? You can’t check it with one trial, because one trial gives you a 0 or a 1, never 0.3. The frequentist interpretation is this: if you repeat the trial many times, the fraction of 1s settles down near 0.3.

Below is a coin with an adjustable bias. Flip it and watch the running fraction of 1s:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat p_n &= \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n} \\ &= \frac{\text{number of 1s}}{n} \end{aligned}$$

The hat on \(\hat p\) means “estimated from data”, as opposed to the true but unknown \(p\).

  • The shaded funnel narrows like \(1/\sqrt{n}\). To make the typical error half as big, you need four times as many flips. We will see where the \(\sqrt{p(1-p)}\) comes from in section 7.
06

Expected value: the balance point

The expected value (or mean) of a random variable is a weighted average: multiply each possible value by its probability, then add the results.

$$\begin{aligned} E[X] &= \sum_x x \cdot P(X=x) \\ &= 0\cdot(1-p) + 1\cdot p = p \end{aligned}$$

So the mean of a Bernoulli variable is just \(p\). There is a nice physical way to picture this. Put a weight of size \(1-p\) at position 0 and a weight of size \(p\) at position 1 on a seesaw. The mean is the point where the seesaw balances, also known as the center of mass.

Find the balance point

Two things are worth noticing:

  • The mean is usually not a value \(X\) can take. If \(p = 0.3\), then \(E[X] = 0.3\), but \(X\) is always 0 or 1. The expected value is a long-run average, not a prediction for a single trial.
  • The average of the \(X\)s equals the fraction of 1s. This is the payoff from encoding outcomes as 0 and 1. The running fraction in the laboratory above was really a running average of \(X\), and the Law of Large Numbers says that average approaches \(E[X] = p\).
07

Variance: how unpredictable?

The mean tells you where the balance point is. The variance tells you how spread out the values are around it. It is defined as the expected squared distance from the mean:

$$\begin{gathered} \operatorname{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big], \\ \text{where } \mu = E[X] = p \end{gathered}$$

A Bernoulli variable has only two values, so there are only two distances to consider. The value 0 is at distance \(p\) from the mean, and the value 1 is at distance \(1-p\). Square each distance and weight it by its probability:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &= \underbrace{(1-p)}_{P(X=0)}\,(0-p)^2 \\ &\quad+ \underbrace{p}_{P(X=1)}\,(1-p)^2 \\ &= p(1-p)\big[\,p + (1-p)\,\big] \\ &= p(1-p) \end{aligned}$$

In the left picture below, each squared distance is drawn as an actual square. The variance is the probability-weighted average of the two square areas.

Squared deviations

A shortcut

Variance can also be computed as \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2\). For a Bernoulli variable, \(0^2 = 0\) and \(1^2 = 1\), so \(X^2 = X\). That means \(E[X^2] = E[X] = p\), and

$$\begin{gathered} \operatorname{Var}(X) = p - p^2 = p(1-p), \\ \sigma = \sqrt{p(1-p)} \end{gathered}$$

Here \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, the square root of the variance. It is measured in the same units as \(X\).

When is it largest?

Look at the curve on the right. Setting the derivative to zero gives \(\frac{d}{dp}(p - p^2) = 1 - 2p = 0\), so the maximum is at \(p = \tfrac12\), where \(\operatorname{Var} = \tfrac14\) and \(\sigma = \tfrac12\).

A fair coin is the hardest Bernoulli trial to predict. As \(p\) moves toward 0 or 1, one outcome becomes nearly certain and the variance shrinks toward 0. This is also where the funnel in section 5 comes from: the average of \(n\) independent trials has variance \(p(1-p)/n\), so its standard deviation is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\).

08

Stacking Bernoullis

Now run \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials with the same \(p\) and add up the results:

$$\begin{aligned} Y &= X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n \\ &= \text{number of successes} \end{aligned}$$

The sum \(Y\) is a new random variable that can be anything from \(0\) to \(n\). Its distribution is called the Binomial distribution, \(\text{Binomial}(n, p)\). Run the machine to watch it build up one trial at a time:

observed share of runsBinomial formula

The formula comes directly from the PMF in section 4. Any specific sequence containing \(k\) ones and \(n-k\) zeros has probability \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\), because the exponents add up. There are \(\binom{n}{k}\) such sequences (ways to choose which \(k\) positions are the 1s), so

$$P(Y = k) = \binom{n}{k}\, p^{k}\,(1-p)^{n-k}.$$

The mean and variance follow from the Bernoulli ones. Expected values always add, and variances add when the trials are independent:

$$\begin{aligned} E[Y] &= \sum_{i=1}^n E[X_i] = np, \\ \operatorname{Var}(Y) &= \sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = np(1-p). \end{aligned}$$

Set \(n\) to 30 and run 1000×. The histogram starts to look like a bell curve, which is an early sign of the Central Limit Theorem. The Bernoulli distribution is the single building block underneath all of this.

09

Going backwards: guessing p

So far we have assumed we know \(p\) and predicted the data. In real life it is usually the other way around. You see the data (clicks, cures, defects) and want to work out \(p\).

Below is a coin with a secret bias. Flip it a few times. The curve shows, for every possible \(p\), how well that value explains what you have seen. This is called the likelihood:

$$\begin{aligned} L(p) &= \prod_{i=1}^{n} p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i} \\ &= p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}, \\ &\phantom{=}\;\; \text{where } k = \text{number of 1s} \end{aligned}$$

The mystery coin

The peak of the curve is the maximum likelihood estimate. To find it, take the logarithm (which turns the product into a sum) and set the derivative to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \ell(p) &= k\ln p + (n-k)\ln(1-p), \\ \ell'(p) &= \frac{k}{p} - \frac{n-k}{1-p} = 0 \\ &\Longrightarrow\;\; \hat p = \frac{k}{n}. \end{aligned}$$

The best guess for \(p\) is simply the fraction of 1s you observed, which is what you would probably have guessed anyway. More importantly, the curve gets narrower as you flip more. Its width is roughly \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\), the same quantity that controlled the funnel in section 5.

A trap at the edges. Flip three times and get three 0s. Then \(\hat p = 0\) and \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n} = 0\), which would suggest you know \(p\) exactly after three flips. You don't: the curve above is still wide. The readout therefore uses the Wilson interval, which gives an honest range even when every flip came up the same way.

Where the formulas come from

Mode and median

The mode is the most probable value. You compare \(P(X=1) = p\) with \(P(X=0) = q\), and whichever is bigger wins. At \(p = \tfrac12\) they tie.

The median \(m\) is a value with at least half the probability on each side: \(P(X \le m) \ge \tfrac12\) and \(P(X \ge m) \ge \tfrac12\). If \(q > \tfrac12\), then \(0\) already holds more than half the probability, so \(m = 0\). If \(p > \tfrac12\), \(m = 1\). At exactly \(p = \tfrac12\), every number between 0 and 1 satisfies the definition.

Skewness: which way is it lopsided?

Skewness is the third central moment divided by \(\sigma^3\). The third central moment is

$$\begin{aligned} E[(X-p)^3] &= q(-p)^3 + p(q)^3 = pq\,(q^2 - p^2) \\ &= pq\,(q-p)(q+p) = pq\,(q-p). \end{aligned}$$ $$\gamma_1 = \frac{pq(q-p)}{(pq)^{3/2}} = \frac{q-p}{\sqrt{pq}}.$$

At \(p=\tfrac12\) the distribution is symmetric and \(\gamma_1 = 0\). When \(p\) is small, \(X\) is usually 0 with a rare jump up to 1, which gives a long right tail and positive skew. As \(p \to 0\), the skewness grows without bound.

Kurtosis: how heavy are the tails?

The fourth central moment uses \(p^3 + q^3 = (p+q)^3 - 3pq(p+q) = 1 - 3pq\):

$$\begin{aligned} E[(X-p)^4] &= q\,p^4 + p\,q^4 \\ &= pq\,(p^3 + q^3) = pq\,(1 - 3pq). \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \text{Kurt} &= \frac{pq(1-3pq)}{(pq)^2} = \frac{1-3pq}{pq}, \\ \gamma_2 &= \text{Kurt} - 3 = \frac{1-6pq}{pq}. \end{aligned}$$

At \(p = \tfrac12\), \(\gamma_2 = -2\). That is the smallest excess kurtosis any distribution can have: a fair coin has no tails at all, just two equal spikes. Rare-event Bernoullis (small \(p\)) have very large kurtosis because almost all of their variance comes from one rare outcome.

Moments and the moment generating function

Since \(0^k = 0\) and \(1^k = 1\), we have \(X^k = X\) for every \(k \ge 1\), so every raw moment equals \(p\). The MGF shows the same thing:

$$\begin{aligned} M_X(t) &= E[e^{tX}] = q\,e^{0} + p\,e^{t} = q + pe^t, \\ M_X^{(k)}(t) &= pe^t \;\Rightarrow\; M_X^{(k)}(0) = p. \end{aligned}$$

The MGF of a sum of independent variables is the product of their MGFs. So the sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli(\(p\)) variables has MGF \((q+pe^t)^n\), which is exactly the Binomial MGF.

Entropy: how many bits of surprise?

Entropy measures, in bits, how much information you gain on average when you see the outcome:

$$\begin{gathered} H(p) = -p\log_2 p - q\log_2 q, \\ \text{with } 0\log_2 0 := 0. \end{gathered}$$

It is \(0\) when \(p\) is 0 or 1 (no surprise at all) and reaches its maximum of exactly 1 bit at \(p = \tfrac12\). A fair coin flip is the standard definition of one bit of information. Among all distributions on \(\{0,1\}\), the fair coin is the most uncertain, both by entropy and by variance.

Fisher information and the precision of \(\hat p\)

Fisher information measures how much a single observation tells you about \(p\):

$$I(p) = E\!\left[\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial p}\ln f(X;p)\right)^{2}\right] = \frac{1}{pq}.$$

The estimate \(\hat p = k/n\) has variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat p) = pq/n = 1/(n\,I(p))\). That is the smallest variance any unbiased estimator can achieve (the Cramér–Rao bound), so the simple fraction of 1s is the best possible unbiased estimate.

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. It is a special case of the Binomial. \(\text{Bernoulli}(p) = \text{Binomial}(1, p)\). Going the other way, the sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli(\(p\)) variables is \(\text{Binomial}(n,p)\).
  2. Powers do nothing. \(X^k = X\) for any \(k\ge1\), so \(E[X^k] = p\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X]-E[X]^2\) simplifies to \(p(1-p)\).
  3. Swapping labels gives another Bernoulli. \(1 - X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)\). Calling “tails” the success just swaps \(p\) and \(q\). The variance \(pq\) stays the same and the skewness changes sign.
  4. AND and OR stay Bernoulli. For independent \(X\sim\text{Bern}(p_1)\) and \(Y\sim\text{Bern}(p_2)\), the product \(XY\) (“both happen”) is \(\text{Bern}(p_1p_2)\), and \(\max(X,Y)\) (“at least one happens”) is \(\text{Bern}(1-q_1q_2)\).
  5. Indicators link probability and expectation. For any event \(A\), the indicator \(\mathbf 1_A\) (1 if \(A\) happens, 0 if not) is Bernoulli with \(p = P(A)\), so \(E[\mathbf 1_A] = P(A)\). This is a very common trick: write a count as a sum of indicators, then use linearity of expectation.
  6. Waiting for a success gives the Geometric distribution. If you repeat Bernoulli(\(p\)) trials until the first 1, the number of trials needed follows a Geometric distribution with \(P(N=k) = q^{k-1}p\) and mean \(1/p\). With \(p = 1/6\), you wait 6 rolls on average for a six.
  7. The fraction of 1s is the best estimate. \(\hat p = k/n\) is the maximum-likelihood estimate. It is unbiased (\(E[\hat p] = p\)), has variance \(pq/n\), and reaches the Cramér–Rao bound.
  8. Maximum uncertainty is at \(p = \tfrac12\). Variance peaks at \(\tfrac14\), entropy peaks at 1 bit, skewness is 0, and excess kurtosis reaches its smallest possible value of −2.
  9. The edge cases are degenerate. At \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), \(X\) is a constant and the variance is 0. Skewness, kurtosis and Fisher information are then undefined or infinite, because there is no spread to divide by.
  10. It has a conjugate prior (Bayesian view). If your uncertainty about \(p\) is described by a \(\text{Beta}(a,b)\) distribution, then after seeing \(k\) ones in \(n\) trials it becomes \(\text{Beta}(a+k,\; b+n-k)\). Each 1 adds to \(a\) and each 0 adds to \(b\).
