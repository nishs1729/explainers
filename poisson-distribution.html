The Poisson Distribution

How many? Raindrops on a paving stone, typos on a page, calls in a minute, stars in a patch of sky. When events are rare, independent and spread at a steady rate, the count in any patch follows the Poisson distribution.

01

Counting rare events

The binomial distribution counts successes out of a known number of trials. Many everyday counts have no such number. How many emails arrive in an hour? How many typos are on this page? How many drops hit one paving stone? There is no “out of \(n\)”, and no ceiling: the count could be 0, 1, 2 or, in principle, anything.

What these counts share is a recipe: a huge number of chances for an event, each one tiny, all independent, at a steady average rate. Only one number survives from all that detail, the average count \(\lambda\) (lambda). The count \(X\) then has the Poisson distribution, written \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\), named after Siméon Denis Poisson, who described it in 1837.

The earlier tutorials reached it from two directions:

  • From coin flips. Split the hour into \(n\) tiny moments, each holding an email with probability \(p = \lambda/n\). The count is \(\text{Binomial}(n, \lambda/n)\), and as \(n \to \infty\) it becomes \(\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\). The Poisson limit section of the Binomial tutorial shows the bars converge.
  • From waiting times. If the gaps between events are independent \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) waits, the number of events in a window of length \(t\) is \(\text{Poisson}(\lambda t)\). The Poisson process section of the Exponential tutorial builds this from simulated timelines.

This page is about the count itself: its shape, why its mean equals its variance, how to tell genuine Poisson data from impostors, and how to estimate \(\lambda\).

The checklist. A count is Poisson when (1) events happen one at a time, (2) counts in separate patches of time or space are independent, and (3) the average rate is the same everywhere. When a real count breaks one of these, it usually shows up as a variance that is too big or too small (sections 3 and 9).

02

The formula, bar by bar

The probability of exactly \(k\) events is

$$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Read it as a recipe. Start with \(P(X = 0) = e^{-\lambda}\), the chance of no events at all. Then each step to the next bar multiplies by \(\lambda\) and divides by the new count:

$$P(X = k) = P(X = k-1)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{k}$$

While \(k < \lambda\) the factor \(\lambda/k\) is bigger than 1 and the bars climb. Once \(k > \lambda\) it is smaller than 1 and they fall. So the tallest bar, the mode, is at \(\lfloor\lambda\rfloor\), the whole-number part of \(\lambda\). When \(\lambda\) is a whole number the factor at \(k = \lambda\) is exactly 1, and there are two equal tallest bars, at \(\lambda - 1\) and \(\lambda\). The bars add up to 1 because \(\sum_k \lambda^k/k! = e^\lambda\).

Small \(\lambda\) piles the probability against the wall at 0, with a long tail to the right. As \(\lambda\) grows the bars spread out like \(\sqrt\lambda\), the skew fades, and the shape turns into a bell (section 7). At \(\lambda = 100\) the bars are near-symmetric, and \(P(X = 0) = e^{-100}\) is about \(4 \times 10^{-44}\).

03

Mean equals variance

The average count is \(\lambda\), as it should be. The \(k = 0\) term vanishes, and pulling out one \(\lambda\) leaves the PMF again:

$$\begin{aligned} E[X] &= \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k\,\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!} \\ &= \lambda\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k-1}e^{-\lambda}}{(k-1)!} = \lambda \end{aligned}$$

The same trick twice gives \(E[X(X-1)] = \lambda^2\), and so

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[X(X-1)] + E[X] - E[X]^2 \\ &= \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 = \lambda \end{aligned}$$

The mean and the variance are the same number. The standard deviation is \(\sqrt\lambda\), so the relative spread is \(1/\sqrt\lambda\): counts around 100 typically land within 10% of it, counts around 4 within 50%.

That gives a quick test for whether data could be Poisson. Compute the dispersion index \(D = s^2/\bar x\), the sample variance over the sample mean. Poisson data give \(D \approx 1\). \(D > 1\) is overdispersion: events come in clumps, or the rate varies from place to place. \(D < 1\) is underdispersion: events keep apart, as when there is a fixed number of slots to fill.

Dispersion check

share of counts equal to \(k\)Poisson with the same mean

All three sources have the same mean. The clumpy counts first draw a rate of their own from a gamma distribution with mean \(\lambda\), then a Poisson count at that rate, so their variance is \(\lambda + \lambda^2/2\). The regular counts fill a fixed number of slots, \(m = \lceil 1.5\lambda\rceil\), each with probability \(\lambda/m\). That is a binomial, whose variance \(\lambda(1 - \lambda/m)\) is below its mean. Only the Poisson bars sit on the dots, and only the Poisson’s \(D\) stays near 1. For genuine Poisson data \(D\) wobbles by about \(\sqrt{2/(n-1)}\), so the readout flags values more than twice that from 1.

04

Horse kicks and flying bombs

In 1898 Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz published a small book called The Law of Small Numbers. Its most famous table counts soldiers of the Prussian cavalry killed by horse kicks: 10 army corps watched for 20 years, 1875 to 1894, or 200 “corps-years”. There were 122 deaths, an average of \(\hat\lambda = 0.61\) per corps-year. Every item on the checklist fits: many soldiers, each with a tiny chance of a fatal kick, mostly independent of each other.

Half a century later R. D. Clarke asked a sharper question. In 1944 London was hit by V-1 flying bombs. Were they aimed at particular districts, or falling at random? Clarke divided 144 km² of south London into 576 squares of ¼ km² and counted 537 hits. If the bombs were aimed, a few squares would take many hits and many squares would be spared, so the counts would be overdispersed.

Observed counts against Poisson

observedexpected under Poisson(\(\hat\lambda\))

Both fit remarkably well. The chi-square statistic adds up \((\text{observed} - \text{expected})^2/\text{expected}\) over the count classes. Classes expecting fewer than 5 are merged first, because the test is unreliable for tiny expected counts. The p-value is the chance of a fit at least this bad if the counts really were Poisson. One degree of freedom is lost for every class after the first, and one more because \(\lambda\) was estimated from the same data.

For the bombs, the counts are just what random scatter would produce. That doesn’t prove the bombs were unaimed. It says that if they were aimed, their guidance was too crude to show at the scale of a quarter of a square kilometer.

05

Random looks clumpy

Look back at the rain at the top of the page. Some squares caught 5 or 6 drops while neighbors stayed dry. Random scatter is clumpy. Our eyes expect randomness to look evenly spread, but evenness is a pattern of its own, and it takes something pushing the points apart.

108 points on 72 squares

share of squares with \(k\) pointsPoisson(1.5)

Hide the squares and ask which picture looks random. Most people pick the evenly spread one. With 1.5 points per square, a truly random scatter leaves about 22% of the squares empty (\(e^{-1.5}\)) and puts 4 or more points in about 1 square in 15. The even scatter has almost no empty squares and \(D\) well below 1. The clustered one has too many empty squares and too many crowded ones, so \(D\) is well above 1.

Clusters on a map. Draw a circle around the densest patch of cancer cases in a country and it will look alarming, even if everyone has the same risk. With thousands of towns, some will have several times the expected count by chance alone. Drawing the circle after seeing the dots is the “Texas sharpshooter” fallacy: firing at a barn, then painting the target around the tightest group of holes. The fair question is not “is this square crowded?” but “are there more crowded squares than the Poisson predicts?”

06

Adding and splitting

Adding. If \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)\) and \(Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)\) are independent, then

$$X + Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$$

Calls to two help lines, merged, are calls to one busier help line. In the coin-flip picture this is obvious: two streams of tiny chances, merged, are one stream with the rates added.

Splitting. Now run it backwards. Emails arrive at \(\lambda\) per hour, and each one is spam with probability \(p\), independently. Then the spam count is \(\text{Poisson}(p\lambda)\) and the real-email count is \(\text{Poisson}((1-p)\lambda)\). This is called thinning. The surprise is that the two counts are independent: a heavy hour for spam tells you nothing about how much real email came in that hour.

The correlation between the two counts hovers around 0, whatever \(p\) is. This independence is special to the Poisson. If exactly 8 emails arrived every hour, one more spam would mean one fewer real email, a perfect negative correlation. A random Poisson total is exactly the amount of extra randomness that cancels it.

Why the split counts are independent

Spam \(= a\) and real \(= b\) means \(a + b\) emails arrived and \(a\) of them were picked as spam. With \(q = 1 - p\),

$$\begin{aligned} P(a, b) &= \frac{\lambda^{a+b}e^{-\lambda}}{(a+b)!}\binom{a+b}{a}p^a q^b \\ &= \frac{(p\lambda)^a e^{-p\lambda}}{a!}\cdot\frac{(q\lambda)^b e^{-q\lambda}}{b!} \end{aligned}$$

The joint probability factors into a \(\text{Poisson}(p\lambda)\) PMF times a \(\text{Poisson}(q\lambda)\) PMF, which is exactly what independence means. Read in reverse, it also says that once you know the total \(a + b = m\), the spam count is \(\text{Binomial}(m, p)\).

07

Large λ: the bell and the square root

A \(\text{Poisson}(100)\) count is the sum of 100 independent \(\text{Poisson}(1)\) counts, by the adding rule. The central limit theorem then says it is close to a normal distribution with the same mean and variance:

$$X \approx N(\lambda,\ \lambda) \quad \text{for large } \lambda$$

Probabilities of whole-number counts are best approximated with a continuity correction: treat the bar at \(k\) as covering \(k - \tfrac12\) to \(k + \tfrac12\), so that \(P(X \le k) \approx \Phi\big((k + \tfrac12 - \lambda)/\sqrt\lambda\big)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF.

Poisson counts have one awkward feature: their spread depends on their size. Big counts wobble more, so a chart of counts has noisy tall bars and calm short ones. Taking square roots fixes this. Near \(\lambda\) the curve \(\sqrt x\) has slope \(1/(2\sqrt\lambda)\), so it shrinks a wobble of size \(\sqrt\lambda\) to about

$$\operatorname{sd}\big(\sqrt X\big) \approx \frac{1}{2\sqrt\lambda}\cdot\sqrt\lambda = \frac12$$

whatever \(\lambda\) is. This is a variance-stabilizing transform. Anscombe’s version, \(\sqrt{X + 3/8}\), settles to \(\tfrac12\) sooner.

The bell, and the square-root trick

\(P(X = k)\), \(k \le\) cut-offabove the cut-off\(N(\lambda, \lambda)\) density
\(\operatorname{sd}(\sqrt X)\)\(\operatorname{sd}(\sqrt{X + 3/8})\)\(\tfrac12\)

The cut-off is \(\lfloor\lambda + \sqrt\lambda\rfloor\), about one standard deviation above the mean. Without the correction the normal answer is off by several percentage points at small \(\lambda\); with it, the error is usually well under one. Meanwhile \(\operatorname{sd}(X) = \sqrt\lambda\) keeps growing but \(\operatorname{sd}(\sqrt X)\) flattens at \(\tfrac12\). That is why photon-count images and gene-expression counts are often analyzed on a square-root (or log) scale.

08

Going backwards: what is λ?

Suppose you count events on \(n\) separate days and get \(x_1, \dots, x_n\), with total \(S\). The best estimate of the daily rate is simply the average:

$$\hat\lambda = \bar x = \frac{S}{n}$$

It is the maximum-likelihood estimate, it is unbiased, and only the total matters: \(S \sim \text{Poisson}(n\lambda)\) by the adding rule, and once you know \(S\), how it was spread over the days tells you nothing more about \(\lambda\).

The quick interval \(\hat\lambda \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat\lambda/n}\) breaks down for small counts; with zero events it is \([0, 0]\). The exact interval (Garwood, 1936) uses the link between counts and waiting times: at least \(k\) events by time \(\lambda\) means the \(k\)-th arrival came before \(\lambda\), so \(P(X \ge k) = P(\text{Gamma}(k, 1) \le \lambda)\). Writing \(G_a^{-1}\) for the quantile function of \(\text{Gamma}(a, 1)\),

$$\begin{gathered} \text{lower} = \tfrac{1}{n}\,G_{S}^{-1}(0.025) \\ \text{upper} = \tfrac{1}{n}\,G_{S+1}^{-1}(0.975) \end{gathered}$$

with lower \(= 0\) when \(S = 0\). Tables often state the same thing with chi-square quantiles, \(\chi^2_{2S}(0.025)/2n\) and \(\chi^2_{2S+2}(0.975)/2n\).

Try a small true rate and a single day. The quick interval often comes out as \([0, 0]\) and covers the truth far less than 95% of the time. The exact interval always covers at least 95%. It is slightly conservative, above 95%, because counts jump in whole steps and it never rounds in the risky direction.

The rule of three. Watch for \(n\) periods and see nothing. How high could the rate still be? The one-sided 95% upper bound solves \(e^{-n\lambda} = 0.05\), so \(\lambda \le -\ln(0.05)/n \approx 3/n\). A drug given to 300 patients without one serious reaction could still cause one in 100. (The two-sided interval in the widget uses 97.5% at each end, giving \(3.69/n\).)

Comparing two rates. Suppose a clinic sees 12 infections in 1,000 patient-days, and another sees 5 in 1,500. Condition on the total of 17. By the splitting rule, if the rates are equal, the first clinic’s share is \(\text{Binomial}(17,\ 0.4)\), with \(0.4 = 1000/2500\). Seeing 12 or more has probability about 0.011. The comparison has become a binomial test.

09

When Poisson fails

Real counts often have a variance well above their mean. The usual cause is that the rate is not the same for everyone. Some drivers have more accidents than others; some web pages get far more visits. Each unit’s count is Poisson given its own rate \(\Lambda\), but the rates differ. The law of total variance then adds the spread of the rates to the Poisson spread:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[\operatorname{Var}(X \mid \Lambda)] \\ &\quad + \operatorname{Var}(E[X \mid \Lambda]) \\ &= \mu + \operatorname{Var}(\Lambda) \end{aligned}$$

where \(\mu = E[\Lambda]\). If the rates follow a gamma distribution with coefficient of variation \(c\) (standard deviation over mean), the counts follow the negative binomial distribution, with variance \(\mu + c^2\mu^2\).

A second failure is too many zeros. Some units cannot have events at all: a shop that was closed, a patient who is immune, a sensor that was off. A zero-inflated Poisson mixes a share \(\pi\) of these structural zeros with ordinary Poisson counts.

Break the Poisson

mixed countPoisson with the same mean

Both failures stretch the variance past the mean, and both put more weight on 0 than a Poisson with the same mean allows. In practice you check \(D\) first. If it is well above 1, a Poisson model will give intervals that are too narrow and effects that look more significant than they are. The usual fixes are a negative binomial or zero-inflated model, or “quasi-Poisson” standard errors scaled up by \(\sqrt D\).

Where the formulas come from

All the factorial moments, from the PGF

The probability generating function is \(G(s) = \sum_k s^k \lambda^k e^{-\lambda}/k! = e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda s} = e^{\lambda(s-1)}\). Differentiating \(r\) times and setting \(s = 1\) gives \(G^{(r)}(1) = \lambda^r\), and that derivative is the \(r\)-th factorial moment:

$$E[X(X-1)\cdots(X-r+1)] = \lambda^r$$

So \(E[X] = \lambda\), \(E[X(X-1)] = \lambda^2\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 = \lambda\). Multiplying PGFs also proves the adding rule: \(e^{\lambda_1(s-1)}e^{\lambda_2(s-1)} = e^{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)(s-1)}\).

Every cumulant is λ

The log of the MGF is \(\lambda(e^t - 1) = \lambda\sum_{r \ge 1} t^r/r!\), so every cumulant equals \(\lambda\): \(\kappa_1 = \kappa_2 = \kappa_3 = \dots = \lambda\). Then

$$\gamma_1 = \frac{\kappa_3}{\kappa_2^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt\lambda}, \qquad \gamma_2 = \frac{\kappa_4}{\kappa_2^{2}} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$$

Both shrink to the normal’s 0 as \(\lambda\) grows, which is the bell of section 7.

Counts and waiting times are two views of one process

Events with independent \(\text{Exp}(1)\) gaps arrive at times \(T_1 < T_2 < \dots\), and \(T_k\) is a sum of \(k\) gaps, so \(T_k \sim \text{Gamma}(k, 1)\). The count by time \(\lambda\) is at least \(k\) exactly when \(T_k \le \lambda\). That identity turns every Poisson tail probability into a gamma one. It is what the exact interval of section 8 uses. See the Exponential tutorial for more on sums of gaps.

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Independent Poissons add. \(\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1) + \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2) = \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\) (section 6).
  2. Thinning gives independent Poissons. Keep each event with probability \(p\): kept \(\sim \text{Poisson}(p\lambda)\), dropped \(\sim \text{Poisson}((1-p)\lambda)\), independent (section 6).
  3. Given the total, the pieces are binomial. If \(X_1, \dots, X_m\) are independent Poissons, then given their sum the counts are multinomial with probabilities proportional to the rates.
  4. The binomial limit. \(\text{Binomial}(n, \lambda/n) \to \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) as \(n \to \infty\) (Binomial tutorial, section 8).
  5. Mixing gives the negative binomial. A Poisson count whose rate is gamma-distributed is negative binomial, the standard model for overdispersed counts (section 9).
  6. Conjugate prior: gamma. If your uncertainty about \(\lambda\) is \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\) (shape \(\alpha\), rate \(\beta\)), then after observing counts \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) it becomes \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum x_i,\; \beta + n)\).
11

Where you will meet it

Call centers and queues

Calls, customers and requests to a server arrive from many independent people. Counts per minute are close to Poisson, and staffing formulas start from that.

Genetics

Mutations scattered along a genome, and the number of sequencing reads covering a given base, are classic Poisson counts. Coverage calculations for sequencing runs use them.

Light and electronics

Photons reach a camera sensor one at a time, at random. The resulting “shot noise” is Poisson, so dim images are noisy in proportion to \(\sqrt{\text{count}}\).

Insurance

The number of claims in a year from a portfolio of policies is modeled as Poisson, or negative binomial when some policyholders are riskier than others.

Football scores

Goals are rare and roughly independent, so each team’s goals in a match are approximately Poisson. Betting models start there.

Quality control

Defects per meter of cable, typos per page, flaws per wafer: when they occur independently at a steady rate, a Poisson count with control limits at \(\lambda \pm 3\sqrt\lambda\) flags unusual batches.

When it fails

Events that trigger each other (aftershocks, retweets, contagion) cluster, and rates that vary between units inflate the variance. Check \(D\) before trusting a Poisson model.
