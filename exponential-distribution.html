The Exponential Distribution

How long until the next one? The next click of a Geiger counter, the next customer, the next request to a web server. When events happen at random at a steady rate, the waiting time follows the exponential distribution.

01

From coin flips to waiting times

The binomial distribution fixes the number of trials and counts the successes. Turn the question around: keep trying until the first success, and ask how long that took.

With separate trials this is easy. Roll a die until the first six. The six arrives on roll \(k\) when the first \(k-1\) rolls fail and roll \(k\) succeeds. With success probability \(p\) and \(q = 1 - p\) as before,

$$P(K = k) = q^{\,k-1}p, \quad k = 1, 2, \dots$$

This is the geometric distribution. On average you need \(1/p\) tries: 6 rolls for a six.

Now let time flow continuously. A Geiger counter clicks at random moments, on average \(\lambda\) times per second. The Greek letter \(\lambda\) (lambda) is the rate. To reuse what we know, chop time into thin slices of width \(\Delta t\). In each slice, flip a coin that comes up “click” with probability \(p = \lambda\Delta t\). Each slice is a Bernoulli trial, and waiting for the first click is waiting for the first success.

The wait \(T\) is longer than \(t\) exactly when the first \(t/\Delta t\) slices all fail:

$$P(T > t) = (1 - \lambda\Delta t)^{t/\Delta t}$$

Let the slices shrink. The right-hand side tends to a limit you may know from compound interest:

$$\lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\,(1 - \lambda\Delta t)^{t/\Delta t} = e^{-\lambda t}$$

That is the whole distribution in one line. A waiting time with \(P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\) has the exponential distribution with rate \(\lambda\), written \(T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)\).

Shrink the slices

first click in this slice (bar area = probability)\(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\)your last run

Each bar is the geometric probability that the first click falls in that slice. Bars are divided by \(\Delta t\), so their area is the probability. With wide slices you get a staircase. As \(\Delta t\) shrinks, the staircase melts into the smooth curve \(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\). The average wait never moves: it is \(\Delta t / p = 1/\lambda\) for every slice width. Shrinking the slices only sharpens the picture.

02

Density, CDF and survival

The curve you just saw is the probability density function (PDF) of the exponential distribution:

$$f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \quad t \ge 0$$

A density is not a probability. Probabilities are areas under it. The chance that the wait lands between \(a\) and \(b\) is the area between them:

$$\begin{aligned} P(a \le T \le b) &= \int_a^b \lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt \\ &= e^{-\lambda a} - e^{-\lambda b} \end{aligned}$$

The curve starts at height \(\lambda\), so when \(\lambda > 1\) the density is above 1 near zero. That is fine. Only the total area must equal 1.

Areas under the density

Drag across the chart to move the nearer end of the shaded interval. Horizontal axis: time \(t\).

Two running totals get their own names. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) says how likely the wait is to be over by time \(t\). The survival function says how likely it is still going:

$$\begin{aligned} F(t) &= P(T \le t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t} \\ S(t) &= P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t} \end{aligned}$$

Set \(S(m) = \tfrac12\) and solve to get the median wait:

$$m = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$$

For radioactive atoms this is the half-life. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years, so its decay rate is \(\lambda = \ln 2 / 5730 \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4}\) per year. After two half-lives a quarter of the atoms remain, and after three an eighth. Every equal stretch of time multiplies the survivors by the same factor \(e^{-\lambda}\) per unit of time. That is why, on a logarithmic scale, the survival curve is a straight line with slope \(-\lambda\). Engineers plot failure data this way to check whether it is exponential.

03

No memory

You have already waited \(s\) minutes for something whose waiting time is exponential. How much longer will you wait? Intuition says it must be “due” by now. The exponential distribution says: exactly as long as if you had just started. The proof takes one line:

$$\begin{aligned} &P(T > s + t \mid T > s) \\ &\quad= \frac{P(T > s + t)}{P(T > s)} = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} \\ &\quad= e^{-\lambda t} = P(T > t) \end{aligned}$$

Given that nothing has happened by time \(s\), the remaining wait \(T - s\) has the same \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) distribution as a fresh wait. This is called memorylessness. A quiet Geiger counter has no idea how long it has been quiet.

All three distributions have an average wait of 2. Only for the exponential does the gold curve land exactly on the blue one, at every \(s\). For a wear-out lifetime, such as a gamma distribution (section 7), the remaining wait shrinks as you wait: an old machine really is closer to failing. For the uniform, the event is certain by \(t = 4\), so after waiting 3.5 it must happen within the next half unit.

Another way to say this uses the hazard rate \(h(t) = f(t)/S(t)\). It is the chance per unit time that the event happens right now, given that it hasn’t happened yet. For the exponential it is constant:

$$h(t) = \frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda t}}{e^{-\lambda t}} = \lambda$$

A constant hazard is memorylessness in another form. Rising hazards describe things that wear out. Falling hazards describe things that are most fragile when new.

Old atoms are not “due”. A uranium-238 atom that has existed since the Earth formed, about 4.5 billion years ago, is exactly as likely to decay in the next second as one created yesterday. If a process really is exponential, a long quiet spell tells you nothing about how soon the next event will come.

Why the exponential is the only memoryless waiting time

Memorylessness says \(P(T > s + t) = P(T > s)\,P(T > t)\), that is \(S(s+t) = S(s)S(t)\) for all \(s, t \ge 0\). Take logarithms: \(g = \ln S\) satisfies \(g(s+t) = g(s) + g(t)\). For a decreasing function the only solutions are straight lines through the origin, \(g(t) = -\lambda t\), so \(S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\). Among waiting times measured in whole steps, the geometric distribution plays the same role: it is the only memoryless distribution on \(1, 2, 3, \dots\)

04

Mean and variance

The rate and the average wait are reciprocals. Three buses an hour means one every 20 minutes on average. For a nonnegative waiting time, the mean is the area under the survival curve:

$$\begin{aligned} E[T] &= \int_0^\infty P(T > t)\,dt \\ &= \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = \frac{1}{\lambda} \end{aligned}$$

The same trick gives the second moment, and from it the variance:

$$\begin{aligned} E[T^2] &= \int_0^\infty 2t\,e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = \frac{2}{\lambda^2} \\ \operatorname{Var}(T) &= \frac{2}{\lambda^2} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2} = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \end{aligned}$$

So the standard deviation, \(1/\lambda\), equals the mean. Exponential waits vary a lot. When the average is 10 minutes, waits of 1 minute and of 25 minutes are both common. About one wait in seven is more than twice the average (\(e^{-2} \approx 0.135\)). Yet most waits are shorter than average: \(F(1/\lambda) = 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632\). The rare long waits pull the mean up above the median, \(0.693/\lambda\).

Why the mean is the area under the survival curve

Write \(T = \int_0^\infty \mathbf 1\{T > t\}\,dt\): integrating the indicator “still waiting at time \(t\)” over all \(t\) gives exactly \(T\). Take expectations and swap the integral with the expectation: \(E[T] = \int_0^\infty P(T > t)\,dt\). In the same way \(T^2 = \int_0^\infty 2t\,\mathbf 1\{T > t\}\,dt\), which gives the formula for \(E[T^2]\).

05

The Poisson process

Keep the clock running. After each event, start a fresh \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) wait for the next one. The events form a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\). This is the counter from the top of the page.

It is the same object we built from slices in section 1. Each thin slice holds an event with probability \(\lambda\Delta t\), independently of the others. So the number of events in a window of length \(t\) counts successes among \(n = t/\Delta t\) slices. That count is \(\text{Binomial}(n, \lambda\Delta t)\) with mean \(np = \lambda t\). As the slices shrink, \(n\) grows with \(np\) fixed, which is exactly the Poisson limit from the Binomial tutorial:

$$\begin{gathered} N(t) \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda t) \\ P(N(t) = k) = \frac{(\lambda t)^k e^{-\lambda t}}{k!} \end{gathered}$$

One process, two descriptions: the gaps are exponential and the counts are Poisson. They fit together exactly. No events by time \(t\) means the first wait is longer than \(t\), and both descriptions give \(P(N(t) = 0) = P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\).

Nothing in the simulation mentions the Poisson distribution. It only draws exponential gaps. The Poisson counts appear on their own, with mean and variance both equal to \(\lambda t\). The counts get a tutorial of their own in The Poisson Distribution.

06

The waiting-time paradox

Buses arrive as a Poisson process, on average every 10 minutes (\(\lambda = 0.1\) per minute). You turn up at a random moment. How long do you expect to wait?

“Half a gap, so 5 minutes” sounds right, but it is wrong. The buses don’t know when you arrived, so by memorylessness your wait is a fresh \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\): 10 minutes on average. Stranger still, the gap you land in, from the bus you just missed to the one you catch, averages 20 minutes, twice the average gap.

There is no contradiction. Long gaps cover more of the clock, so a random arrival is more likely to fall into one. A 20-minute gap is twice as likely to catch you as a 10-minute gap. This is length-biased sampling, also called the inspection paradox. Weighting each gap length by how long it is gives the density of the gap you land in:

$$g(t) = \frac{t\,f(t)}{E[T]} = \lambda^2 t\,e^{-\lambda t}$$

Its mean is \(2/\lambda\). Your arrival splits that gap into two pieces, the time since the last bus and the wait for the next. Each piece is an independent \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\).

Switch to buses that run exactly every 10 minutes and the paradox disappears: the average wait drops to 5 minutes and every gap is 10. Randomness alone doubles your wait. The same bias appears whenever you sample by landing on something. Ask students how big their classes are and you get a larger average than the school’s own average class size, because big classes contain more students to ask.

07

Races: the first of many clocks

Three independent exponential clocks run at rates \(\lambda_A\), \(\lambda_B\) and \(\lambda_C\). Think of three machines, any of which might fail first, or three radioactive isotopes in one sample. When does the first clock ring, and which one is it?

The first ring is later than \(t\) only if all three are:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\min > t) &= e^{-\lambda_A t}\,e^{-\lambda_B t}\,e^{-\lambda_C t} \\ &= e^{-(\lambda_A + \lambda_B + \lambda_C)t} \end{aligned}$$

So the first ring is itself exponential, with the rates added. And each clock’s chance of ringing first is its share of the total rate:

$$P(A \text{ first}) = \frac{\lambda_A}{\lambda_A + \lambda_B + \lambda_C}$$

Race three clocks

share of races wontheory: \(\lambda_i / \sum \lambda\)

After the first ring, the other clocks have no memory of the time that passed, so the race starts afresh among the rest. Chaining these two facts is how computers simulate chemical reactions and queues: draw one exponential with the total rate to find when the next event happens, then pick which event in proportion to its rate. This is the Gillespie algorithm.

Why the winning chance is \(\lambda_A / \sum\lambda\)

Write \(\Lambda = \lambda_A + \lambda_B + \lambda_C\). Clock A rings in a short interval around \(t\) with probability \(\lambda_A e^{-\lambda_A t}\,dt\), and the other two are both still silent then with probability \(e^{-(\lambda_B + \lambda_C)t}\). Add up over all \(t\):

$$\begin{aligned} &P(A \text{ first}) \\ &\quad= \int_0^\infty \lambda_A e^{-\lambda_A t}\,e^{-(\lambda_B+\lambda_C)t}\,dt \\ &\quad= \lambda_A \int_0^\infty e^{-\Lambda t}\,dt = \frac{\lambda_A}{\Lambda} \end{aligned}$$

The same calculation shows that which clock wins is independent of when the first ring happens.

Adding waits instead of racing them. The time until the \(k\)-th event of a Poisson process is a sum of \(k\) independent \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) gaps. It follows the gamma distribution with shape \(k\), also called the Erlang distribution, with density \(\lambda^k t^{k-1} e^{-\lambda t}/(k-1)!\), mean \(k/\lambda\) and variance \(k/\lambda^2\). The wear-out curve in section 3 was this with \(k = 3\).

08

Making exponentials from uniforms

Every simulation on this page needs exponential waits, but a computer’s random-number generator only gives uniform numbers \(U\) between 0 and 1. The fix is to run the CDF backwards. Solve \(F(t) = u\) for \(t\):

$$u = 1 - e^{-\lambda t} \;\iff\; t = -\frac{\ln(1-u)}{\lambda}$$

Feed in uniform \(u\)’s and out come exponential \(t\)’s. It works because

$$P(T \le t) = P\big(U \le F(t)\big) = F(t)$$

This is called inverse transform sampling. Since \(1 - U\) is also uniform, \(T = -\ln(U)/\lambda\) works just as well.

Pick a height, go across, drop down

uniform \(u\) (left edge)path to \(t\)resulting \(t\)’s\(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\)

Try the evenly spaced \(u\)’s. They land unevenly: bunched near 0, where the CDF is steep, and spread thin in the tail, where it is flat. The steepness of \(F\) is the density \(f\), so steep means likely. Move \(\lambda\) and every point slides at once. Changing the rate only rescales time.

09

Going backwards: what is \(\lambda\)?

In practice you don’t know the rate. You time \(n\) waits \(t_1, \dots, t_n\) and want to estimate \(\lambda\). The likelihood is the probability density of your data, viewed as a function of \(\lambda\):

$$\begin{aligned} L(\lambda) &= \prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda e^{-\lambda t_i} \\ &= \lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum t_i} \end{aligned}$$

Take the logarithm, differentiate and set the result to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \ell(\lambda) &= n\ln\lambda - \lambda\textstyle\sum t_i \\ \ell'(\lambda) &= \frac{n}{\lambda} - \textstyle\sum t_i = 0 \\ \hat\lambda &= \frac{n}{\sum t_i} = \frac{1}{\bar t} \end{aligned}$$

The maximum likelihood estimate is the natural one: events counted divided by time watched. It is slightly too high on average, since \(E[\hat\lambda] = \frac{n}{n-1}\lambda\), and \((n{-}1)/\sum t_i\) is the unbiased version. With 5 waits the difference is 25%; with 100 it is 1%.

An exact confidence interval

The total \(\lambda \sum t_i\) is a sum of \(n\) independent \(\text{Exp}(1)\) waits, so it follows a gamma distribution with shape \(n\), whatever \(\lambda\) is. If \(g_{0.025}\) and \(g_{0.975}\) are the 2.5% and 97.5% points of that gamma distribution, then

$$\left[\frac{g_{0.025}}{\sum t_i},\; \frac{g_{0.975}}{\sum t_i}\right]$$

is a 95% confidence interval for \(\lambda\). Textbooks often state the same fact as \(2\lambda\sum t_i \sim \chi^2_{2n}\). Unlike the binomial intervals, this one needs no approximation. It covers the true rate exactly 95% of the time, even for \(n = 1\). For example, 10 waits that add up to 12.5 minutes give \(\hat\lambda = 0.8\) per minute, with 95% interval \([0.38, 1.37]\).

With a handful of waits the likelihood is broad and lopsided, and the interval stretches further to the right than to the left. With hundreds it becomes narrow and nearly symmetric. Its width then shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\), roughly \(\hat\lambda \pm 1.96\,\hat\lambda/\sqrt n\).

Studies that end early. In a reliability test or a medical study, some items are still working when the study stops. Their lifetimes are censored: you only know they exceed the time watched. If \(d\) items failed and all items together were watched for a total time \(\tau\), counting the survivors’ time too, the maximum likelihood estimate is still events divided by time watched: \(\hat\lambda = d/\tau\). Dropping the survivors, or pretending they failed at the end, makes the rate look too high.

Where the formulas come from

All the moments at once, from the MGF

The moment generating function is \(M(s) = E[e^{sT}] = \int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-(\lambda - s)t}\,dt = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - s}\) for \(s < \lambda\). Expand it as a geometric series:

$$\frac{\lambda}{\lambda - s} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{s^k}{\lambda^k} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{k!}{\lambda^k}\cdot\frac{s^k}{k!}$$

The coefficient of \(s^k/k!\) is the \(k\)-th moment, so \(E[T^k] = k!/\lambda^k\). That gives \(E[T] = 1/\lambda\), \(E[T^2] = 2/\lambda^2\), \(E[T^3] = 6/\lambda^3\) and \(E[T^4] = 24/\lambda^4\).

Skewness 2 and excess kurtosis 6

The cumulants of \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) are \(\kappa_r = (r-1)!/\lambda^r\), read off from \(\ln M(s) = -\ln(1 - s/\lambda)\). So \(\kappa_2 = 1/\lambda^2\), \(\kappa_3 = 2/\lambda^3\) and \(\kappa_4 = 6/\lambda^4\), and

$$\gamma_1 = \frac{\kappa_3}{\kappa_2^{3/2}} = 2, \qquad \gamma_2 = \frac{\kappa_4}{\kappa_2^{2}} = 6$$

Both are free of \(\lambda\). Every exponential has the same shape; the rate only stretches the time axis. A sum of \(k\) independent exponentials (the gamma distribution) has skewness \(2/\sqrt k\) and excess kurtosis \(6/k\), shrinking toward the normal’s 0 and 0.

Entropy, and why the exponential is the “most random” waiting time

The differential entropy is \(H = -E[\ln f(T)] = -E[\ln\lambda - \lambda T] = 1 - \ln\lambda\) nats. It can be negative when \(\lambda > e\); differential entropy measures spread relative to a unit of time, not an absolute count of bits. Among all distributions on \([0, \infty)\) with a given mean, the exponential has the largest entropy. If all you know about a waiting time is its average, the exponential assumes the least beyond that.

Fisher information and the best estimate

The log-density of one observation is \(\ln\lambda - \lambda t\). Its second derivative in \(\lambda\) is \(-1/\lambda^2\), so each wait carries Fisher information \(1/\lambda^2\), and \(n\) waits carry \(n/\lambda^2\). Any unbiased estimator therefore has variance at least \(\lambda^2/n\). The likelihood depends on the data only through \(\sum t_i\), so the total time watched is a sufficient statistic: once you know it and \(n\), the individual waits tell you nothing more about \(\lambda\).

Structural facts worth remembering

  1. Memoryless, and the only one. No other continuous distribution satisfies \(P(T > s+t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)\) for all \(s, t\) (section 3).
  2. Its discrete twin is the geometric. Rounding up, \(\lceil T \rceil\) is geometric with success probability \(1 - e^{-\lambda}\). The exponential is the geometric with infinitely fine slices (section 1).
  3. Scaling. \(cT \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda/c)\) for \(c > 0\). Every exponential is a stretched \(\text{Exp}(1)\): \(T = E/\lambda\) with \(E \sim \text{Exp}(1)\).
  4. Sums: gamma. The sum of \(k\) independent \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) waits is \(\text{Gamma}(k, \lambda)\), the Erlang distribution.
  5. Special case of bigger families. \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) is the gamma distribution with shape 1 and the Weibull distribution with shape 1. Also, \(2\lambda T \sim \chi^2_2\).
  6. Conjugate prior: gamma. If your uncertainty about \(\lambda\) is \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\) (shape \(\alpha\), rate \(\beta\)), then after observing \(n\) waits it becomes \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha + n,\; \beta + \sum t_i)\).
11

Where you will meet it

Radioactive decay

Each nucleus decays after an exponential time, independently of its age. Half-lives, carbon dating and the clicking of a Geiger counter all follow from this.

Queues and call centers

In queueing theory the classic model, the M/M/1 queue, assumes exponential gaps between arrivals and exponential service times. The “M” stands for Markov, which here means memoryless. Staffing formulas for call centers start from it.

Reliability

Electronic parts in mid-life fail at a roughly constant rate. Their lifetime is then exponential, and the “mean time between failures” quoted on data sheets is \(1/\lambda\).

Servers and networks

Requests from many independent users arrive at a server roughly as a Poisson process. Load tests generate traffic with exponential gaps for this reason.

Survival analysis

Medical and actuarial studies model time to an event through its hazard rate. The exponential, with constant hazard, is the baseline that richer models such as the Weibull and Cox models build on.

When it fails

Things that wear out have rising hazards; use a Weibull or gamma instead. Traffic that comes in bursts, or follows a daily cycle, has more variable gaps than the exponential allows. Check with a log-scale survival plot: a straight line means exponential.
